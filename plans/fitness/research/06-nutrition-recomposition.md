# 06 — Nutrition for recomposition (fat loss + strength), with mass-gain, pure-strength and maintenance variants

Research memo for the ilyadays fitness planner. Date: 2026-11-08. Audience: the site owner (adult male, desk job, trains alone on machines 3×/week, goals: lose fat, gain strength, keep/gain some muscle, longevity) and the implementation agents.

Evidence grading used below: **Strong** = meta-analysis / position stand with consistent RCTs; **Moderate** = several RCTs or one well-designed meta-regression with caveats; **Weak/contested** = small or conflicting trials. Where evidence is weak, a practical default is stated and labelled as such.

---

## 1. Summary

1. **Protein is the lever.** Eat 2.2–2.6 g/kg bodyweight/day while cutting (≈2.5–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass), 1.8–2.2 g/kg at maintenance, 1.6–2.2 g/kg in a surplus. Above ~1.6 g/kg adds little when calories are adequate; in a deficit the dose-response is linear up to at least ~3 g/kg [1][2][3][5]. Intakes of 2.5–3.3 g/kg for a year showed no renal/hepatic harm in healthy trained men [6].
2. **Deficit size decides recomposition.** Keep the deficit ≤ 500 kcal/day (15–25% of expenditure) and the rate at 0.5–0.75% bodyweight/week; a ~500 kcal/day deficit is where lean-mass gains stop, and 0.7%/week preserved and even added lean mass where 1.4%/week did not [13][14].
3. **Surplus for strength, not mass: small.** 5–10% above maintenance (≈150–300 kcal), aiming at 0.1–0.25% bodyweight/week; a 15% surplus added fat, not muscle or squat strength, versus 5% [17][18].
4. **Estimate TDEE with Mifflin-St Jeor × activity factor (1.375–1.55 for this owner), then replace it after 2–3 weeks with the observed expenditure implied by logged intake and the smoothed weight trend.** Expect adaptive thermogenesis of 10–15% beyond composition-predicted after ~10% weight loss; the 7,700 kcal/kg rule overestimates loss and should only seed the model [19][20][22][23][25][48].
5. **Per-meal protein:** 0.4 g/kg (30–50 g, ≥2.5 g leucine) in 3–5 feedings; there is no "30 g cap" — 100 g produced a larger, >12 h anabolic response than 25 g. Total daily intake dominates; the post-workout "window" is several hours wide [7][8][9][10][11][12].
6. **Carbs are the flexible macro; fat has a floor.** Carbohydrate does not change strength performance in fed lifters doing ≤10 sets/muscle; fill remaining calories with carbs (≥3 g/kg on training days). Keep fat at 0.6–1.0 g/kg and never below 0.5 g/kg or 20% of calories for long: 20%-fat diets lowered testosterone 10–15% [28][29]. Fibre 14 g/1,000 kcal (25–38 g/day) [30].
7. **Supplements worth using:** creatine monohydrate 3–5 g/day (loading optional; +1–2 kg water early; no proven bone benefit) [32][33][34]; caffeine 3–6 mg/kg 45–60 min pre-training, stop ≥9 h before bed for ~100 mg and ≥13 h for ~200 mg, ≤400 mg/day [35][36][37]. Vitamin D only to RDA unless indoor/winter (1,000–2,000 IU) [52]; omega-3 from two fish meals/week [53].
8. **Hydration and alcohol:** ~3.7 L total water/day, avoid >2% bodyweight sweat loss (strength −2%, power −3%, high-intensity endurance −10%) [31][38][39]. Alcohol ≥1 g/kg after training cuts muscle protein synthesis 24–37% even with protein; keep to ≤2 drinks, none within ~4 h post-session, and count the 7 kcal/g [40][41].
9. **Refeeds/diet breaks are adherence tools.** MATADOR (obese men) showed benefit; in trained lifters, 1-week breaks every 3 weeks and 2-day weekly refeeds did not beat continuous dieting on fat or lean mass. Default: 1–2 weeks at maintenance after every 6–8 weeks of deficit [42][43][44].
10. **Track the trend, not the scale.** Weigh daily (morning, post-void), smooth with a ~7-day-half-life exponential average, judge the 14-day slope, and adjust intake by 100–150 kcal only after two consecutive off-target weeks. Stop cutting at ~10–15% body fat / waist-to-height < 0.5, or earlier if strength drops >5–10%, sleep/mood degrade, or the trend stalls 3 weeks despite adherence [45][46][47][48][49][51].

## 2. Publishable section — Eating for recomposition: the evidence, in numbers

**The one variable that matters most: protein.** Across 49 randomised trials and 1,863 participants, adding protein to a resistance-training programme increased fat-free mass and strength gains, and the benefit plateaued at roughly 1.6 g per kilogram of bodyweight per day, with the upper 95% confidence bound near 2.2 g/kg [1]. That is the number for people eating at maintenance or in a surplus. A deficit changes the picture, because an energy shortfall suppresses muscle protein synthesis and the body begins borrowing amino acids from muscle. A 2014 review of lean, resistance-trained athletes dieting for competition concluded that 2.3–3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass was needed to hold on to muscle, scaled upward for leaner people and larger deficits [2]. The 2025 update to that review, a Bayesian meta-regression of 29 studies, found a >97% probability of a linear dose-response between protein intake and favourable fat-free-mass change during energy restriction, stronger when protein is expressed per kg of fat-free mass, in men, in studies longer than four weeks and when body fat is lower; its exploratory recommendations are at least 1.9 g/kg bodyweight (2.5 g/kg FFM) when muscle retention matters, rising toward 3.2 g/kg bodyweight (4.2 g/kg FFM) where the risk of loss is high [3]. The authors are explicit that between-study heterogeneity makes those numbers hypotheses rather than prescriptions. The single most relevant trial for a recomposition goal put young men in a 40% deficit with hard daily training for four weeks: the group eating 2.4 g/kg gained 1.2 kg of lean mass while losing 4.8 kg of fat, versus +0.1 kg lean and −3.5 kg fat at 1.2 g/kg [5]. High intakes are also safe for healthy kidneys: resistance-trained men eating 2.5–3.3 g/kg for a year showed no adverse change in renal or hepatic markers or blood lipids [6]. A broader meta-analysis of healthy adults found the lean-mass benefit of extra protein significant in under-65s only at ≥1.6 g/kg combined with resistance training, and described the gains as small [4], which is the right framing: protein protects and adds a little; training does the building.

Practical default: 2.2–2.6 g/kg bodyweight while cutting (about 2.8 g/kg FFM when body fat is known), 1.8–2.2 g/kg at maintenance and 1.6–2.2 g/kg in a surplus.

**Distribution is secondary, but not nothing.** Meal-level data suggest about 0.4 g/kg per meal across four or more meals maximises the daily anabolic response, which lands most adults at 30–50 g per feeding [7]. The per-meal dose that saturates synthesis is about 0.24 g/kg in young men and 0.40 g/kg in older men, the difference being anabolic resistance with age [8]; each feeding should carry roughly 2.5–3 g of leucine, which 30–40 g of animal protein, or a large serving of soy or legumes, provides. The old idea that anything beyond 25–30 g in one sitting is "wasted" was tested directly in 2023: 100 g of milk protein after exercise produced a larger and longer (>12 h) anabolic response than 25 g, with negligible extra amino-acid oxidation [9]. The honest summary is that total daily protein dominates, spreading it across three to five feedings is a modest optimisation, and a skipped meal is not a catastrophe if the daily total is met.

**Timing: the window is a door.** The International Society of Sports Nutrition's nutrient-timing position recommends 20–40 g of high-quality protein every 3–4 hours and a protein feeding within about two hours either side of training, while noting that muscle stays sensitised to protein for roughly 24 hours after a session and that timing matters little once total protein and energy needs are met [10]. A meta-analysis of timing trials found that once total protein intake was controlled for, peri-workout protein had no independent effect on strength or hypertrophy [11], and the same authors' review concluded that for anyone who ate a protein meal in the hours before training the post-exercise window spans several hours, not thirty minutes [12]. Pre-sleep casein (30–40 g) raises overnight synthesis and is a convenient slot for a fourth feeding [10]. For a three-day-a-week machine lifter the rule is simple: a normal protein-containing meal one to three hours before, another within two to three hours after.

**Deficit size is where recomposition is won or lost.** A meta-analysis of resistance training performed in energy deficit found that deficits impaired lean-mass gains (effect size −0.57) but not strength gains, and that a deficit of about 500 kcal/day was the point at which lean-mass gains disappeared; the authors advise anyone trying to keep muscle while losing weight to stay under 500 kcal/day [13]. The cleanest rate-of-loss trial compared 0.7% and 1.4% of bodyweight per week in elite athletes lifting four times a week on 1.6 g/kg protein: intake was cut 19% versus 30% (−469 vs −791 kcal/day), both groups lost about 5.6% of bodyweight, but the slow group gained 1.0 kg of lean mass and improved its jump while the fast group lost 0.3 kg of lean mass and did not [14]. The ISSN's diet position agrees that higher baseline body fat tolerates a more aggressive deficit [15], and the recomposition literature in trained people consistently combines a modest deficit, high protein and progressive training [16]. Default rule: 0.5–0.75% of bodyweight per week (300–500 kcal/day for most men), up to 1% per week only above roughly 25% body fat, slowing to 0.3–0.5% below roughly 15%.

**Gaining: small surpluses, slow rates.** For strength rather than mass, the surplus should be small. A review of off-season nutrition suggests 10–20% above maintenance and 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week for novices and intermediates, with advanced lifters more conservative [17]. An eight-week trial in trained lifters comparing 5% and 15% surpluses against maintenance found no extra muscle thickness or squat strength from the larger surplus, a bench-press advantage, and more skinfold gain: faster weight gain was mostly fat [18]. For a "strong, not big" goal, 5–10% (about 150–300 kcal) and 0.1–0.25% of bodyweight per week is the right setting, and most of the year can be spent at maintenance.

**Estimate expenditure, then stop trusting the estimate.** The Mifflin-St Jeor equation (men: 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5) predicted resting metabolic rate within 10% of measured values in more people than its rivals [19][20]; when body fat is reliably known, the Katch-McArdle/Cunningham form (370 + 21.6 × fat-free mass) is a reasonable alternative [21]. Multiplying by an activity factor of 1.2–1.9 gives a starting total, but the factor is where most of the error lives, and the body adapts: after a 10% weight loss, total energy expenditure falls 10–15% below what the new body composition predicts, mostly through lower non-resting expenditure and more efficient muscle, and the adaptation persists for years in people who keep weight off [25][26]. Expenditure is also partly constrained, so steps and sessions do not sum linearly [27]. The 3,500-kcal-per-pound (7,700 kcal/kg) rule overestimates loss because it ignores these dynamics, and the weight response to a fixed intake change has a half-time of about a year [22][23][24]. Hall's analysis also shows that the energy cost of a kilogram lost depends on what is lost: about 9,400 kcal per kg of fat but only about 1,800 per kg of lean tissue, so a well-run high-protein cut in which 85–90% of the loss is fat "costs" closer to 8,000–8,600 kcal/kg, whereas bulk weight that is half fat costs roughly 5,500 kcal/kg [22]. The practical answer is the approach used by adaptive-expenditure apps: start from the formula, log intake and daily weight, and after two to three weeks replace the formula with the expenditure implied by intake minus the energy value of the trend-weight change, re-estimated weekly [48].

**Carbohydrate and fat.** For a fed lifter doing up to about ten sets per muscle group, carbohydrate intake per se does not change strength performance (11 of 19 acute and 15 of 17 longer studies found no effect) because such sessions barely dent glycogen [28]. Carbohydrate is therefore the flexible macronutrient: it fills whatever calories remain after protein and fat, with about 3 g/kg on training days as a comfortable floor and less on rest days during a cut [17]. Fat has a floor. Six controlled studies pooled together show that moving from about 40% to about 20% of calories from fat lowered testosterone by 10–15% [29]; keeping fat at 0.6–1.0 g/kg, and never below about 0.5 g/kg or 20% of calories for long, protects hormones and adherence [2][17]. Fibre at 14 g per 1,000 kcal (25–38 g/day) is the standard adequate intake [30] and matters more in a deficit, where satiety is the binding constraint.

**Supplements that earn their place.** Creatine monohydrate at 3–5 g/day, with or without a 20 g/day loading week, increases high-intensity work capacity by 10–20%; there is no compelling evidence of renal harm in healthy people, and the only consistent side effect is 0.5–1 litre of early fluid retention [32][33]. A meta-analysis found no added bone-density benefit from creatine plus training in older adults [34], so take it for strength, not bone. Caffeine at 3–6 mg/kg about 60 minutes before training improves strength, power and endurance; the minimum effective dose may be about 2 mg/kg and 9 mg/kg produces frequent side effects [35]. The cost is sleep: caffeine cut total sleep time by about 45 minutes on average, and to avoid that a 107 mg coffee needs about 8.8 hours and a 217 mg pre-workout about 13.2 hours before bed [36]; 400 mg/day is the general safety ceiling for healthy adults [37]. Vitamin D: the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline advises against routine testing and against doses above the RDA for healthy adults under 75 [52]; an indoor worker in a northern winter can still reasonably take 1,000–2,000 IU/day, but it is not a performance intervention. Omega-3: two oily-fish meals a week cover it; supplements above about 2 g/day EPA+DHA show small strength effects mainly in older adults and no consistent muscle-mass effect [53].

**Hydration and alcohol.** Adequate total water for men is about 3.7 L/day including food [31]; the ACSM advises starting exercise euhydrated and avoiding more than 2% bodyweight loss through sweat, replacing roughly 1.5 L per kilogram lost afterwards [38]. Hypohydration reduces strength by roughly 2%, power by 3% and high-intensity endurance by 10% [39], which matters more for the cardio half of a longevity programme than for machine sets. Alcohol is the one common habit that measurably blunts adaptation: 1.5 g/kg (about eight standard drinks) after training cut myofibrillar protein synthesis by 24% even when taken with protein, and by 37% with carbohydrate [40]; across 12 studies, hormonal and synthetic effects appear at about 1 g/kg and above, while the lowest doses studied (0.6–0.7 g/kg) did not impair strength or jump performance [41]. The planner's rule: count the calories (7 kcal/g), nothing within about four hours after training, and treat more than two drinks on a night as a recovery cost.

**Refeeds and diet breaks: adherence tools, not metabolic magic.** In men with obesity, alternating two weeks of dieting with two weeks at maintenance produced more fat loss and a smaller drop in expenditure than continuous dieting (−360 vs −749 kJ/day after adjusting for composition) [42]. In resistance-trained adults, however, one-week maintenance breaks every three weeks matched continuous dieting on fat loss, lean mass, strength and resting expenditure [43], and two-day weekly carbohydrate refeeds nominally preserved fat-free mass and resting metabolic rate, but a reanalysis found only "dry" fat-free mass differed and the authors conceded the FFM and RMR differences were not significant [44]. Use breaks because they make a four-to-six-month cut tolerable and keep training quality up, not because they protect muscle.

**Measurement.** Weigh daily, in the morning after the bathroom and before eating, and act only on the trend. Day-to-day weight moves 1–2 kg with water, glycogen, sodium and gut contents, and 4,657 weigh-ins from 80 adults showed a reproducible weekly rhythm of about 0.35% with peaks after the weekend and troughs on Friday [47]. Regular self-weighing is consistently associated with better weight outcomes [45], and randomised comparisons found daily and weekly weighing equally effective, so frequency is a data-quality choice rather than a behavioural necessity [46]. An exponentially weighted average with roughly a seven-day half-life, or a seven-day rolling mean, removes most of the noise; compare the two-week slope with the target rate and adjust intake by about 100–150 kcal only when it has drifted for two consecutive weeks [48].

**When to stop.** Reference healthy body-fat ranges for men are 8–19% at ages 20–39 and 11–21% at 40–59 [49]; fitness-industry charts classify 6–13% as "athlete" and 14–17% as "fitness" [50]. For a longevity-oriented lifter the useful stopping band is roughly 10–15%, with a waist-to-height ratio below 0.5 as the simplest cardiometabolic check [51]. Stop a cut early if main-lift performance falls more than 5–10% for two weeks, sleep, mood or libido degrade, or the trend stalls for three weeks despite logged adherence: a maintenance block fixes all three better than a deeper deficit does. Below about 30 kcal of energy availability per kilogram of fat-free mass per day, hormonal and bone costs rise; the threshold evidence comes mostly from women and the male threshold may be lower, but it is the right floor for someone planning years, not weeks [54].

| Macro | Rule | Floor / ceiling |

### 3.5 Hydration and alcohol

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| Baseline fluid | ~35 ml/kg/day from drinks + food water (≈ 3.7 L total for men) [31] |
| Training | start euhydrated (pale-straw urine); +0.5–1.0 L per training hour; in heat weigh before/after and drink ~1.5 L per kg lost [38] |
| Dehydration cost | > 2% BW loss: strength −2%, power −3%, high-intensity endurance −10% [39] |
| Alcohol accounting | 7 kcal/g; standard drink = 14 g (US) / 10 g (UK, AU); log it against the carb/fat budget |
| Alcohol limits | 0 within 4 h after training; ≤ 2 drinks per occasion; ≤ 7 drinks/week in a cut (≤ 4 preferred); ≥ 1 g/kg (≈ 5–8 drinks) = measurable MPS/hormone impairment [40][41] |

### 3.6 Refeeds and diet breaks (evidence: weak/contested in trained people)

| Tool | Default | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Diet break | 7–14 days at estimated maintenance, protein unchanged | after every 6–8 weeks of continuous deficit; mandatory if a cut passes 12 weeks; do not count the break toward "weeks to goal" [42][43] |
| Refeed day | optional 1–2 days/week at maintenance, extra calories as carbohydrate | weekly average deficit still applies (other days absorb it); use for training quality and adherence, not for metabolism [44] |
| Maintenance block after a cut | ≥ 4 weeks and ≥ 50% of the cut's length | before any surplus phase; confirms the new TDEE |

### 3.7 Weight tracking and the adjustment loop

```
weigh-in protocol: every morning, after voiding, before food/drink, same scale, same clothing state
trend[t]   = trend[t-1] + alpha * (weight[t] - trend[t-1]);  alpha = 0.10  (≈ 7-day half-life); seed trend with mean of first 3 weigh-ins
slope      = (trend[t] - trend[t-14]) / 2                      # kg per week, needs ≥ 10 weigh-ins in the 14 days
rate_pct   = slope / trend[t] * 100
TDEE_obs   = mean(intake, last 14 logged days) - slope * rho / 7   # rho = kcal per kg of weight change (3.8 below)
TDEE_est   = w * TDEE_obs + (1 - w) * TDEE_formula;  w = clamp((logged_days - 7) / 14, 0, 1)
clamp week-to-week change of TDEE_est to ± 150 kcal
new intake = TDEE_est + target_change_kcal_per_day            # negative for a cut
adjust only if |rate_pct - target_rate| > 0.15 %/week for 2 consecutive evaluations; step = ± 100–150 kcal (≈ 5% TDEE)
ignore the first 14 days of any new phase and 14 days after starting creatine (water shifts of 1–2 kg)
flag, do not adjust, if trend moves > 1.5 kg in 7 days (travel, illness, sodium, menstrual-cycle n/a)
```

Rationale: regular self-weighing improves outcomes [45]; daily vs weekly frequency does not differ in RCTs, but daily data make the trend estimate usable [46]; weekly rhythm ≈ 0.35% with Sunday/Monday peaks [47]; adaptive-expenditure approach as in MacroFactor [48].

### 3.8 Energy density of weight change (ρ, kcal per kg)

| Situation | ρ | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Default cut (BF% ≥ 25) | 7,700 | classic rule; fits obese cohorts [22][23] |
| Cut with RT + ≥ 2.2 g/kg protein (85–90% of loss is fat) | 8,300 (use 9,400·f + 1,800·(1 − f), f = fat fraction) | Hall 2008 densities: fat 9.4 kcal/g, lean 1.8 kcal/g [22] |
| Bulk (≈ 50% of gain is lean) | 5,500–5,600 | same formula with f ≈ 0.5 [22][48] |
| Projection correction | multiply static weeks-to-goal by 1.10 for adaptive thermogenesis | 10–15% TEE adaptation at 10% loss [25]; half-time ≈ 1 year [23] |

### 3.9 Stop rules and phase transitions

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| Body fat ≈ 10–12% (men), or waist-to-height ≤ 0.46 and the goal is met | end cut → maintenance block [49][50][51] |
| Longevity band reached: BF 10–15% and WHtR < 0.5 | hold maintenance; prefer slow recomposition over further cutting |
| Main-lift performance −5% or more on ≥ 2 lifts for 2 weeks | diet break (≥ 1 week) |
| Sleep, mood, libido or hunger flags for ≥ 2 weeks | diet break |
| Trend stall (< 0.25 %/week) for 3 weeks with ≥ 85% logging adherence | diet break first, then re-estimate TDEE; do not deepen the deficit beyond the 3.1 limits |
| Cut duration ≥ 12 weeks | mandatory 2-week break |
| Bulk: BF% > 18, waist +4 cm, or strength stalls while weight rises | back to maintenance or a short cut |
| Energy availability < 25 kcal/kg FFM/day | raise intake immediately [54] |
| Never | hold < 8% BF long-term; chase < 10% for health reasons |

Body-fat estimation: consumer BIA scales carry ±4–5 percentage-point error; use the same device under the same conditions for trend only, plus a tape measure at the navel. DEXA once or twice a year if available.

### 3.10 Calculators the site should implement

```
BMR_msj_male    = 10*W + 6.25*H − 5*age + 5                 (W kg, H cm)            [19]
BMR_msj_female  = 10*W + 6.25*H − 5*age − 161
BMR_km          = 370 + 21.6*FFM                             (FFM = W*(1 − BF))      [21]
BMR             = BF known from DEXA/calipers within 8 weeks ? mean(BMR_msj, BMR_km) : BMR_msj
TDEE_formula    = BMR * activity_multiplier                  (1.2 / 1.375 / 1.55 / 1.725 / 1.9; owner default 1.45)
cross-check     = BMR*1.15 + 0.38*W*(steps/1000) + sessions_per_week*300/7   (approximate; walking ≈ 0.5 kcal/kg/km, 1,000 steps ≈ 0.75 km)
TDEE            = adaptive estimate from 3.7 once ≥ 14 logged days exist
protein_g       = W * protein_per_kg(goal, BF)               (3.2)      — or FFM * ffm_rate, capped at 2.6 g/kg BW
fat_g           = clamp(0.8*W, max(0.5*W, 0.20*intake/9), 0.40*intake/9)
carbs_g         = (intake − 4*protein_g − 9*fat_g) / 4       (check floors in 3.3)
target_kcal     = TDEE + delta;  delta = −clamp(0.20*TDEE,300,500) cut | +clamp(0.07*TDEE,150,300) bulk | 0 maintenance
expected_weekly_change_kg = delta * 7 / rho                  (rho from 3.8)
expected_weekly_change_pct = expected_weekly_change_kg / W * 100
weeks_to_goal   = |W − W_goal| / |expected_weekly_change_kg| * 1.10 + floor(weeks/7)   (one break-week per ~7 weeks)
goal_weight_from_bf = FFM / (1 − BF_goal)                    (assumes FFM is held; add 0.5–1.0 kg FFM per 12 weeks if desired)
```
