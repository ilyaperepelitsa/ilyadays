# 01 — Training variables by goal mode

Research brief for the ilyadays fitness planner. Date: 2026-11-08. Audience: the site owner (adult male, trains alone, prefers machines, 3 days/week, no direct leg work at first) and the implementation agents.

Scope: resistance-training prescription for six goal modes — (a) maximal strength, (b) hypertrophy, (c) fat loss / recomposition, (d) muscular endurance, (e) general health and longevity maintenance, (f) power — plus machines vs free weights, training to failure vs RIR, block length, deloads, weekly structures for 2/3/4 days, and a mode-conversion table for switching modes mid-program.

Evidence grades used below: **A** = several meta-analyses pointing the same way; **B** = one meta-analysis/meta-regression, a preprint, or meta-analyses with important caveats; **C** = expert consensus or coaching practice without controlled trials.

## Summary

1. Weekly hard sets per muscle is the main dial for muscle growth. Gains rise roughly 0.24–0.37 % per extra weekly set with diminishing returns and no clear plateau in the data up to about 25 sets; 12–20 weekly sets per muscle is the best-supported target for trained lifters, with 4–6 sets as the minimum effective dose [1][2][3]. Strength saturates much earlier: 1 set is a minimum effective dose and detectable gains stop accumulating at roughly 4–6 direct weekly sets per lift, after which more sets buy little [2][27][28].
2. Load matters for strength, not for size: training above ~80 % 1RM (1–6 reps) maximizes 1RM gains, while any load of roughly 30–85 % 1RM taken close to failure grows muscle about equally [13][14][15][16]. Default rep ranges: strength 3–6, hypertrophy 6–15 (machines/isolation up to 20), endurance 15–25.
3. Proximity to failure: strength gains are unchanged across 0–5+ RIR, so lift heavy at 2–4 RIR; hypertrophy improves modestly as sets approach failure, so run most hypertrophy sets at 1–3 RIR and finish blocks at 0–1 RIR on machines, where failure is safe [5][6][7][8]. Lifters under-estimate RIR by about 1 rep; calibrate by occasionally taking a machine set to failure [9].
4. Frequency per muscle is secondary once weekly volume is fixed (hypertrophy: trivial effect; strength: more weekly exposures help, with diminishing returns beyond 2) [2][21][22]. Practical floor: train every muscle at least twice a week and keep per-session volume at or below ~8–12 fractional sets per muscle [2][4][31].
5. Rest 2–5 min between heavy compound sets (strength), 1.5–3 min for hypertrophy compounds, 60–90 s for isolation; resting under 60 s costs a little growth and more strength [17][18][19]. Tempo barely matters between 0.5 and 8 s per rep; avoid super-slow (>10 s) reps [23]. Put the lift you most want to improve first in the session (strength ES 0.32; no hypertrophy effect) [24].
6. Machines and free weights produce the same hypertrophy (SMD −0.06); strength gains are specific to the tested modality (free-weight tests favor free-weight training, SMD −0.21; machine tests favor machines, SMD 0.29). A machine-based program is fully legitimate; just test and progress on the machines you train on [11][12].
7. Blocks of 4–8 weeks (3–6 weeks of accumulation + a 5–7 day deload) match both the periodization evidence for strength (periodized > non-periodized, ES 0.31–0.43; undulating > linear in trained lifters) and the deloading practices surveyed in strength/physique athletes (deload every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks for 6.4 ± 1.7 days) [25][26][33][34][35]. Periodization does not improve hypertrophy per se (ES 0.13, NS); it is fatigue management [26].
8. A deload should cut volume by about half and reduce load 10–20 % (or add 3–4 RIR) while keeping frequency; a full week of complete rest mid-block cost lower-body strength (−4.5 kg squat 1RM) without helping hypertrophy, so cessation deloads are a last resort [33][36].
9. Fat loss / recomposition is a nutrition-constrained hypertrophy mode: keep the deficit at or under ~500 kcal/day (larger deficits abolish lean-mass gain while strength is still preserved), lose 0.5–0.7 % body weight per week, eat 1.6–2.4 g protein/kg (2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass when lean), keep loads heavy, and trim volume to the lower-middle of the hypertrophy range [37][38][39][40][41][42]. Adding cardio does not blunt strength or size, only explosive power, and least when done in separate sessions [43].
10. For longevity, dose is small: 30–60 min/week of resistance training sits at the bottom of the J-shaped mortality curve (10–27 % lower all-cause mortality), each +1 MET of VO2max cuts mortality ~13 %, and bone responds to high loads (≥80–85 % 1RM, 5×5 twice weekly in LIFTMOR), not light pump work [44][45][47][48][51][53]. Two sessions a week at RIR 2–3 plus 2–3 aerobic sessions is a complete maintenance prescription.

## Publishable section — What the evidence says about training variables by goal

### One stimulus, several dials

Every resistance-training program manipulates the same handful of variables: how heavy (intensity), how many sets (volume), how close to failure (effort), how often (frequency), how long between sets (rest), how fast (tempo), in what order, and for how many weeks before backing off. The goal decides the settings. Over the last decade the meta-analytic literature has become good enough that most settings can be stated as numbers rather than opinions, and this section summarises those numbers for six goals.

### Volume: the hypertrophy dial

Muscle growth follows weekly hard-set volume. Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger pooled 15 studies and found each additional weekly set per muscle added about 0.37 % to the percentage gain in muscle size, with higher-volume arms of studies out-growing lower-volume arms by roughly 3.9 percentage points [1]. The 2025 Pelland meta-regression (67 studies, 2,058 participants) confirmed the slope at 0.24 % per set around the typical 12-set week, with diminishing but still positive returns and no detectable plateau in the data, which thin out beyond ~25 sets. The same analysis identified ~4 weekly "fractional" sets (direct sets count 1, synergist sets count 0.5) as the minimum effective dose for measurable growth [2]. Baz-Valle's review of trained lifters found 12–20 weekly sets per muscle optimal for the quadriceps and biceps when each muscle was trained twice a week, with the triceps still responding above 20 [3]. A companion meta-regression on per-session volume suggests the marginal benefit of additional sets in a single session becomes undetectable around 11 fractional sets per muscle, which is why volume beyond ~10 sets per muscle should be spread over additional sessions rather than stacked [4]. Renaissance Periodization's volume landmarks (MEV, MAV, MRV) are coach-derived rather than trial-derived, but their ranges — for example chest MEV 4–6 and MRV 16–24 weekly sets, back MEV 6–8 and MRV 20–26, biceps MEV 8–10 and MRV 20–26, triceps MEV 4–6 and MRV 16–20 — bracket the meta-analytic 12–20 target sensibly and give per-muscle starting points [31][32].

Strength is a different curve. Pelland's strength model showed a single weekly set already produced a detectable 1RM gain, further detectable increments stopped around 4 fractional weekly sets, and the best-fit model indicated a functional plateau rather than continued growth [2]. Ralston's meta-analysis found 5+ weekly sets beat ≤5 (ES difference 0.18), and Androulakis-Korakakis showed one set of 6–12 reps at ~70–85 % 1RM, 2–3 times a week, maintains or slowly increases squat and bench 1RM in trained men [27][28]. In trained powerlifters, 3–9 direct weekly sets per lift were enough and 1–3 were not [2]. Strength work, in other words, is low-volume, heavy and frequent; hypertrophy work is higher-volume and only moderately heavy.

### Load and repetitions

Currier's Bayesian network meta-analysis (178 strength studies, n = 5,097; 119 hypertrophy studies, n = 3,364) ranked higher-load (>80 % 1RM), multi-set, three-times-weekly prescriptions best for strength, while all prescriptions produced comparable hypertrophy as long as multiple sets were performed [13]. Lopez's network meta-analysis found high (≤8 RM) and moderate (9–15 RM) loads outgained low loads (>15 RM) for strength (SMD 0.60–0.63 and 0.34–0.35) but no load effect on hypertrophy [15]. Schoenfeld's low- vs high-load meta-analysis reached the same split, and his 2021 re-examination of the "repetition continuum" concluded that whole-muscle growth is similar at any load of roughly 30 % 1RM or more when sets are taken near failure, that heavy loads are required for maximal strength, and that evidence for a load-specific endurance zone is equivocal [14][16]. The practical implication is that rep range is chosen for strength specificity, joint comfort and time efficiency, not for "toning" or "bulking": 1–6 reps for strength, 6–15 for most hypertrophy work (up to 20–30 on machines and isolation movements, which are safer near failure), and 15–25 reps only when local endurance is itself the goal. The classic NSCA table (100 % 1RM = 1 rep, 87 % = 5, 80 % = 8, 75 % = 10, 67 % = 12, 65 % = 15) is a reasonable average, but a 2024 meta-regression of 7,289 individuals shows wide between-person spread (SD ~2.5 reps at 80 % 1RM, ~4.4 reps at 60 %) and exercise dependence (at 70 % 1RM: ~14 reps on bench press, ~19 on leg press), so loads should be anchored to each exercise's own rep history rather than a universal percentage [57][58].

### Effort: failure versus repetitions in reserve

Two 2022–2024 syntheses settled most of the failure debate. Grgic's meta-analysis of 15 studies found no overall difference between failure and non-failure training for strength (ES −0.09) or hypertrophy (ES 0.22), a strength penalty for failure training when volume was not equated (ES −0.32), and a small hypertrophy advantage for failure in trained lifters (ES 0.15) [8]. Robinson's meta-regression across 55 studies found the RIR slope for strength indistinguishable from zero across a wide range, while hypertrophy increased as sets ended closer to failure; the authors' recommendation was 0–5 RIR for hypertrophy and roughly 3–5 RIR for strength work with heavier loads [5][61]. Refalo's meta-analysis found only a trivial edge for set failure and his 8-week trial in trained lifters showed identical quadriceps growth at 0 RIR versus 1–2 RIR [6][7]. Velocity-loss data agree: capping velocity loss at ≤25 % favours strength, exceeding 20–25 % favours hypertrophy [10]. One caveat applies to every RIR-based plan: people under-estimate their reps in reserve by about one rep on average, and accuracy is better in sets of 12 or fewer reps and closer to failure [9]. A planner should therefore keep RIR-driven load selection in the ≤12-rep zone and schedule periodic machine sets to true failure to recalibrate.

### Frequency, rest, tempo and order

When weekly volume is held constant, training a muscle 2× versus 1× or 3× makes little difference to growth (ES 0.07 in the 2019 volume-equated meta-analysis; a slight, uncertain positive slope in Pelland) [2][21]. The older finding that twice weekly beats once (ES 0.49 vs 0.30) came from studies where higher frequency also meant more volume [20]. Strength does gain from additional weekly exposures to the lift being tested — Pelland estimated 12.7 % at one session versus 17.3 % at two, with accelerating diminishing returns after that [2][22]. Rest intervals: a 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis found a small hypertrophy benefit for resting longer than 60 s and no detectable differences once rest exceeds ~90 s; a 2025 meta-analysis in trained men found short versus long rest irrelevant for hypertrophy (SMD 0.08) but longer rest better for strength [18][19]. ACSM's guidance of 2–3 min for heavy multi-joint sets and 1–2 min for hypertrophy work remains consistent with that [17]. Repetition tempo between 0.5 and 8 s produces similar hypertrophy; super-slow reps over 10 s are inferior [23]. Exercise order has a specific effect: whatever is trained first gains more strength (ES 0.32), with no effect on hypertrophy (ES 0.03) [24].

### Machines versus free weights

Haugen's 2023 meta-analysis (13 studies, 1,016 participants) found no difference in hypertrophy between machine and free-weight training (SMD −0.055), no difference in isometric strength or jump height, and modality-specific strength: free-weight tests improved more after free-weight training (SMD −0.21, p = 0.023) and machine tests tended to improve more after machine training (SMD 0.29, p = 0.064) [11][12]. For someone who trains alone, machines also make 0–1 RIR sets safe, which matters because that is where the hypertrophy signal is strongest. The only reasons to add free weights or impact loading are specific: jump and power performance, and the axial and hip loading that bone adaptation needs.

### Periodization, blocks and deloads

Periodized programs out-perform non-periodized ones for strength (ES 0.43 overall; 0.31 when volume is equated), and undulating models beat linear ones in trained lifters (ES 0.61, borderline), while hypertrophy is indifferent to periodization (ES 0.13, NS) [25][26]. The purpose of a block structure for hypertrophy is therefore fatigue management and autoregulation, not a growth mechanism. Surveys and a Delphi consensus of strength and physique coaches converge on deloads lasting 5–7 days every 4–6 weeks (athlete survey: 6.4 ± 1.7 days every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks), with volume reduced (100 % agreement), intensity either maintained or reduced, frequency unchanged, and reactive triggers of stalled performance, soreness and joint aches [33][34][35]. The one controlled test, Coleman's 9-week trial in trained lifters, found that a full week of training cessation at the midpoint changed nothing for hypertrophy but reduced lower-body strength gains (−4.5 kg squat 1RM, posterior probability 0.85), so complete rest is a poorer deload than a reduced-volume week [36].

### Fat loss and recomposition

Resistance training during an energy deficit preserves strength and most lean mass, but the deficit size is the constraint: Murphy and Koehler's meta-regression indicates that a deficit of about 500 kcal/day eliminates lean-mass gain, while strength gains are unaffected [37]. Slower is better: athletes losing 0.7 % body weight per week gained 2.1 % lean mass and lost 31 % of fat mass, versus no lean-mass change at 1.0–1.4 %/week [41]. Protein must be high — 1.6–2.4 g/kg body weight, or 2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass for lean lifters in hard deficits — and in a 40 % deficit with 2.4 g/kg protein plus hard training, young men gained 1.2 kg lean mass while losing 4.8 kg fat in four weeks [38][39][40]. Training in this mode looks like hypertrophy training with heavy loads retained and volume trimmed toward the lower-middle of the range, since recovery capacity falls in a deficit. Concurrent aerobic work costs nothing in hypertrophy (SMD −0.01) or maximal strength (−0.06) and only trims explosive strength (−0.28), least when cardio is in a separate session [43].

### Health and longevity maintenance

The dose that moves mortality is small. Momma's meta-analysis of 16 cohorts links muscle-strengthening activity to 10–17 % lower all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes, with a J-shaped curve bottoming at 30–60 min/week; Shailendra's puts the maximum reduction at 27 % around 60 min/week [44][45]. Strength itself is a biomarker: higher grip strength carries a hazard ratio of 0.69 for all-cause mortality and higher knee-extension strength 0.86 across ~2 million people [46]. Aerobic fitness is at least as important: each additional MET of cardiorespiratory fitness lowers all-cause mortality by about 13 %, with no upper limit of benefit observed even in the fittest cohort [47][48]. Four-by-four-minute intervals at 90–95 % HRmax three times a week improved VO2max 7.2 % in eight weeks, and interval training beats steady-state by about 1.2 mL/kg/min [49][50]. Bone needs load: LIFTMOR's twice-weekly, 30-minute sessions of 5×5 at ≥80–85 % 1RM on deadlift, squat and overhead press plus jumping chin-ups improved lumbar spine BMD 2.9 % versus a 1.2 % loss in controls, high-intensity programs raise lumbar BMD more than moderate ones (0.031 vs 0.012 g/cm²), and in men resistance training alone or with impact loading is the most osteogenic exercise [51][52][53][54]. WHO's guideline — 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity plus muscle-strengthening of all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week — is the floor, not the ceiling [55].

### Power, briefly

Power rests on strength: an athlete cannot be powerful without first being relatively strong, and the training load that maximizes power is 0–50 % 1RM for ballistic movements and 50–90 % for weightlifting derivatives, performed as fast as possible for 1–5 reps, 3–5 sets, with 3–5 min rest and never to failure [17][56]. Because concurrent aerobic work blunts explosive gains most when performed in the same session, power work belongs first in a session and away from cardio [43].

### Putting it together

For a lifter training three days a week on machines, the evidence points to a simple architecture: heavy, low-rep, low-volume work on a few key machine lifts for strength; 12–20 weekly fractional sets per priority muscle at 1–3 RIR for growth, spread over two or three sessions so no muscle gets more than ~10 sets in a session; 2–3 min rest on compounds and 60–90 s on isolation work; 4–6 week blocks ending in a half-volume deload; and a diet that never exceeds a ~500 kcal/day deficit at a loss rate of 0.5–0.7 % body weight per week. Aerobic intervals twice a week and, eventually, some heavy axial loading complete the longevity picture.

### Table 10 — Mode conversion: what changes and how to carry loads over

**Load carry-over formula (all switches):** `new_load = e1RM / (1 + (target_reps + target_RIR) / 30) × 0.95` for the first session after the switch, where `e1RM = old_load × (1 + (old_reps + old_RIR) / 30)` from the most recent comparable set on the *same exercise and machine*. Drop the 0.95 factor from session 2 if the first session hit the target reps at or below the target RIR. The "RIR shift" rule is: load for n reps at k RIR equals the load for an (n + k)-rep max.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-rep load → | 100 | 94 (94) | 92 (91) | 87 (86) | 82 (81) | 79 (72) | 73 (70) | 66 |
| 5-rep load → | 106 (107) | 100 | 97 (98) | 92 (92) | 88 (86) | 83 (77) | 78 (75) | 70 |
| 6-rep load → | 109 (109) | 103 (102) | 100 | 95 (94) | 90 (88) | 86 (79) | 80 (76) | 72 |
| 8-rep load → | 115 (116) | 109 (109) | 106 (106) | 100 | 95 (94) | 90 (84) | 84 (81) | 76 |
| 10-rep load → | 121 (124) | 114 (116) | 111 (113) | 105 (107) | 100 | 95 (89) | 89 (87) | 80 |
| 12-rep load → | 127 (139) | 120 (130) | 117 (127) | 111 (119) | 105 (112) | 100 | 93 (97) | 84 |

Note on the brief's example: a 10-rep load is ~75 % of **1RM**, but ~86–88 % of the **5-rep load** (75/87 by NSCA, 75/85.7 by Epley). Converting 5-rep strength loads to 10-rep hypertrophy loads at 75 % would under-load by about 12 %. The planner should use the ratio table or the e1RM formula, not the %1RM number.

Worked example with RIR: strength set 5 reps @ 2 RIR = 7RM ≈ 81 % 1RM; hypertrophy target 10 reps @ 2 RIR = 12RM ≈ 71 % 1RM; ratio 71.4/81.1 = 0.88 → use 88 % of the strength working load (then ×0.95 for the first session = 84 %).

Universal switch rules: (1) never carry loads across modalities (machine ↔ free weight) or across machine models; (2) keep exercise selection stable across a switch so e1RM history remains valid; (3) reset the block counter and RIR schedule at the switch; (4) switching more often than every 4 weeks defeats the purpose — treat sub-4-week changes as within-block adjustments, not mode changes.

### Applying to the owner's profile (3 days/week, machines, no legs initially, goals: fat loss + strength + modest pec development + strong back + arm and ab strength + longevity)

- Run **fat loss / recomposition mode with a strength bias**: main machine presses and rows/pulldowns at 5–8 reps, 2–3 RIR, 4–6 direct sets per lift per week; chest, back, biceps, triceps and abs at 10–14 fractional sets/week in 8–15 reps; side/rear delts 6–10 sets. That is ~18–22 working sets per session, which fits 55–70 min with the rest intervals above.
- Deficit ≤500 kcal/day, 0.5–0.7 % body weight per week, protein 1.6–2.4 g/kg (upper end as leanness increases). Two aerobic sessions per week (one 4×4 interval, one 30–40 min steady), on non-lifting days or ≥3 h after lifting.
- Blocks of 5 weeks + 1 deload week (sets ×0.5, load ×0.9). Re-test e1RM on the main lifts in the first week of each block.
- Longevity caveat to schedule later: bone density and VO2max respond to heavy axial/hip loading and intervals; a machine-only upper-body program leaves the hip and spine under-loaded. Plan to add heavy leg press or hack squat (5–8 reps at ≥80 % 1RM) and some impact (jumps, drop landings) once legs are allowed.

### Where the evidence is weak or contested (and the default used)

- Hypertrophy at very high volumes (>25 sets/week): data are sparse; default ceiling 20–24 sets [2][3].
- Per-session volume cap: based on one preprint plus coaching practice; default ≤10 (hard cap 12) [4][31].
- Exact RIR–hypertrophy slope: RIR in trials was estimated, not measured; the direction is consistent, the magnitude is modest [5][6].
- Endurance-specific loading: equivocal; the ACSM numbers are used as the default [16][17].
- Deload prescription: surveys and consensus, one RCT of cessation only; defaults follow the consensus [33][34][36].
- RP volume landmarks: coach-derived averages, not trial outputs; used as per-muscle starting points inside the meta-analytic 12–20 envelope [31][32].
- The frequency–strength effect is a 2025 meta-regression finding that contradicts earlier volume-equated nulls; default 2–3 exposures per lift either way [2][22].

      "convert loads per exercise via e1RM from the most recent set on the same exercise and machine, then apply 0.95 for the first session",
      "finish the current training week before switching",
      "strength -> hypertrophy: deload 5-7 days first if >= 3 weeks accumulated, then start at RIR 3",
      "hypertrophy -> strength: no deload required; run 1 week at RIR 3-4 before RIR 2",
      "any -> fat loss: switch at a block boundary and set nutrition constraints",
      "keep exercise selection stable across the switch",
      "reset block counter and RIR schedule at the switch",
      "changes more frequent than every 4 weeks are within-block adjustments, not mode changes"
