Training builds muscle and strength. Nutrition decides whether a calorie deficit takes some of that muscle along with the fat. Two numbers do most of the work: protein per kilogram of body weight and the size of the deficit. Everything else is fine-tuning.

> **In short:** about 2.2 g of protein per kg of body weight every day, a deficit of 300–500 kcal a day, a loss of about 0.5 % of body weight a week, and decisions made on the trend of several weeks, never on a single weigh-in.

## Protein is the lever

Across 49 trials with 1,863 people, extra protein increased the muscle gained from resistance training, and the benefit levelled off at about 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day [1]. That is the number for people who eat enough.

A deficit raises the need, because the body starts borrowing amino acids from muscle:

- A review of lean, trained athletes dieting for competition put the requirement at 2.3–3.1 g per kg of **fat-free mass**, rising with leanness and with the size of the deficit [2].
- A meta-regression of 29 studies of dieting, trained people found a more than 97 % probability that more protein meant better retention of fat-free mass, roughly in a straight line. The link was stronger per kg of fat-free mass, in men, in studies longer than four weeks and in leaner people. The studies differ a lot, so the authors call the result exploratory [3].
- The trial closest to recomposition put young men on a 40 % deficit with hard training six days a week for four weeks. On 2.4 g/kg they gained 1.2 kg of lean mass and lost 4.8 kg of fat; on 1.2 g/kg they gained 0.1 kg and lost 3.5 kg [4].

High intakes look safe for healthy kidneys: trained men eating about 2.5–3.3 g/kg a day for a year showed no harmful changes in blood lipids or in liver and kidney markers [5].

## Spread it out, don't chase the clock

- **Per meal:** about 0.4 g/kg, across at least four meals [6]. For most men that is 30–40 g.
- **No hard cap:** after exercise, 100 g of protein produced a larger and longer (more than 12 hours) anabolic response than 25 g [7]. A big dinner is not wasted.
- **Timing:** the ISSN advises 20–40 g every 3–4 hours and high-quality protein within two hours after training, while calling total daily intake the priority [8]. When total protein was equal, timing had no independent effect on strength or size [9]. The post-workout window is hours wide, not 30 minutes.

## Deficit size decides recomposition

A meta-analysis of resistance training in an energy deficit found that the deficit blunted lean-mass gains (effect size −0.57) but not strength gains, and that a deficit of about 500 kcal a day was enough to stop lean-mass gains [10].

The rate of loss tells the same story. Elite athletes were asked to lose 0.7 % or 1.4 % of body weight a week (the fast group managed about 1.0 %). Both lost about 5.5 %, but the slow group gained 2.1 % lean mass while the fast group's did not change [11]. Guidance for natural bodybuilders preparing for a contest lands on 0.5–1 % a week [12].

Gaining has its own trap. In trained lifters, a 15 % surplus added no more muscle thickness or squat strength than a 5 % surplus, but more skinfold fat [13]. Off-season guidance suggests 0.25–0.5 % a week for novices and intermediates, and slower for advanced lifters [14].

## Estimate maintenance, then let the scale correct it

The Mifflin–St Jeor equation estimates resting expenditure; for men it is `10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5` [15]. In a systematic comparison it came within 10 % of measured values in more people than the other common equations [16]. Multiplying by an activity factor (1.2 for sedentary up to 1.9 for very active) gives total daily expenditure, and the activity factor is the least certain part.

Then the body adapts. People keeping off 10 % of their weight spend 10–15 % less energy a day than their new body composition predicts [17]. The 7,700 kcal-per-kg rule is only an average: fat tissue holds about 9,400 kcal per kg and lean tissue about 1,800 [18]. Weight also approaches a new steady state slowly; for an overweight adult, half of the eventual change from a lasting diet change takes about a year [19].

The fix is to measure. Log intake and weigh daily; after two to three weeks, compute real expenditure as intake minus the energy in the change of trend weight, and keep re-estimating. Adaptive diet apps such as MacroFactor work this way [20].

## Carbohydrate, fat and fibre

- **Protein first, fat second, carbohydrate fills the rest.** For fed lifters doing up to about ten sets per muscle group in a workout, carbohydrate intake is unlikely to change strength performance [21]. Carbs are the flexible macro; tilt them toward training days.
- **Fat has a floor.** In pooled intervention studies, low-fat diets lowered men's testosterone [22]. Off-season bodybuilding guidance uses 0.5–1.5 g of fat per kg a day [14].
- **Fibre:** the reference intake is 14 g per 1,000 kcal, or 38 g a day for men aged 19–50 [23].

## Supplements and habits

- **Creatine monohydrate**, 3–5 g every day. The ISSN finds it safe and well tolerated in healthy people, and the one consistently reported side effect is a small gain in body weight [24]; note the start date so the trend isn't misread.
- **Caffeine**, 3–6 mg/kg, usually about an hour before training, improves strength and power; as little as 2 mg/kg may work, and 9 mg/kg brings frequent side effects [25]. It costs sleep, 45 minutes on average: a 107 mg coffee needs about 9 hours before bed and a 217 mg pre-workout drink about 13 hours [26]. Up to 400 mg a day raises no safety concern for healthy adults [27].
- **Alcohol:** a heavy dose after training (1.5 g/kg, about a dozen standard drinks) cut muscle protein synthesis by 24 % even with protein, and by 37 % with carbohydrate [28]. Smaller amounts were not tested, so the rule is simple: little, never on a training evening, and counted in the calorie budget.

## Diet breaks and the scale

- In resistance-trained adults, a week at maintenance after every three weeks of dieting gave the same fat loss, fat-free mass, strength and resting expenditure as continuous dieting [29]. Breaks are an adherence tool, not metabolic magic.
- Weight climbs over the weekend, peaks on Sunday or Monday and drifts down through the week [30]. Daily weighing and a smoothed trend take care of it; the [analytics explainer](/fitness/science/analytics) shows how.
- A single boundary, waist under half of height (waist-to-height ratio below 0.5), flagged early health risk better than a matrix of BMI and waist size [31].

## What this means for me

My phase: strength-leaning fat loss, three upper-body machine sessions a week, keeping every kilogram on the stack while the waist shrinks. These are my daily rules.

- **Protein 2.2 g per kg of body weight**, training day or not. With a body-fat reading, about 2.8 g per kg of fat-free mass, which works out higher per kg of body weight as I get leaner (capped at 2.6 g/kg) [2][3]. A bad day above 1.6 g/kg is no disaster: that is where the benefit levels off outside a deficit [1].
- **Four protein meals** of at least 0.4 g/kg, one in the hours before training and one within two hours after [6][8].
- **Deficit 300–500 kcal a day**, about 20 % of maintenance; never more than 500 [10].
- **Pace check on the trend** (the weekly rate on the progress page, a 28-day slope): about −0.5 % of body weight a week, −0.75 % at most; faster than −1 % means eat more [11][12]. Slower than −0.25 % for three honest weeks means re-estimate maintenance before cutting deeper [20].
- **Fat about 0.8 g/kg, never below 0.5 g/kg; carbs fill the rest**, more on training days [14][21].
- **Change calories in steps of 100–150 kcal**, only after two off-target weeks in a row (practical default).
- **Creatine 5 g daily; caffeine only before training, at least 9–13 hours before bed; alcohol rare and never after a session** [24][26][28].
- **A one- to two-week maintenance break every 6–8 weeks** of dieting, for adherence (practical default, see [29]).
- **Stop the cut** when the waist is under half my height, or earlier if the main lifts drop for two weeks running [31].

## Caveats

- Maintenance equations describe groups. For one person the error is often 10 % or more, and the activity factor is a guess [16].
- Protein targets above 1.6 g/kg rest on studies that differ a lot; treat them as sensible defaults, not thresholds [3].
- Much of the evidence comes from young men, athletes and short trials (four weeks in [4]); older or less-trained bodies may respond differently.
- The kidney-safety data come from healthy men [5]. With kidney disease or any other medical condition, protein targets are a question for a doctor.
- Weeks-to-goal numbers assume a steady deficit; adaptation makes real progress slower than the arithmetic [17][19].
- Creatine, salt, carbohydrate and travel move water by a kilogram or more. Judge fat loss over weeks, not days.
- This page explains evidence. It is not individual medical or dietetic advice.
