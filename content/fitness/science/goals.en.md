Every program turns the same dials: load, sets, closeness to failure, frequency, rest, order and block length. The goal decides the settings, and a decade of meta-analyses lets most of them be written as numbers. The planner's five goal modes are those numbers, applied to the same exercises.

> **In short:** muscle grows with weekly hard sets (about 10–20 per muscle), strength with heavy loads and practice, and both with sets that stop one to three reps short of failure. In a deficit, keep the weights heavy and the volume moderate. When I switch modes, each working weight is converted through my estimated one-rep max.

## Volume builds muscle, strength needs less

- In a 2017 meta-analysis, each extra weekly set per muscle added about 0.37 % to muscle growth [1].
- A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 people found 0.24 % per extra set around the typical 12-set week. Returns diminish without a clear plateau, although few studies go beyond about 25 sets; four weekly sets was the smallest dose with detectable growth [2].
- In trained lifters, 12–20 weekly sets per muscle worked best [3].
- Strength saturates much sooner: one weekly set already gave a detectable gain, and extra sets stopped adding detectable strength after about four [2].

The planner counts **fractional sets**, as that meta-regression did: 1 for the target muscle and 0.5 for each helper, so a chest press is half a set for the triceps and front delts [2].

## Load, reps and effort

A 2023 network meta-analysis ranked loads above 80 % of the one-rep max (1RM), several sets and three sessions a week highest for strength, while every multi-set prescription grew muscle about equally [4]. From about 30 % of 1RM upward, any load builds similar muscle when sets end close to failure, and the evidence for a special "endurance" rep range is equivocal [5]. So reps are chosen for the goal, the joints and the clock.

Effort is counted in **reps in reserve (RIR)**: how many more reps I could have done.

- Strength gains barely change with how close sets get to failure, while muscle growth rises modestly as sets end closer to it [6].
- Training to failure adds at most a trivial amount of growth [7]; overall, failure and non-failure training give similar results [8].
- People underestimate their reps in reserve by about one rep and judge best in sets of 12 reps or fewer [9]. The planner reads a logged RIR as ±1 and estimates strength only from sets where reps + RIR ≤ 12.

## Frequency, rest and order

- With weekly sets equal, one, two or three sessions a week grow a muscle about equally [10]; strength gains a little from practising a lift more often, with diminishing returns beyond two sessions [2]. The modes use two or three sessions per muscle.
- **Rest:** growth was slightly better with rests longer than 60 s, with no detectable difference beyond about 90 s [11]. The ACSM position stand advises 2–3 minutes for heavy multi-joint sets and 1–2 minutes for lighter work [12].
- **Order:** the exercise done first gains more strength (effect size 0.32), with no effect on growth [13]. My priority lift goes first.

## Machines are not second best

Across 13 studies with 1,016 people, machines and free weights built the same muscle, and strength improved most on the equipment people trained on [14]. A machine program is fully legitimate if I test and progress on my own machines and never carry a weight from one model to another. What machines lack is heavy loading through the hips and spine; more on that below.

## Blocks and deloads

- Periodization improves strength a little even when total volume is equal, but does not change muscle growth [15]. For muscle, blocks are fatigue management.
- Coaches agree that a deload reduces volume rather than stopping training [16]; surveyed strength and physique athletes take about a week every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks [17]. A full week off in the middle of a program brought no extra growth and slightly smaller lower-body strength gains [18].

So the planner never prescribes a week off. After every five weeks of training (eight in maintenance) comes a lighter week: 40 % fewer sets at the same weights, two fewer reps and two more reps in reserve. In fat-loss mode it can double as a diet break.

## Training in a calorie deficit

- A deficit blunted lean-mass gains but not strength gains, and about 500 kcal a day was enough to stop lean-mass gains [19].
- Elite athletes losing about 0.7 % of body weight a week gained lean mass; a group losing faster did not [20].
- Protein matters: in a 40 % deficit with hard training, young men on 2.4 g/kg gained 1.2 kg of lean mass and lost 4.8 kg of fat in four weeks [21]. The [nutrition explainer](/fitness/science/nutrition) has the daily rules.
- Cardio does not blunt muscle growth or maximal strength, only explosive strength, and least when done in a separate session [22].

So: heavy weights, moderate volume because recovery is worse in a deficit, reps before weight, and held strength counts as a win.

## The five goal modes

Main lifts are each day's primary presses, rows and pulldowns; "other" covers the secondary compound lifts, isolation, small-muscle and trunk work. Weekly sets are fractional sets per muscle (strength: direct sets per main lift), given as start → target → ceiling.

| Mode | Reps: main · other | RIR: main · other | Sets a week | Rest: main · isolation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | 3–6 · 6–15 | 2 · 2 | 4 → 8 → 12 | 3 min · 90 s |
| Muscle growth | 8–12 · 10–25 | 2 · 1 | 10 → 16 → 22 | 2.5 min · 75 s |
| Fat loss & recomposition | 6–10 · 8–20 | 2 · 1–2 | 10 → 13 → 16 | 2.5 min · 75 s |
| Muscular endurance | 15–20 · 15–30 | 2 · 1 | 8 → 12 → 15 | 75 s · 45 s |
| Health & maintenance | 5–8 · 8–20 | 2 · 2 | 6 → 9 → 12 | 2.5 min · 75 s |

The ranges condense [2][3][4][5][6][11][12][19]; the cut-offs are planner defaults. Rules shared by every mode:

- **Progression:** double progression. Reps climb to the top of the range at the target RIR, then the weight goes up one step (at least 2.5 %, 5 % in endurance mode) and the reps start again from the bottom [12].
- **Misses:** after two failed sessions in a row on one exercise (three in strength mode), its weight drops 10 %.
- **Maintenance** is the minimum that keeps strength and muscle: two sets per exercise and about 30–60 minutes of lifting a week, the range linked with the lowest mortality [23], plus two or three cardio sessions [24].

## Switching modes without losing weights

A set stopped with k reps in reserve counts as a set to failure with k more reps, so 8 reps at RIR 2 is a 10-rep max. Rep maxima map onto percentages of 1RM:

| Reps + RIR | % of 1RM | Reps + RIR | % of 1RM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 100 | 11 | 72.5 |
| 2 | 95 | 12 | 70 |
| 3 | 93 | 13 | 68 |
| 4 | 90 | 14 | 67 |
| 5 | 87 | 15 | 65 |
| 6 | 85 | 16 | 64 |
| 7 | 83 | 17 | 63 |
| 8 | 80 | 18 | 62 |
| 9 | 77 | 19 | 61 |
| 10 | 75 | 20 | 60 |

Values for 1–10 and 12 come from the NSCA training load chart [25]; the others are the planner's interpolation toward the Epley formula's 60 % at 20 reps, not measurements.

On a switch, each exercise keeps its implied 1RM, its target moves to the bottom of the new range at the new RIR, and the first session is 5 % lighter. Example: chest press 60 kg × 8 at RIR 2 in fat-loss mode is 75 % of 1RM, so the implied 1RM is 80 kg. Strength mode starts at 3 reps at RIR 2, which is 87 %: `80 × 0.87 × 0.95 = 66 kg`, rounded down to the stack's step. From that same set the first session moves about +10 % for strength, +5 % for maintenance, −5 % for muscle growth and −20 % for endurance. The [load calculator](/fitness/tools) estimates the 1RM from any set and the load for other reps and RIR; up to 12 reps its formula agrees with this table within one percentage point.

The table is an average. At 70 % of 1RM people manage about 14 reps on the bench press but about 19 on the leg press, and the spread between people at 80 % is about ±2.5 reps [26]. After the first session my own logged sets take over. I switch at the end of a week and not more often than every four weeks; shorter changes are adjustments, not new modes.

## What this means for me

My situation: fat loss with a strength lean, three upper-body machine days with core work, no legs yet, and an eye on the long run.

- **Mode: fat loss & recomposition.** Main lifts use 6–10 reps at RIR 2: heavy enough to protect strength, and wide enough that a 5 kg jump on a weight stack can be earned with reps. The secondary presses and the assisted pull-up get 8–12 reps at RIR 2, as do loaded abs; isolation gets 10–15 and small muscles 12–20, both at RIR 1.
- **Priorities:** the chest press, pec deck and assisted pull-up carry an extra set, and chest-and-back and biceps-and-triceps pairs run as supersets, so a session takes about an hour.
- **Volume:** the three days give chest and back roughly 16–19 fractional sets a week, at or a little above the top of the fat-loss band (16). When recovery slips, I cut sets before weight.
- **Success** is held or slowly rising weights while the waist shrinks; a flat estimated 1RM during a cut is normal [19].
- **Cardio** comes after lifting: every day ends with a 12–15-minute finisher, and a rest day gets a 4 × 4 interval session [22]. The [longevity explainer](/fitness/science/longevity) has the details.
- **After the cut** I switch to muscle growth (at maintenance calories or a small surplus) or to strength. Weights convert and exercises stay, so the history stays valid.
- **Bone, honestly:** seated, back-supported machines load my arms and shoulders, not my hips. Bone responds only where it is loaded [27]; the trial that raised bone density used heavy deadlifts, squats and overhead presses [28]; and dieting itself costs about 1–1.5 % of hip bone density [29]. Upper-body machine work does next to nothing for my hips. The program itself has one loaded carry, the farmer's carry on day C. The rest are optional additions, not template slots: a suitcase carry at the end of day A makes two carries a week, and the legs add-on, off by default, brings in the [leg press](/fitness/exercises/leg-press-45) around week 5 (week 1 if the cut runs past 12 weeks or 10 % of body weight) and heavy [trap-bar deadlifts](/fitness/exercises/trap-bar-deadlift) from about week 13.

## Caveats

- Most trials last 8–12 weeks and study young adults, mostly men (in the 2026 meta-regression the programs lasted about 10 weeks and the average age was 25 [2]); averages hide large individual differences.
- RIR in the trials was estimated, not measured; the link between effort and growth is consistent in direction but modest in size [6][9].
- The weekly set bands, the 40 % deload and the 10 % reset after misses are planner defaults built on meta-analyses, surveys, a consensus and one trial of complete rest [2][16][17][18], not on trials of these exact rules.
- This page explains evidence. It is not medical advice.
