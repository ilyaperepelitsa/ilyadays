Most of what goes wrong in a gym is predictable: a jump in load, a set ground out with failing form, a shoulder in a bad position, a warning sign ignored. These are the rules the planner follows and the ones I follow myself, with the evidence behind each.

> **In short:** injuries are rare and mostly overuse. Warm up for five minutes plus ramp sets, add load in small steps, end every set one to three reps short of failure or at the first broken rep, keep the shoulders out of the "high-five" position, and use the pain traffic light: 0–3 carry on, 4–5 modify, 6 or more stop.

## How risky is lifting?

A review of 20 studies across the weight-training sports found roughly 2–4 injuries per 1,000 hours of training, and bodybuilding, the closest thing to ordinary gym training, was the lowest [1]. The only prospective study of recreational gym-goers counted about two injuries per 1,000 hours, and a quarter of them reported one over six months [3]. At three hours a week, that is about one notable injury every three to seven years.

| Activity | Injuries per 1,000 hours | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bodybuilding | 0.24–1 | [1] |
| Recreational resistance training | about 2 | [3] |
| Powerlifting | 1.0–4.4 | [2] |
| Weightlifting | 2.4–3.3 | [2] |
| Team sports such as soccer and rugby | about 15–81 | [1] |

The same body parts top almost every list: shoulder, lower back, knee, elbow and wrist [1]. The shoulder alone accounts for up to 36 % of resistance-training injuries [4]. In US emergency departments the leading cause was a weight dropped on the body, and free weights were involved in nine cases out of ten [5]. A previous injury roughly doubles the chance of hurting the same area again [6]. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand finds no increase in serious adverse events from resistance training in healthy adults of any age [7].

## How lifters get hurt

Four mechanisms cover most of it, and each has a guard built into the plan:

| Mechanism | Evidence | Guard in the plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Load spikes | Sudden jumps in load are the classic route to overuse injury [8] | Reps rise before the weight does; load steps stay near 10 % |
| Fatigue breaks technique | Tired back muscles let the spine bend more, and its bending moment rose 36 % [9]; the bar path drifts near failure [10] | Sets end one to three reps short of failure, or at the first rep that looks different |
| Ego loading | Lifters pressed more weight in front of an audience [11] | No true one-rep maxes; strength is estimated from ordinary sets |
| Too little preparation | Blamed by injured lifters themselves; weak evidence [1] | A five-minute warm-up and ramp sets |

The ACSM now states that training to failure does not add strength, size or power [7]. The best-documented protection is the training itself: across randomised trials, strength training cut sports injuries to about a third, while stretching made no difference [12].

## The warm-up the planner prescribes

The warm-up follows the RAMP idea: raise body temperature, activate and mobilise the working joints, then potentiate with ramp sets [13]. Muscle temperature starts climbing within three to five minutes of easy exercise [14]. Adding easy cycling to a lifting-specific warm-up raised the leg-press one-rep max by 8.4 % [15], but fifteen minutes at a hard pace lowered it by about 4 % [16]. Before heavy sets, a ramp that included a set at 80 % of the working weight beat light sets alone [17]. Static stretches of 60 seconds or more per muscle cost about 4.6 % of performance; short ones about 1 % [18].

What the planner does:

1. **General warm-up:** five minutes of easy cardio (bike, rower or brisk incline walk) at a pace where you can still talk in full sentences. I stretch it to eight to ten minutes on heavy days, after a week off or in a cold gym.
2. **Shoulder activation**, my addition when a band or cable is free: 15 band pull-aparts and 12–15 external rotations per arm before the first press or pull.
3. **Ramp sets** before the first working set of every multi-joint exercise, as a share of that day's working weight:

| Working weight | Ramp sets |
| --- | --- |
| Below 80 % of the estimated one-rep max | 50 % × 8 |
| 80 % or more (before there is an estimate: target reps plus reps in reserve of 8 or fewer) | 50 % × 8, 70 % × 4, 85 % × 2 |

Single-joint machines, holds, carries and bodyweight moves get no ramp. A second multi-joint exercise for muscles that are already warm keeps only its heaviest ramp set. Steps are rounded to the machine, and duplicates are dropped. In the fat-loss mode (6–10 reps with two in reserve) that usually means a single ramp set. The ladder is adapted from the NSCA one-rep-max testing warm-up [19]; the exact percentages are coaching practice.

No warm-up has been shown to prevent injuries on the day [20]. I keep it because the first working set goes better, and the last ramp set doubles as a pain check: a joint that hurts at 70 % will hurt more at 100 %.

## Shoulders

Most shoulder trouble in lifters comes from one position, the "high-five": arm out to the side, rotated back, elbow behind the body [4][21]. In a review of 112 ruptures of the big chest muscle, weight training was the most common setting, with the arm out to the side and rotated back under load [22]. The rules:

- **Nothing behind the neck.** Pull and press in front of the head; the front pulldown works the back muscles at least as well [23].
- **Presses:** hands no wider than about 1.5 times shoulder width, elbows 45–60° from the body, shoulder blades pulled back. In a 2024 model, narrower grips and retracted shoulder blades lowered the load on the collarbone joint and the shear inside the shoulder [24].
- **Pec deck and flyes:** set the start so the arms open only to the line of the torso, never behind it [21].
- **Raises:** to shoulder height, thumbs neutral. Lifters who raised above 90° showed more signs of impingement, and those who trained the external rotators showed fewer [25].

A shoulder routine done three times a week cut shoulder problems by 28 % in elite handball players [26], which is why face pulls stay in the program.

## Lower back

Brace by tightening all around the trunk, as if about to be poked, instead of sucking the belly in: in a laboratory model, bracing made the spine 32 % more stable than hollowing [27]. Keep the spine roughly neutral under load, but don't fear every bend: low-quality evidence finds no link between bending the lower back while lifting and later back pain [28]. Exercise choice protects more. Of three rows tested, the standing bent-over row put the highest load on the lower back [29], so my rows are chest-supported or seated.

Short breath-holds on the hardest reps are normal, but blood pressure reached about 320/250 mmHg during heavy leg presses [30], so anyone with high blood pressure should avoid long breath-holds and grinding sets. The lower-back machine trains the lumbar muscles only when the pelvis is strapped in [31]; start it light and slow.

## Elbows, wrists and neck

Curls, pushdowns and presses load small tendons, and tendon pain is a load problem rather than a rest problem [32]. When an elbow complains, I halve that exercise's sets, take 10–20 % off, switch to a neutral or EZ grip and lower over three seconds, then build back up. Heavy slow resistance and eccentric programmes work in tendon trials [33][34]. Isometric holds can ease tendon pain for a while, though not reliably better than slow reps [35]. In ab work the neck stays still: hands on the chest or the machine handles, never pulling on the head.

## Tendons are slower than muscles

In a 12-week study, strength rose by about 30 % within two months, while tendon stiffness did not change until the third month [36]. Tendons respond mainly to loads above about 70 % of maximum [37], and the collagen turnover after a session lasts about three days [38]. That is the reason for small steps, reps before load, and at least 48 hours between hard sessions for the same muscles; the A/B/C rotation over three non-consecutive days does that by design.

## The pain traffic light

In Silbernagel's pain-monitoring model, people with Achilles tendon pain kept exercising with pain up to 5 out of 10, provided it settled by the next morning and did not climb from week to week, and they did as well as those who rested [39]. In chronic muscle and joint pain, exercising into some pain was no worse than pain-free exercise [40]. Because I coach myself, the app uses a stricter version:

| Pain during the set (0–10) | Level | What to do today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 and steady | Green | Carry on and progress as planned |
| 4–5, or rising from set to set | Amber | Modify: 10–20 % lighter, a shorter range, a three-second lowering or a pain-free swap from the same family; no progression until two green sessions |
| 6 or more, or any sharp, catching or radiating pain | Red | Stop that exercise and train other areas |

Pain must be back to its usual level by the next morning, and it must not trend upward week to week. Soreness that appears 12–24 hours after a session, peaks at 24–72 hours and fades within a week is delayed-onset muscle soreness, not injury [41]; it shrinks after the first exposure to an exercise [42].

## Red flags: a doctor, not the app

- Chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, dizziness or fainting [43].
- A sudden, severe headache during or right after a lift [44].
- Numbness, tingling or weakness running down an arm or leg, or new bladder or bowel problems [45].
- A pop followed by bruising, weakness or a changed muscle shape; torn tendons repair best early [22].
- Dark, cola-coloured urine with severe muscle pain or swelling one to three days after a hard session, a sign of muscle breakdown [46].

## Sleep and recovery

Adults need at least seven hours of sleep [47]. One night without sleep cut muscle protein synthesis by 18 % [48], and short sleep lowers performance by about 7.6 % [49]. The planner does not read sleep yet, so after a night under six hours I skip top sets and keep one more rep in reserve on the big lifts. Planned deloads reduce training rather than stop it: in a 2024 trial, a full week off in the middle of a nine-week programme blunted strength gains [50]. ## What this means for me

- **Before each session:** five minutes on the bike, band pull-aparts and external rotations if a band is free, then the ramp sets the app shows.
- **Machine setup first:** the chest-press handles start at mid-chest, the pec-deck arms stop at the line of my torso, and the pulldown bar comes to the upper chest, never behind the neck.
- **Lower-back machine** on day B: pelvis strapped in, two seconds back and three forward, well short of failure for the first weeks.
- **Crunch machine and knee raises:** the neck moves with the trunk, and on the captain's chair I curl the pelvis up instead of swinging the legs.
- **Farmer's carry:** hinge to pick the dumbbells up and set them down before the grip fails, so nothing lands on a foot.
- **Every set:** stop at the first rep that looks different from the first one, rate pain against the traffic light, and treat amber as "modify", not "push through".
- **No legs yet.** When the legs block starts, the leg press goes only as deep as my pelvis stays on the pad, with the safety stops engaged.

## Caveats

- Injury rates come mostly from retrospective, self-reported studies with different definitions of an injury; treat them as an order of magnitude [1].
- The shoulder and lower-back rules rest on biomechanical models, muscle-activity studies and case series. No trial randomises technique and counts injuries [4][24].
- The pain model was tested in Achilles tendon pain under supervision and is extrapolated here to the shoulder and elbow [39].
- This page explains training; it is not medical advice. Pain that does not settle after two weeks of modified training deserves a physiotherapist or a doctor.
