MARCUS AURELIUS, MEDITATIONS
A new English translation from the Greek (project-v1)

Made 2026-11-23. All 418 passages of the Meditations, in the 38 sections of the Stoic Constitution. Every passage was translated from the Greek alone by one model, checked against the Greek by a second, and each point was settled in writing by a third reading. The owner read the first six on 23 November 2026 and said “looks good”; the other 412 have not been read by the owner. None has been checked by a person who reads Greek.
How it was made:
- A first translator was given the Greek of a section’s passages and nothing else: no earlier translation, no article. It was asked for plain English of today that keeps the speaker, the person addressed, every “all” and “not”, and each image, and to translate one reading and note the other wherever the Greek is open.
- A second reader was given only the Greek and that English, without the first translator’s notes, and asked to list every place where the English says more, less or other than the Greek. Across the book it raised 1,458 points; it called 132 passages sound, 275 open to minor fixes, and 5 wrong in a way that changed what Marcus says.
- Each point was then settled in writing: accepted (969), partly accepted (465) or declined (189), with the reason. For the first six passages the editor of this project settled them; for the rest a third reader did, given the Greek, the draft and the points, and it also fixed 137 faults neither earlier reader had caught (marked “Found while settling”). The result is the English shown here. Under each passage you can open both lists: what the Greek leaves open (3,201 notes), and what the second reader questioned.
- All three readers were the same model, Claude Fable 5.2, each in a fresh context that saw only what its brief allowed. Most sections were read in Claude Code; in eleven sections at least one reading was made through the Anthropic API with the same briefs. The briefs are kept in the project (translation-translator.md, translation-checker.md, translation-settler.md).
- So that a Greek word does not become a different English word from one passage to the next, the settled renderings of the first six passages were given to every reader as preferences (for example “the universe” for κόσμος, “the nature of the whole”, “the ruling part”); where a passage’s own argument needed another word, a note says so.
- The 1634 translation numbers and cuts its sections differently from modern editions. Each of its passages was matched to the Greek by reading the openings and endings side by side, after a whole-book alignment had proposed the matches; together the 418 passages cover every chapter of the Greek exactly once. Where a passage joins two or more modern chapters (58 passages), they are shown as one with the chapter breaks kept (6.9–10). Where it cuts inside a chapter (63 passages), the passage carries a letter (11.8a is the first part, 3.11b a later part) and says which Greek it leaves out.
- Before a section was stored, its files were checked by script: every quoted Greek word is an exact part of the passage, every point has its decision, the typography is plain, no archaic forms. Where a reader had typed a Latin letter inside a quoted Greek word, or another Unicode form of the same accented letter, the quotation was corrected to the exact characters of the source; no wording was changed that way.
What it cannot promise:
- All three readers are language models. A model’s Greek can be confidently wrong; the Greek is shown beside the English so that anyone who reads Greek can object.
- The Greek is a digitised copy of Leopold’s edition and has slips of its own (an accent, a breathing, a misprinted letter). Where a slip was read through, a note says so. Where the text itself does not construe (for example 8.35, 9.28a, 7.31b), the English marks the place and does not smooth it over.
- Notes that rely on memory of the manuscripts or of an editor’s conjecture say “from memory, unchecked” (124 notes). None was checked against a critical edition. In 5.8 the source prints ἐκεῖ σε (“you there”) where the text may be the one word ἐκεῖσε (“to that place”); the printed edition was not consulted. Two scraps of the source are outside every passage: the note at the end of Book 1 (Τὰ ἐν Κουάδοις πρὸς τῷ Γρανούᾳ) and a stray chapter numeral inside 12.17.
- The passage numbers are those of modern editions; the 1634 number is given beside each. The sections were grouped on the 1634 English, not on this translation. On the new English, 352 of the 418 passages are still nearest their own section; the table above lists the rest by section. No passage was moved.
- A translated passage is never edited in place. A correction is a new revision with a new id; the old one is kept in the record.
Source text: M. Antoninus Imperator ad se ipsum, edited by Jan Hendrik Leopold, 1908. Digitised text from the Perseus Digital Library (CC BY-SA); the Greek is not included in this file.
Passages are numbered by book and chapter as in modern editions. A dash joins chapters translated as one passage; a letter marks part of a chapter.

BOOK 1

1.1–4  From my grandfather Verus: good character and freedom from anger.

From the reputation and memory of the man who fathered me: modesty and manliness.

From my mother: reverence for the gods, and readiness to share, and holding back not only from doing wrong but even from coming to such a thought; and also plainness in the way of living, far from the way the rich live.

From my great-grandfather: not going to public lecture rooms, and having good teachers at home, and learning that on such things one must spend unsparingly.

1.5  From the man who brought me up: becoming neither a Green nor a Blue, nor a Small-Shield or a Big-Shield man; and bearing hard work, and needing little; and doing things with my own hands, and not meddling; and being slow to accept slander.

1.6  From Diognetus: not being busy over empty things; and disbelieving what is said by those who deal in marvels and by sorcerers about spells and about the driving out of spirits and things of that kind; and not keeping quails, nor getting excited over such things; and putting up with plain speaking; and becoming at home in philosophy, and hearing the lectures first of Baccheius, then of Tandasis and Marcianus; and writing dialogues as a boy; and wanting a pallet bed and an animal skin, and everything of that kind that belongs to the Greek training.

1.7  From Rusticus: getting the impression that my character needed correcting and treatment; and not being turned aside into emulating the sophists, nor writing about the doctrines, or delivering little speeches of exhortation, or making a show, to strike the imagination, of the man of discipline or the man of action; and keeping away from rhetoric and poetry and clever talk; and not walking about at home in formal dress, nor doing things of that kind; and writing short letters plainly, like the one he himself wrote to my mother from Sinuessa; and, towards those who have been angry and have done wrong, being easy to win back and easy to reconcile, as soon as they themselves are willing to come back; and reading with care, and not being content with a rough grasp of the whole, nor quickly assenting to glib talkers; and getting to read the notes of Epictetus’s teaching, which he shared with me from his own copy.

1.8  From Apollonius: freedom, and unmistakably leaving nothing to the dice, and looking to nothing other than reason, not even for a little while; and being always the same, in sharp pains, at the loss of a child, in long illnesses; and seeing clearly, in a living example, that the same man can be most intense and can be relaxed; and not being irritable when explaining; and seeing a man who plainly counted his experience and skill in passing on the doctrines as the least of his good qualities; and learning how one should take from friends what are counted as favours, neither being lowered because of them nor letting them go by without feeling.

1.9  From Sextus: kindliness; and the example of a household governed by its father; and the idea of living according to nature; and dignity without pretence; and skill in reading his friends, with care for them; and patience with lay people and with those who hold unexamined opinions; and adapting himself readily to everyone, so that his company was more pleasant than any flattery, and yet at that very moment those same people held him in the greatest respect; and finding out and setting in order, with a sure grasp and by method, the principles necessary for life; and never giving so much as a sign of anger or of any other passion, but being at once most free of passion and most affectionate; and praise given quietly, and wide learning without display.

1.10  From Alexander the grammarian: not to find fault, and not to pounce reproachfully on people who have come out with a wrong form of a word, a mistake in grammar or a jarring sound, but deftly to come out with just the very thing that should have been said, by way of an answer, or of backing them up, or of a joint discussion about the matter itself, not about the expression, or through some other such well-tuned reminder in passing.

1.11  From Fronto: to have taken note of what a tyrant’s envy, shiftiness and play-acting are like, and that on the whole these people we call patricians are somehow rather lacking in affection.

1.12  From Alexander the Platonist: not often or without need to say to anyone, or write in a letter, “I am busy”; and not to keep excusing oneself in this way from the duties that relations with those one lives among call for, by pleading the business that surrounds one.

1.13  From Catulus: not making light of a friend who finds some fault, even if they happen to be finding fault without reason, but trying actually to bring them back to their usual self; and speaking well of one’s teachers wholeheartedly, as in what is recorded about Domitius and Athenodotus; and true love for one’s children.

1.14  From my brother Severus: love of family, love of truth and love of justice; and through him coming to know Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dio, Brutus, and getting an impression of a state with equal laws, governed on the basis of equality and an equal right to speak, and of a kingship that honours above all things the freedom of those it rules; and also from the same man: evenness and steadiness in the honour paid to […]; and doing good and sharing readily, unsparingly; and hopefulness, and trust that he was loved by his friends; and being open with those who met with his censure; and that his friends had no need to guess about what he wanted or did not want, but that it was plain.

1.15  From Maximus: self-mastery and being carried off course in nothing; and good spirits in illness as in other hard circumstances; and a character well blended, mild and dignified; and getting through what lay before him without complaining; and that everyone believed of him that whatever he said was what he thought, and that whatever he did he was not acting badly. And not being amazed, and not being startled, and never hurrying or hanging back or at a loss or downcast or putting on a grin, or again angry or suspicious; and doing good, and forgiving, and not lying; and giving the impression of one not bent rather than of one being straightened; and that no one would ever have thought they were looked down on by him, nor have gone so far as to suppose themselves better than him; and being pleasantly witty.

1.16  From my father: gentleness, and holding firm, unshaken, to what had been decided after examination; and no empty pride in what are counted as honours; and love of work, and perseverance; and listening to those who had something to bring to the common good; and giving each person what they deserved, without being turned aside; and knowing from experience where there is need to tighten and where to slacken; and putting a stop to love affairs with youths; and his sense of being one among others, and that his friends were not bound to dine with him every time, nor obliged to travel with him, and that those who had been kept away by some need always found him the same; and careful inquiry in council, and persistence, and not “he gave up the search too soon, content with the impressions that lay to hand”; and keeping his friends, never tiring of them quickly nor being infatuated with them; and self-sufficiency in everything, and cheerfulness; and foresight from far off, and arranging the smallest things in advance without making a drama of it; and that acclamations and all flattery were held in check under him; and always watching over what the empire needed, and managing its spending thriftily, and putting up with the blame that came for some things of this kind; and being neither superstitious about the gods, nor, with people, a courter of the public or eager to please or fond of the crowd, but sober in everything and firm, and never lacking in taste, and no lover of novelty; and using the things that add to the ease of life, of which fortune gave him plenty, without conceit and at the same time without apology, so that when they were there he took them without affectation, and when they were not he did not need them; and that no one could have said he was a sophist, or a home-bred buffoon, or a pedant, but a ripe man, complete, beyond flattery, able to take charge of his own affairs and other people’s. Besides these: honouring those who truly practise philosophy, and not reproaching the others, nor yet being easily led by them; and again, being good company and gracious, but not to excess; and taking care of his own body in due measure, not as someone in love with life would, nor for the sake of looks, nor yet carelessly, but so that through his own attention he needed very little from medicine, or from drugs and external dressings; and above all, giving way without envy to those who had some ability, such as ability in speaking, or the ability that comes from the study of laws or customs or some other matters, and joining eagerly in their efforts, so that each should be well thought of according to their own strengths; and doing everything by the ancestral ways, without setting out to be seen doing even this, keeping the ancestral ways. And again: not being easily shifted and restless, but staying long in the same places and at the same business; and after his attacks of headache, coming straight back fresh and at full strength to his usual work; and that he did not have many secrets, but very few and very rarely, and those only about public matters; and good sense and measure in putting on shows, in building works, in handouts and things of that kind, which is the way of a person who looks to the thing itself that has to be done, not to the good name that comes of having done it. Not one to bathe at odd hours, not fond of building, not one to be inventive about food, nor about the weave and colours of clothes, nor about the beauty of bodies. The formal dress from Lorium, coming up from the villa below; a tunic at Lanuvium for the most part; how he dealt with the cloak at Tusculum that was asking to be excused; and all that way of behaving. Nothing harsh, nor yet relentless, nor violent, nor such that one could ever have said “to the point of sweat”; but everything reasoned out, each part taken separately, as if at leisure, without disturbance, in order, with strength, in agreement with itself. What is recorded of Socrates would fit him: that he was able both to go without and to enjoy those things which most people are weak at going without and give way in enjoying. To be strong, and to hold out and stay sober in each of the two, is the mark of a man who has a soul that is sound and unbeaten, as in the illness of Maximus.

1.17a  From the gods: that I had good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good people close to me, relatives, friends, nearly all of them; and that I was not carried away into any offence against any of them, although I had a disposition from which, if it had so happened, I might well have done something of the kind; but it is the gods’ kindness that no combination of circumstances came about that would have shown me up. And that I was not brought up for longer with my grandfather’s concubine; and that I preserved the bloom of my youth; and that I was not made a man before the season, but even took some time more. That I was placed under a ruler and father who was to take all the conceit out of me and bring me to the thought that it is possible to live at a court and need neither bodyguards, nor clothes that mark one out, nor torches and statues of this sort and similar show; but that one may draw oneself in until one is very nearly a private person and not, on that account, be more lowly or more slack in what has to be done as a leader for the common good. That I happened to have such a brother, able by his character to rouse me to take care of myself, and at the same time gladdening me with his respect and affection. That my children were not born lacking in natural gifts, nor deformed in their little bodies. That I did not make more progress in rhetoric, in poetry and in the other pursuits, in which I might perhaps have been held fast, if I had felt myself getting on easily. That I was quick to set those who brought me up in the rank they seemed to me to desire, and did not put it off with the hope that, since they were still young, I would do it later. That I came to know Apollonius, Rusticus, Maximus. That I had an impression, clearly and often, of the life according to nature, of what kind of life it is, so that, as far as it rests with the gods and with the gifts, help and inspirations that come from there, nothing prevents me from living according to nature already, and that I still fall short of this is by my own fault and by not keeping to the reminders from the gods, which are all but teachings. That my body has held out so long in a life like this. That I touched neither Benedicta nor Theodotus, but that later too, when I fell into the passions of love, I recovered. That, though I was often angry with Rusticus, I did nothing more that I would have regretted. That my mother, who was to die young, still lived with me in her last years. That whenever I wanted to help someone who was poor or in need of something else, I was never told that I had no money to do it with; and that no similar need befell me myself, so as to take from another. That my wife is as she is, so compliant, so warmly affectionate, so unaffected. That I had no lack of suitable people to bring up my children. That remedies were given me through dreams, among others against spitting blood and against dizziness; and of this, at Caieta, as you will use. That, when I set my heart on philosophy, I did not fall into the hands of some sophist, nor sit down to writing, or to resolving syllogisms, or to busying myself with the things in the sky. For all these things “need the gods’ help and fortune.”



BOOK 2

2.1  At dawn, say to yourself beforehand: I shall meet a meddler, an ungrateful person, an insolent one, a deceitful one, an envious one, an unsocial one. All these things have come upon them through ignorance of what is good and bad. But I, who have seen the nature of the good, that it is honourable, and of the bad, that it is shameful, and the nature of the very person who does wrong, that they are akin to me, sharing not the same blood or seed but mind and a portion of the divine, can neither be harmed by any of them, for no one will wrap me in what is shameful, nor be angry with one who is akin to me, nor hate them. For we have come into being to work together, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. So to work against one another is against nature; and to be indignant and to turn away is working against.

2.2  Whatever this is that I am, it is bits of flesh, a little breath, and the ruling part. Let the books go. Be pulled no longer. It is not allowed. Instead, as one already dying, despise the bits of flesh: gore, little bones, a web, a small plaiting of sinews, little veins and arteries. Look too at what kind of thing the breath is: wind, and not even always the same, but at every moment vomited out and gulped in again. Third, then, is the ruling part. Think of it this way: you are old. No longer let this be a slave, no longer let it be pulled about on strings by unsocial impulse, no longer let it be discontented with what is fated now or look with suspicion on what is fated to come.

2.3  What comes from the gods is full of providence. What comes from chance is not without nature, or without the spinning together and interweaving of the things that providence governs. Everything flows from there. And there is besides what is necessary, and what benefits the whole universe, of which you are a part. And what the nature of the whole brings is good for every part of nature, and so is what preserves that nature. And the universe is preserved by changes: those of the elements and, in the same way, those of compound things. Let this be enough for you, and let these be your principles. As for your thirst for books, throw it away, so that you do not die grumbling, but truly gracious, and thankful to the gods from your heart.

2.4  Remember how long you have been putting these things off, and how many times you have been given a set time by the gods and make no use of it. Now at last you must perceive what universe you are a part of, and from what governor of the universe you came into being as an outflow, and that a limit of time has been marked out for you. If you do not use it to clear away the clouds, it will be gone, you will be gone, and it will not be possible again.

2.5  At every hour take firm care, as a Roman and a man, to do what is in hand with exact and unfeigned dignity, affection, freedom and justice, and to win yourself respite from all other impressions. You will win it if you carry out each action as the last of your life, rid of all aimlessness, of turning away in passion from what reason decides, of pretence, of self-love and of discontent with what has been allotted to you. You see how few the things are which, once mastered, let a person live a life that flows smoothly and is god-fearing: for the gods will demand nothing more of whoever keeps to these.

2.6  Do violence, do violence to yourself, soul; but you will have no chance left to honour yourself. For each person has one life, and yours is almost finished, while you do not respect yourself but place your good fortune in the souls of others.

2.7  Do the things that fall on you from outside distract you at all? Then give yourself leisure to learn some further good thing, and stop wandering. But now there is the other kind of drifting to guard against as well. For people play the fool through their actions too, those who are worn out by life and have no target at which to direct every impulse and, in general, every impression.

2.8  Hardly anyone has been seen to be unhappy through not attending to what goes on in another person’s soul. But those who do not keep track of the movements of their own soul are bound to be unhappy.

2.9  These things must always be remembered: what the nature of the whole is, and what mine is, and how this one stands to that one, and what kind of part it is of what kind of whole; and that there is no one who prevents you from always doing and saying what is in keeping with the nature of which you are a part.

2.10  Theophrastus speaks as a philosopher when, in his comparison of wrongdoings (as one might compare such things in a more everyday way), he says that offences committed from desire are more serious than those committed from anger. For the one who is angry is seen to turn away from reason with a certain pain and a shrinking inside that goes unnoticed; but the one who does wrong from desire, overcome by pleasure, is seen to be somehow more unrestrained and more womanish in their wrongdoing. Rightly, then, and in a way worthy of philosophy, he said that the wrong done with pleasure is open to a greater charge than the wrong done with pain. And in general the one is more like a person who has first been treated unjustly and has been forced by pain into anger; the other is moved to do injustice by an impulse of their own, carried towards doing something from desire.

2.11  To do and say and think each thing in the knowledge that it is possible to leave life at this very moment. As for going away from among human beings: if gods exist, it is nothing to fear, for they would not throw you into anything bad. And if either they do not exist or they do not care about human affairs, what is it to me to live in a universe empty of gods or empty of providence? But they do exist, and they do care about human affairs; and they have put it wholly in a person’s own power not to fall into the things that are truly bad. And if any of the rest were bad, they would have provided for that too, so that it would be in everyone’s power not to fall into it. But what does not make a human being worse, how could that make a human being’s life worse? The nature of the whole would not have overlooked these things, either through ignorance or because, though it knew, it could not guard against them beforehand or set them right; nor would it have made so great a mistake, through lack of power or lack of skill, that good things and bad things happen alike and in a jumble to good people and to bad. Now death and life, good repute and ill repute, pain and pleasure, wealth and poverty, all these happen alike to the good and to the bad among human beings, and they are neither fine nor shameful. So they are neither good nor bad.

2.12a  How quickly all things vanish, the bodies themselves into the universe, the memories of them into eternity. What all the things perceived by the senses are like, and above all those that lure with pleasure or frighten with pain or are loudly celebrated through vanity: how cheap and contemptible and filthy and quick to perish and dead.

2.12b  To attend [to these things] belongs to the power of mind. What these people are, whose judgments and voices give a good name. What dying is, and that, if a person looks at it by itself alone and, by dividing up the notion, dissolves the things imagined into it, they will no longer judge it to be anything other than a work of nature; and if anyone is afraid of a work of nature, they are a child. This, though, is not only a work of nature but also something good for nature.

2.12c–13  How a human being touches god, and with what part of themselves, and in what state this part of the human being is when that happens.

Nothing is more wretched than a person who goes round everything in a circle and, as it is said, “searches into the things beneath the earth”, and looks by guesswork for what is in the souls of their neighbours, but does not notice that it is enough to keep only to the guardian spirit within them and to serve it truly. To serve it is to keep it clean of passion and aimlessness and discontent with what comes from the gods and from people. For what comes from the gods deserves reverence because of their excellence; and what comes from people is dear because of kinship, and sometimes also, in a way, pitiable because of their ignorance of what is good and bad. This disability is no less than the one that takes away the power to tell white from black.

2.14  Even if you are going to live three thousand years, or even three times ten thousand, still remember that no one loses any life other than this one they are living, and no one lives any life other than the one they are losing. So the longest comes to the same thing as the shortest. For the present is equal for everyone, so what perishes is equal too, and in this way what is lost shows itself to be an instant. For no one could lose either the past or the future, for how could anyone take from a person what that person does not have? So these two things must be remembered. One is that all things from eternity are of the same kind and come round in a circle, and that it makes no difference whether a person will see the same things for a hundred years, or for two hundred, or for time without limit. The other is that the one who lives longest and the one who will die soonest lose an equal amount. For the present is the only thing a person is going to be deprived of, if indeed this is the only thing they have, and a person does not lose what they do not have.

2.15  That everything is judgment. For what is said in answer to Monimus the Cynic is plain; but plain too is the usefulness of the saying, if one takes what is nourishing in it so far as it is true.

2.16  The human soul does violence to itself most of all when it becomes, so far as it lies with it, an abscess, a thing standing apart, and a kind of growth on the universe. For to be vexed at anything that happens is a standing apart from nature, in some part of which the nature of each of the other things is contained. Next, when it turns away from some person, or even moves against them with the aim of doing harm, as the souls of angry people do. Third, it does violence to itself when it is beaten by pleasure or by pain. Fourth, when it plays a part and does or says something in a feigned and untrue way. Fifth, when it lets some action or impulse of its own fly at no target, but does anything whatever at random and without attention, when even the smallest things ought to be done with reference to the end. And the end of rational living beings is to follow the reason and law of the oldest city and constitution.

2.17  In human life, the time is a point, the substance is in flux, perception is dim, the compound of the whole body is ready to rot, the soul is a whirl, fortune is hard to make out, fame is undiscerning. To put it briefly: everything of the body is a river, everything of the soul is a dream and smoke, life is a war and a stranger’s stay abroad, and fame after death is forgetting. What, then, can escort a person through? One thing and only one: philosophy. And this lies in keeping the spirit within free from abuse and unharmed, stronger than pleasures and pains, doing nothing at random, nothing falsely or with pretense, not needing another person to do something or not do it; and further, accepting what happens and what is allotted as coming from that same place, wherever it is, from which it came itself; and, on top of all this, waiting for death with a cheerful mind, as nothing other than a dissolving of the elements of which every living thing is put together. And if for the elements themselves there is nothing terrible in each one continually changing into another, why should anyone look with suspicion on the change and dissolution of all? For it is according to nature, and nothing that is according to nature is bad. At Carnuntum.



BOOK 3

3.1  It is not only this that has to be reckoned with, that life is being used up day by day and a smaller part of it is left. This too has to be reckoned with: if a person were to live longer, this at least is unclear, whether their mind will later on be equal, unchanged, to the understanding of things and of the study that is directed toward close acquaintance with divine and human things. For if a person begins to lose their wits, there will be no lack of breathing, of taking nourishment, of having impressions and impulses, and of everything else of that kind. But making use of oneself, getting every point of duty exact, setting out clearly what appears before one, stopping to consider this very question, whether it is now time to lead oneself out of life, and everything of that kind that needs thoroughly trained reasoning: this is extinguished first. So one must hurry, not only because one comes nearer to death at every moment, but also because the grasp of things and the power to follow them give out first.

3.2  One should also take note of things like this: that even what comes about on top of the things that come about by nature has something graceful and attractive about it. For example, when bread is baking, some parts of it break open at the side; and so these parts too, which stand open like this and are in a way contrary to what the baker’s craft sets out to do, somehow look right and stir up an eagerness for the food in a way of their own. Again, figs gape open when they are at their ripest; and in olives ripened on the tree, the very nearness to rotting adds a certain beauty of its own to the fruit. And ears of grain bending down, and the frown on the lion’s brow, and the foam running from the mouths of wild boars, and many other things are far from good-looking if one looks at them by themselves; and yet, because they come as consequences of the things that come about by nature, they help to adorn them and they charm the mind. So if someone has a deeper feeling for, and understanding of, the things that come about in the whole, there is almost nothing, even among the things that happen as consequences, that will not seem to that person to be put together in a way that somehow gives pleasure. And that person will look even at the real gaping jaws of wild animals with no less pleasure than at all those that painters and sculptors show in imitation; and will be able to see a kind of prime and ripeness in an old woman and an old man, and to see with their own chaste eyes the seductive charm of the young. And many such things, which will not convince everyone, will strike only the one who has become truly at home with nature and its works.

3.3  Hippocrates, after curing many illnesses, fell ill himself and died. The Chaldaeans foretold the deaths of many, and then what was fated caught up with them too. Alexander and Pompey and Gaius Caesar, after so many times wiping out whole cities completely and cutting down in battle many tens of thousands of horsemen and foot soldiers, one day went out of life themselves as well. Heraclitus, after saying so much as a student of nature about the burning up of the universe, was filled inside with water and died smeared with cow dung. Lice killed Democritus, and other lice killed Socrates. What of this? You went on board, you sailed, you came to harbour: get off. If to another life, nothing is empty of gods there either. And if in a state without sensation, you will stop putting up with pains and pleasures and being a slave to a vessel that is so much worse than the thing that serves it: for the one that serves is mind and divine spirit, the other earth and gore.

3.4  Do not wear away the part of your life that is left in impressions about other people, when you are not referring them to something of common benefit (for in truth you are deprived of other work). That is, forming impressions of what so-and-so is doing, and for what purpose, and what they are saying, and what they have in mind, and what they are contriving, and all such things as make you wander away from watching over your own ruling part. So you must avoid, in the chain of your impressions, even what is random and to no purpose, but far the most what is meddling and malicious. And you must accustom yourself to form only those impressions about which, if someone suddenly asked, “What are you thinking now?”, you would answer at once and frankly, “This, and this”; so that from them it would be plain straight away that everything is simple and kindly and belongs to a social animal, one that cares nothing for imaginings of pleasure or of enjoyment in general, or for any rivalry, or envy and suspicion, or anything else that you would blush to explain you had in your mind. For a man of this kind, who no longer puts off being, so to speak, among the best already, is a kind of priest and servant of the gods. He also makes use of what is enshrined within him, which makes the person unstained by pleasures, unwounded by any pain, untouched by any insult, insensible to any wickedness, an athlete in the greatest contest, the contest not to be thrown by any passion, dyed deep in justice, welcoming with his whole soul everything that happens and is dealt out, and not often, nor without a great need that serves the common benefit, forming an impression of what another person is saying or doing or thinking. For it is only what is his own that he has for his activity, and what is spun for him out of the whole he thinks of without a break; and the first he makes fine, and the second he is convinced are good. For the lot dealt to each person is carried along with them and carries them along with it. He remembers too that everything rational is kin, and that to care for all human beings is in keeping with the nature of a human being, but that the good opinion to hold on to is not that of everyone, but only of those who live in agreement with nature. As for those who do not live so, he keeps in mind all the time what sort of people they are at home and outside the house, by night and by day, and what they are like and with what kind of people they are mixed up. So he sets no store either by praise that comes from such people, who are not even pleased with themselves.

3.5a  Do not act unwillingly, or unsocially, or without examining, or while pulled the other way. Do not let elegance dress up your thought. Do not be a person of many words or a busybody. Further, let the god in you be the guardian of a living being that is male, old, engaged in public affairs, a Roman and a ruler, who has set himself in order, as someone would be who is waiting for the signal to withdraw from life, easily released, needing neither an oath nor any human being as witness.

3.5b  And let there be cheerfulness within, and no need of service from outside, and no need of the quiet that others provide. So one must be upright, not set upright.

3.6  If you find in human life anything better than justice, truth, self-control, courage, and, in a word, than your thought being content with itself in the things where it has you acting according to right reason, and in fate, in the things that are allotted without your choosing; if, I say, you see anything better than this, turn to it with your whole soul and enjoy what is found to be best. But if nothing appears better than the spirit itself that is seated in you, which has put its own impulses under itself, which examines the impressions, which has pulled itself away, as Socrates used to say, from the feelings of the senses, and has put itself under the gods, and cares for human beings; if you find everything else smaller and cheaper than this, give room to nothing else. Once you have tipped and turned aside toward anything else, you will no longer be able, without being pulled about, to honor above all else that good which is proper to you and is yours. For it is not permitted that anything whatever of a different kind, such as praise from the many, or offices, or wealth, or the enjoyment of pleasures, should take up a position against the rational and civic good. All these, even if for a little while they seem to fit, suddenly take control and carry you off course. But you, I say, simply and freely choose the better and hold on to it. “But the better is what benefits.” If it benefits you as a rational being, keep to that. But if as an animal, say so, and guard your verdict without conceit. Only see that you make the examination without slipping.

3.7  Never value as a benefit to yourself anything that will one day force you to break faith, to abandon your self-respect, to hate someone, to suspect, to curse, to play a part, to desire something that needs walls and curtains. For whoever has preferred their own mind and spirit, and the rites of its virtue, does not make a tragedy of things, does not groan, will need neither solitude nor a crowd. Greatest of all, they will live neither pursuing nor fleeing. Whether they will have the use of the soul, enclosed in the body, for a longer stretch of time or a shorter one matters to them not at all. For even if they have to depart this moment, they will go as free of ties as if they were about to do any other of the things that can be done with self-respect and in good order, on their guard through their whole life against this one thing only: that their thought should take any turn foreign to a living being that has mind and is civic.

3.8  In the mind of someone who has been disciplined and thoroughly cleansed you would find nothing full of pus, nor indeed anything stained, nor anything festering under the skin. Nor does fate overtake their life while it is unfinished, as one might say of a tragic actor who leaves before finishing and playing the play through. And further, nothing slavish, nothing affected, nothing tied to anything, nothing split off, nothing liable to be called to account, nothing lurking in a hole.

3.9–10  Revere the power of forming judgments. Everything lies in this, that there should no longer arise in your ruling part a judgment that does not follow nature and the constitution of the rational animal. And this promises freedom from haste, affinity with human beings, and following the gods.

So throw everything away and hold on to only these few things; and remember as well that each person lives only this present, an instant. The rest has either been lived or is uncertain. Small, then, is what each person lives, and small the little corner of the earth where they live; small too is even the longest fame after death, and even that is handed down a line of little human beings who will die very soon and who do not know even themselves, let alone someone who died long ago.

3.11a  To the precepts already named let one more be added: always to make a definition or outline of the thing that comes up in an impression, so as to see it as it is in its substance, bare, whole and entire, and taken apart, and to say to oneself its own name and the names of the things it was put together from and into which it will be broken up. For nothing so produces greatness of mind as being able to test methodically and truly each of the things that come up in life, and always to look into them in such a way as to take in at the same time what kind of use this thing supplies to what kind of universe, what worth it has in relation to the whole, and what in relation to the human being, who is a citizen of the highest city, of which the other cities are like houses.

3.11b  What is it, and what is it compounded of, and how long is it in its nature to last, this thing that is now making the impression on me? And what virtue is needed for it: gentleness, for example, courage, good faith, simplicity, self-sufficiency, and the rest? So in each case one must say: this comes from god; this comes by the shared lot, and the spinning and winding together, and this kind of coincidence and chance; and this comes from one of my own tribe, a kinsman and partner, who does not know, however, what is in accordance with nature for them. But I do know. That is why I treat them according to the natural law of partnership, with good will and justly; and yet at the same time, in things that are neither good nor bad, I aim at what accords with worth.

3.12  If you do the work that is present, following right reason, earnestly, vigorously, with good will, and with nothing as a side business, but keep your own spirit standing pure, as if you had to give it back this very moment; if you join this together, waiting for nothing and fleeing nothing, but content with the present work that is according to nature and with heroic truth in what you say and utter, you will live well. And there is no one who can prevent this.

3.13  As doctors always have their instruments and blades at hand for emergency treatments, so have your principles ready for knowing things divine and human, and for doing everything, even the smallest thing, as one who remembers the bond that ties the two to each other. For you will not do any human thing well without referring it at the same time to the divine, nor the other way round.

3.14  Stop wandering. For you are not going to read either your little notebooks or the deeds of the ancient Romans and Greeks and the extracts from the writings, which you were putting away for yourself for old age. So hurry to the end, and letting go of empty hopes, come to your own rescue, if you care at all about yourself, while it is possible.

3.15–16a  They do not know how many things are meant by “stealing”, by “sowing”, by “buying”, by “being at rest”, by “seeing what must be done”, which is not done with the eyes but with a different kind of sight.

Body, soul, mind. To the body belong sensations, to the soul impulses, to the mind principles.

3.16b  To be stamped with impressions belongs to cattle as well. To be pulled by the strings of impulse belongs also to wild beasts, and to effeminate men, and to Phalaris and Nero. And to have the mind as leader towards what appear to be the fitting things to do belongs also to those who do not believe in gods, and those who desert their country, and those who do what they do once they have shut the doors. If, then, the rest is shared with those just mentioned, what is left as the good person’s own is to love and welcome the things that happen to them and are spun together for them; and not to muddy the spirit seated within the chest, nor disturb it with a crowd of impressions, but to keep it gracious, following god in good order, neither saying anything contrary to what is true nor doing anything contrary to what is just. And if all people disbelieve that the good person lives simply and modestly and cheerfully, the good person is not angry with any of them, and is not turned aside from the road that leads to the end of life, at which one must arrive pure, quiet, ready to be released, fitted to one’s own portion without being forced.



BOOK 4

4.1  The thing that is master within, when it is in accordance with nature, stands towards what happens in such a way that it always shifts easily to what is possible and given. For it is not fond of any assigned material, but directs its impulse towards the things that come first, with a proviso, and what is brought in instead it makes into material for itself, like fire when it masters the things that fall on it, things by which a small lamp would have been put out. But a bright fire very quickly makes what is heaped on it its own, consumes it, and from those very things rises higher.

4.2  Let no act be done at random, or otherwise than according to a rule that goes to make up the art.

4.3  People look for retreats for themselves: country places, seashores, mountains. You too are in the habit of longing very much for such things. But all of this is the mark of someone with no training at all, when you are free, at whatever hour you wish, to retreat into yourself. For nowhere that a person retreats to is quieter or freer from business than their own soul, above all for someone who has such things within that, on bending to look into them, they are at once in complete ease; and by ease I mean nothing other than good order. So give yourself this retreat continually, and renew yourself. And let the things be short and elementary which, as soon as they meet you, will be enough to wash away all distress and to send you back without resentment at the things you are returning to. For what will you resent? The badness of people? Think over the conclusion: that rational animals have come to be for the sake of one another, and that forbearance is a part of justice, and that they go wrong against their will, and how many before now, after feuding, suspecting, hating, fighting with spears, have been laid out and turned to ash; and then stop at last. Or will you also resent what is dealt out from the whole? Renew for yourself the either-or, “either providence or atoms”, and all the grounds on which it was proved that the universe is, as it were, a city. Or will the things of the body still touch you? Consider that the mind does not mix with the breath, whether that moves smoothly or roughly, once the mind has taken itself back and come to know its own power; and, for the rest, all that you have heard and agreed to about pain and pleasure. Or will petty fame distract you? Look at how fast everything is forgotten, and at the gulf of endless time on either side, and the emptiness of the echo, and how changeable and undiscerning those are who seem to give praise, and the narrowness of the place in which that petty fame is confined. For the whole earth is a point, and how small a corner of it is this dwelling place? And here, how many are there, and what sort of people, who will give the praise? For the rest, then, remember the withdrawal into this little country place of your own; and before everything do not be torn about and do not strain, but be free, and look at things as a man, as a human being, as a citizen, as a mortal animal. And among the things readiest to hand, which you will bend to look into, let there be these two. One, that things do not touch the soul but stand outside it, unmoving, and disturbances come only from the judgment within. The other, that all these things you see will change in almost no time and will no longer be; and keep thinking of how many things you have yourself already seen change. The universe is alteration; life is judgment.

4.4  If mind is common to us, then reason too, by which we are rational, is common. If that is so, then the reason that commands what is to be done or not done is also common. If that is so, law too is common. If that is so, we are citizens. If that is so, we share in some civic body. If that is so, the universe is, as it were, a city. For in what other common civic body will anyone say that the whole race of human beings shares? And it is from there, from this common city, that our very mind and reason and sense of law come; or from where else? For just as what is earthy in me has been portioned off from some earth, and what is wet from another element, and what is breath from some source, and what is warm and fiery from some source of its own (for nothing comes from nothing, just as nothing goes away into what is not), so too mind has come from somewhere.

4.5  Death is the same kind of thing as birth, a mystery of nature: a combining out of the same elements, a dissolving into the same. And in short, it is not a thing anyone would be ashamed of: for it is not contrary to what follows in sequence for a living being with mind, nor contrary to the reason on which it is built.

4.6  It is the nature of these things to be done in this way by people of this kind, of necessity; and whoever does not want this wants the fig tree to have no sap. But in general remember this: that within a very short time both you and this man will be dead, and a little later not even your names will be left.

4.7–9  Take away the judgment, and “I have been harmed” is taken away. Take away “I have been harmed”, and the harm is taken away.

What does not make a human being worse than they were does not make their life worse either, and does not harm them either from outside or from within.

The nature of what is beneficial is bound by necessity to do this.

4.10  That “everything that happens, happens justly”. If you watch closely, you will find it so. I do not mean only according to the order in which one thing follows another, but that it is according to what is just, and as if by someone handing out what accords with worth. So keep watching, as you have begun, and whatever you do, do it with this: with being good, in the sense in which the good person is properly understood. Keep this in every action.

4.11  Do not judge things to be such as the person who abuses you decides they are, or wants you to decide they are, but see them as they are in truth.

4.12  One must always have these two kinds of readiness. The first is to do only what the reason of the kingly and lawgiving art suggests, for the benefit of human beings. The second is to change one’s mind, if it turns out that someone is at hand who sets one right and leads one away from some mistaken notion. But the leading away must always come from something persuasive, as being just or of common benefit, and what is preferred must be of that kind only, not because it appeared pleasant or well thought of.

4.13  “Do you have reason?” “I do.” “Then why do you not use it? For when it is doing its own work, what else do you want?”

4.14–15  You came to exist as a part within something. You will vanish into what gave birth to you; or rather, you will be taken back up into its seminal reason, through change.

Many little lumps of incense on the same altar: one fell earlier, another later, but it makes no difference.

4.16  Within ten days you will seem a god to the very people to whom you now seem a beast and an ape, if you turn back to the principles and to reverence for reason.

4.17  Not as though you were going to live ten thousand years. What must come hangs over you. While you live, while it is possible, become good.

4.18  How much leisure a person gains who does not look at what their neighbour said or did or thought, but only at what they themselves are doing, so that this very thing may be just and holy, or in keeping with the good man: not to look round at a dark character, but to run straight along the line, not thrown this way and that.

4.19–20  Whoever is worked up about fame after death does not picture that each of those who remember them will very soon die too; then in turn the one who takes over from that one, until the whole memory also goes out, as it passes on through those that are lit and go out. But suppose even that those who will remember are immortal, and the memory immortal: what then is that to you? And I do not speak of its being nothing to the dead; but to the living, what is praise, except perhaps for the sake of managing some matter? For leave aside now, out of season, the gift of nature, which from here on belongs to some other discussion.

Everything that is beautiful in any way at all is beautiful of itself and ends in itself, and does not have praise as a part of itself. At any rate, what is praised becomes neither worse nor better. I say this also of the things called beautiful in the more ordinary way, such as material things and things made by craft (for what does the truly beautiful need? No more than law does, no more than truth, no more than goodwill or a sense of shame). Which of these is beautiful because it is praised, or is ruined when it is blamed? For does a little emerald become worse than itself if it is not praised? And what of gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a small knife, a little flower, a little tree?

4.21  If souls go on existing, how has the air had space for them from eternity? And how does the earth have space for the bodies of those who have been buried over so long an age? For just as here the change and dissolution of these bodies, after they have gone on existing for a certain time, makes space for other dead, so the souls that pass over into the air, after holding together for a while, change and are poured out and are set alight, being taken back into the generative reason of the whole, and in this way they give space to those that come to settle there after them. That is what a person might answer on the supposition that souls go on existing. But one should consider not only the multitude of bodies buried in this way, but also that of the animals eaten every day by us and by the other animals. For how great a number is consumed and, in a way, buried like this in the bodies of those that feed on them! And yet the space takes them in, through their being turned into blood, through their alteration into what is airy or fiery. What is the way to inquire into the truth in this matter? Division into what is matter and what is cause.

4.22  Not to roam away, but at every impulse to give what is just, and at every impression to keep the sure grasp.

4.23  Universe, everything that fits well with you fits well with me. Nothing that is timely for you is too early or too late for me. Nature, everything your seasons bear is fruit to me. All things from you, all things in you, all things into you. One man says, “Dear city of Cecrops.” But you, will you not say, “Dear city of Zeus”?

4.24  “Do few things,” he says, “if you mean to be cheerful.” Might it not be better to do what is necessary, and whatever the reason of a living being that is civic by nature requires, and in the way it requires? For this brings not only the cheerfulness that comes from doing things well, but also the cheerfulness that comes from doing few things. For most of what we say and do is not necessary, and if a person strips it away they will have more leisure and be less troubled. So on each occasion one must also remind oneself: “Is this perhaps one of the things that are not necessary?” And one must strip away not only the actions that are not necessary but impressions too, for then superfluous actions will not follow after them either.

4.25–26  Try how the life of the good person, too, goes for you: the person who is pleased with what is allotted from the whole and content with their own action being just and their disposition kind.

You have seen those things; look at these too. Do not disturb yourself: make yourself simple. Someone does wrong? They do wrong to themselves. Something has happened to you? That is well: everything that happens was, from the beginning, fated along with you out of the whole and spun into your thread. In sum, life is short. The present must be turned to profit, with good reasoning and justice. Be sober, without strain.

4.27  Either an arranged order, or a stirred brew jumbled together. But in fact an order. Or can a kind of order exist in you, and disorder in the all? And this when all things are, as they are, separated out and spread out, and feel with one another.

4.28–29a  A dark character, a womanish character, a stubborn character, like a wild beast, like cattle, childish, dull, counterfeit, clownish, like a petty trader, tyrannical.

If the one who does not know the things that are in the universe is a stranger to the universe, no less a stranger too is the one who does not know the things that happen.

4.29b  An exile is whoever exiles themselves from the reason that belongs to citizens. Blind is whoever shuts the eye of the mind. A beggar is whoever is in need of another and does not have from themselves everything that is useful for life. An abscess standing apart from the universe is whoever stands apart and separates themselves from the reason of the common nature by being displeased with what happens: for that nature brings this forth, the same one that brought you forth too. A piece split off from the city is whoever splits their own soul off from the soul of rational beings, which is one.

4.30  One person practises philosophy without a tunic, another without a book. Here is another, half naked: “I have no bread,” he says, “and yet I hold to reason.” And I, for my part, do not have the nourishment that comes from learning, and yet I hold to reason.

4.31  Love the little trade you have learned, and rest in it; and go through what is left of your life having entrusted everything that is yours to the gods with your whole soul, and making yourself neither the tyrant nor the slave of any human being.

4.32  Think, for the sake of an example, of the times of Vespasian, and you will see all the same things: people marrying, raising children, falling ill, dying, making war, keeping festivals, trading, farming, flattering, insisting on their own way, suspecting, plotting, praying for certain people to die, grumbling at how things are, falling in love, hoarding treasure, wanting consulships, wanting kingships. Well then, that life of theirs is no longer anywhere. Pass on again to the times of Trajan: again all the same things. That life too is dead. In the same way look over the other inscriptions of times and of whole nations, and see how many people strained with all their strength and a little later fell and were broken up into the elements. But above all one should go back over those whom you yourself knew being dragged after empty things, people who neglected to do what fits their own constitution, to hold on tight to that and to be content with that. And here it is necessary to remember that the attention given to each action also has its own worth and proportion. For in this way you will not give up in frustration, if you do not occupy yourself with the lesser things more than is fitting.

4.33–34  Expressions that were once in ordinary use are now obsolete words. So too the names of those who were once much sung are now, in a way, obsolete words: Camillus, Caeso, Volesus, Dentatus, and little by little Scipio and Cato too, then Augustus too, then Hadrian and Antoninus too. For all things quickly fade and turn into tales, and quickly too complete forgetting buries them. And I say this of those who shone in some wonderful way; for the rest, the moment they have breathed their last, are “unseen, unheard of”. And what is everlasting remembrance at all? All of it empty. What then is there that one should put serious effort into? This one thing: thought that is just, actions that are social, speech such as never to lie, and a disposition that welcomes everything that happens as necessary, as familiar, as flowing from an origin and spring of that kind.

Willingly join in giving yourself over to Clotho, and let her spin you together with whatever events she wishes.

4.35–36  Everything lasts a day, both what remembers and what is remembered.

Observe continually that all things come about by change, and accustom yourself to the thought that the nature of the whole loves nothing so much as to change the things that exist and make new things like them. For everything that exists is, in a way, the seed of what will come from it. But you picture as seeds only those that are cast into earth or womb, and that is very much a layman’s view.

4.37  Any moment now you will be dead, and you are not yet simple, nor untroubled, nor free of the suspicion that you could be harmed from outside, nor gracious towards everyone, nor someone who places wisdom only in acting justly.

4.38  Look closely into their ruling parts, and at the wise as well: what sort of things they run from, and what sort of things they chase.

4.39  What is bad for you does not have its existence in another’s ruling part, nor yet in any turn and alteration of what surrounds you. Where, then? In the place where you have that which judges about bad things. So let that not judge, and all is well. Even if what is nearest to it, the little body, is cut, is burned, festers, rots, still let the part that judges about these things keep quiet. That is, let it decide that nothing is either bad or good which can happen equally to a bad man and a good one. For what happens equally to the one who lives against nature and to the one who lives in accordance with nature is neither in accordance with nature nor against nature.

4.40  To think constantly of the universe as one living thing, having one substance and one soul; and of how all things are handed up to its one perception; and how it does all things by one impulse; and how all things are joint causes of all the things that come about; and what the spinning together and winding together is like.

4.41  You are a little soul carrying a corpse, as Epictetus used to say.

4.42–43  There is nothing bad for things that are in the course of change, just as there is nothing good for things that come to exist out of change.

Eternity is a kind of river of things that come to be, and a violent current. For as soon as each thing has been seen it has been carried past, and another is being carried past, and yet another will be carried along.

4.44–45  Everything that happens is as ordinary and familiar as the rose in spring and fruit in summer. For illness is like that too, and death, and slander, and plotting, and all the things that gladden or grieve fools.

What comes next always follows what went before as kin to it. For it is not like a counting-off of separate items that has only compulsion in it, but a connection that has reason in it. And just as the things that exist have been arranged to fit together, so the things that come to be show not bare succession but a wonderful kinship.

4.46  Always remember the saying of Heraclitus, that the death of earth is to become water, and the death of water is to become air, and of air, fire, and back again. And remember too the one who forgets where the road leads; and that people are at odds with the very thing they are most constantly in company with, the reason that governs the whole; and that the things they run into every day seem foreign to them; and that we must not act and speak like people asleep, for then too we think we are acting and speaking; and that we must not act and speak like children of their parents, that is, simply as we have had it handed down.

4.47  Just as, if one of the gods told you that you will die tomorrow or at any rate the day after, you would no longer count it a great thing that it should be the day after rather than tomorrow, unless you are utterly abject (for how much is the gap between?), so too think it nothing great that it should be many years from now rather than tomorrow.

4.48  Keep in mind continually how many doctors have died, after many times knitting their brows over the sick; how many astrologers, after foretelling other people’s deaths as if that were something great; how many philosophers, after insisting on countless things about death or immortality; how many champions, after killing many; how many tyrants, after using their power over lives with terrible arrogance, as if they were immortal; and how many whole cities have, so to speak, died: Helice and Pompeii and Herculaneum and countless others. Go over as well all those you know, one after another. One buried this person and was then laid out, another buried that one, and all of it in a short time. For the sum of it is this: always see human things as lasting a day and cheap, yesterday a little slime, tomorrow a mummy or ash. So pass through this instant of time in accord with nature and come to rest with good grace, as an olive might fall when it has grown ripe, speaking well of the earth that bore it and grateful to the tree that grew it.

4.49a  To be like the headland against which the waves break without pause; but it stands, and around it the water that had swelled up is put to sleep.

4.49b  “I am unfortunate, because this has happened to me.” No, rather: “I am fortunate, because although this has happened to me I go on undistressed, neither broken by what is present nor afraid of what is coming.” For a thing of this kind could have happened to anyone, but not everyone would have gone on undistressed at it. Why then is that a misfortune rather than this a piece of good fortune? And do you call anything at all a human being’s misfortune that is not a failure of the human being’s nature? And does anything seem to you to be a failure of the human being’s nature that is not against what that nature wills? Well then: you have learned what it wills. Surely this that has happened does not prevent you from being just, great-hearted, self-controlled, sensible, not rash, not deceived, modest, free, and the rest, the things with which, when they are present together, the human being’s nature has in full what is its own? From now on, remember, at everything that leads you towards distress, to use this principle: not “this is a misfortune”, but “to bear it nobly is good fortune.”

4.50  A layman’s help toward contempt of death, but an effective one all the same, is to call back to mind those who clung on and lingered in life. What more do they have, then, than those who died before their time? In any case they lie somewhere or other, Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus, Lepidus or anyone else of that kind, who carried many out to burial and then were carried out themselves. Altogether, the interval is small, and even this, through how many things, in what company and in what a little body it is drained out! So do not treat it as something that matters. For look behind at the yawning expanse of eternity, and ahead at another that has no limit. In this, then, what difference is there between a child three days old and a Nestor of three generations?

4.51  Always run to the short road. And the short road is the one according to nature, so that you say and do everything in the soundest way. For a purpose of this kind frees you from toil and from hanging back, and from all contriving and affectation.



BOOK 5

5.1  At dawn, when you are reluctant to get up, have this ready to hand: “I am getting up to do a human being’s work. Why then am I discontented, if I am on my way to do the things I was born for, the things for whose sake I was brought into the universe? Or was I made for this, to lie under the blankets and keep myself warm?” “But this is more pleasant.” So were you born to feel pleasure? And in general, were you born to be acted on or to act? Do you not see the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders, the bees doing what is their own to do and, for their part, putting the universe together? And then you are not willing to do what belongs to a human being? You do not run to what is according to your nature? “But one must rest too.” I say so too. Nature, though, has given measures for this as well; it has given them for eating and drinking as well; and yet you go beyond the measures, beyond what is enough. But in your actions it is not so: there you stop short of what you can do. For you do not love yourself, since otherwise you would love your nature too, and what it wills. But those who love their own crafts wear themselves away at the works of those crafts, unwashed and unfed. Do you honour your own nature less than the engraver honours engraving, or the dancer dancing, or the money-lover money, or the lover of empty fame their scrap of fame? These people too, when the passion takes them, would sooner advance the things they care about than eat or sleep. But to you do social actions seem cheaper and worth less effort?

5.2  How easy it is to push away and wipe out every impression that is troublesome or out of place, and at once to be in a complete calm.

5.3  Judge yourself worthy of every word and deed that accords with nature, and do not let the blame or the talk that comes afterward from some people pull you away; but if it is a fine thing to have been done or said, do not judge yourself unworthy of it. For those people have a ruling part of their own and act on an impulse of their own. You, for your part, do not look round at those things, but go the straight road right through, following your own nature and the common nature; and the road of these two is one.

5.4  I make my way through the things that accord with nature until I fall and rest, breathing my last into that from which I draw breath every day, and falling on that from which my father gathered the bit of seed, my mother the bit of blood and my nurse the bit of milk; from which every day, for so many years, I have been fed and watered; which bears me as I tread on it and make free use of it for so many purposes.

5.5  They cannot admire your sharpness of mind. So be it. But there are many other things of which you cannot say, “Because I am not made for it by nature.” Offer those, then, which are wholly in your power: being genuine, dignity, bearing hard work, not loving pleasure, not complaining of your lot, needing little, kindness, freedom, plainness, no idle talk, greatness. Do you not see how many things you are already able to offer, for which there is no excuse of want of natural gift or of unfitness, and yet you still stay down below of your own will? Or are you actually forced to grumble, to be stingy, to flatter, to blame your poor body, to try to please, to show off and to be tossed about so much in your soul, because you were built without natural gift? No, by the gods. Rather, you could have been rid of these long ago, and be faulted only, if at all, for being rather slow and finding things rather hard to follow. And this too you must work on, not disregarding it and not taking pleasure in your dullness.

5.6  One kind of person, when they have done someone a good turn, is ready also to charge the favour to that person’s account. Another is not ready to do that, but all the same thinks of the other, privately, as a debtor, and knows what they have done. A third, in a way, does not even know what they have done, but is like a vine that has borne its grapes and looks for nothing more after it has once borne its own fruit. A horse that has run, a dog that has tracked, a bee that has made honey, and a human being who has done good, do not shout about it, but pass on to another thing, as a vine passes on to bearing its grapes again in season. So one must be among these, the ones who in a way do it without being aware of it. “Yes, but this is the very thing one must be aware of. For it is characteristic of the social being,” someone says, “to perceive that it is acting socially and, by Zeus, to want its fellow to perceive it too.” What you say is true, but you take what is being said now in the wrong way. Because of this you will be one of those I mentioned before, for they too are led astray by a certain plausible reasoning. But if you want to understand what it is that is being said, do not be afraid that because of it you will leave undone any social act.

5.7  A prayer of the Athenians: “Rain, rain, dear Zeus, down on the ploughland of the Athenians and the plains.” Either one should not pray, or one should pray like this: simply and like a free person.

5.8  When people say, “Asclepius ordered horse-riding for this person, or cold baths, or going barefoot”, it is the same kind of thing as saying, “The nature of the whole ordered an illness for this person, or a maiming, or a loss, or something else of that kind.” For in the first case “ordered” means something like this: he set this in order for this person as suited to health. And in the second, what falls to each person has in some way been set in order for them as suited to fate. For we say that these things “fall” to us in just the way that builders say the squared stones in walls or in pyramids “fall” into place, fitting with one another in a particular arrangement. For in general there is one fitting-together; and just as the universe is made complete, as the body it is, out of all bodies, so fate is made complete, as the cause it is, out of all causes. Even people with no training at all understand what I mean, for they say, “It brought him this.” So then, this was brought to this person, and this was ordered for this person. Let us accept these things, then, as we accept the things Asclepius orders. After all, many of those too are rough, but we welcome them in the hope of health. Let the carrying out and completion of what seems good to the common nature seem to you the same kind of thing as your own health, and so welcome everything that comes about, even if it seems rather harsh, because it leads you there: to the health of the universe, and to the good progress and success of Zeus. For it would not bring this to anyone if it did not bring good to the whole. For not even any ordinary nature brings anything that is not suited to what it governs. So you should hold dear what falls to you, for two reasons. One is that it came about for you, and was ordered for you, and stood in some relation to you, spun together from far back out of the oldest causes. The other is that what comes to each person individually is also a cause, for the one that governs the whole, of good progress and of completion and, by Zeus, of continuance itself. For the complete whole is maimed if you cut through the connection and continuity at any point at all, of its causes just as of its parts. And you do cut through it, as far as it lies with you, whenever you are discontented, and in a way you destroy it.

5.9–10a  Do not be disgusted, do not give up, do not lose patience, if doing each thing from right principles does not come to you time after time; but when you have been knocked aside, come back again, and be glad if most of what you do is more in keeping with a human being, and love what you are coming back to. And do not come back to philosophy as to a schoolboy’s minder, but as people with sore eyes go to the little sponge and the egg, as another goes to a poultice, as to a medicinal wash. For in this way you will make no show of obeying reason, but will find rest in it. Remember that philosophy wants only what your nature wants; but you were wanting something else, not in accord with nature. For what is more agreeable than these things? For is it not through this that pleasure trips us up? But look whether greatness of soul, freedom, simplicity, fairness of mind, reverence are more agreeable. For what is more agreeable than wisdom itself, when you think of how the power of understanding and knowledge never stumbles and flows smoothly in everything?

Things are, in a way, so veiled that to not a few philosophers, and not ordinary ones, they have seemed altogether impossible to grasp; except that to the Stoics themselves, at least, they seem hard to grasp. And every assent of ours can shift: for where is the person who does not shift? Pass on, then, to the objects themselves: how short-lived they are, how cheap, how they can be in the possession of a catamite, a prostitute or a robber. After that go on to the characters of the people you live with: one can hardly put up with even the most charming of them, not to say that a person can hardly bear even themselves. In such gloom and filth, then, and in so great a flow of substance and of time, of movement and of the things moved, what there is that can be highly valued, or pursued in earnest at all, I cannot even imagine. For on the contrary …

5.10b  One must comfort oneself and wait for the natural dissolution, and not chafe at the delay, but rest in these things alone. One: that nothing will happen to me that is not according to the nature of the whole. The other: that it is in my power to do nothing against my god and spirit, for there is no one who will force me to go against him.

5.11  “Just what, then, am I using my own soul for now?” Ask yourself this at every turn, and examine: “What do I have now in this part, the one they call the ruling part? And whose soul, then, do I have now? Not a small child’s, surely? Not a youth’s? Not a silly woman’s? Not a tyrant’s? Not a farm animal’s? Not a wild beast’s?”

5.12  What kind of things they are that seem good to most people, you can gather from this as well. For if a person had in mind certain things that truly are good, such as wisdom, self-control, justice, courage, then with these in mind first they could not listen to the words “because of the good things”, for it will not fit. But a person who has first in mind the things that appear good to most people will listen, and will readily accept what the comic poet said as a fitting thing to add. So even most people have an impression of the difference. For otherwise this line would not jar and be refused in the one case, while when it is said of wealth and of the pieces of good luck that make for luxury or reputation we accept it as aptly and wittily said. Go on, then, and ask whether one should honour and judge to be good the kind of things which, when they have first been brought to mind, can fittingly be followed by the line that their owner, because of the plenty, “has nowhere to shit”.

5.13  I am made up of what is causal and what is material, and neither of these will perish into what is not, just as neither came to be out of what is not. So every part of me will be assigned, through change, to some part of the universe, and that in turn will change into another part of the universe, and so on to infinity. It was through a change of this kind that I too came to be, and my parents, and, for one who goes back, into another infinity. For nothing prevents one from saying so, even if the universe is governed in finite cycles.

5.14–15  Reason and the art of reasoning are powers that are content with themselves and with the works that are their own. So they set out from their own starting point and travel towards the end set before them, which is why actions of this kind are called katorthōseis, “acts that go straight”, as signifying the straightness of the road.

None of the things that do not fall to a human being, as a human being, should be called a human being’s. They are not requirements of a human being, human nature does not promise them, and they are not perfections of human nature. So the end for a human being does not lie in them either, nor does that which makes the end complete, the good. For if any of these things did fall to a human being, it would not fall to a human being to look down on them and rise up against them, nor would a person who proves to be in no need of them be praiseworthy, nor would a person who stints themselves in any of them be good, if these things really were goods. But as it is, the more of these things, or of others like them, a person bears to take away from themselves, or even bears having one of them taken away, the better that person is.

5.16  Whatever kind of impressions you often form, your thought will be of that kind; for the soul is dyed by its impressions. So dye it with an unbroken run of impressions such as these. For example: where it is possible to live, there it is also possible to live well; but it is possible to live at court; therefore it is also possible to live well at court. And again: each thing is carried toward that for the sake of which it was built; and what it is carried toward, in that lies its goal; and where its goal is, there also is the benefit and the good of each thing; therefore the good of a rational animal is community. For that we were born for community has long since been shown. Or was it not plain that the worse things are for the sake of the better, and the better for the sake of one another? And things with a soul are better than things without one, and rational things better than things with a soul.

5.17–19  To chase after impossible things is madness. And it is impossible that bad people should not do things of this kind.

Nothing happens to anyone that they are not fitted by nature to bear. The same things happen to someone else, and either because they do not know that it has happened, or because they are showing off greatness of mind, they stand firm and remain unhurt. It is a terrible thing, then, that ignorance and the wish to please should be stronger than wisdom.

Things themselves do not touch the soul in any way at all, nor do they have a way in to the soul, nor can they turn or move the soul. But it alone turns and moves itself, and whatever verdicts it thinks itself worthy to pass, it makes the things that stand before it to be of that kind for itself.

5.20  In one respect nothing is more our own than a human being, in so far as we must do good to them and bear with them. But in so far as some of them stand in the way of the works that are our own, a human being becomes for me one of the indifferent things, no less than sun or wind or wild animal. By these an activity may be hindered, but there are no hindrances to impulse and disposition, because of the proviso and the turning round. For the mind turns round every obstacle to its activity and shifts it into what comes first, and what holds back this work becomes a help to the work, and what stands in this road becomes a way forward on the road.

5.21  Of the things in the universe, honour what is supreme: this is what uses all things and directs all things. In the same way, of the things in you too, honour what is supreme: this is what is akin to that other. For in your case too, this is what uses the others, and your life is governed by it.

5.22–25  What is not harmful to the city does not harm the citizen either. At every impression of having been harmed, apply this rule: if the city is not harmed by this, I have not been harmed either. And if the city is harmed, one should not be angry, but should show the one who harms the city what it is that is being overlooked.

Think often of the speed with which the things that are and the things that come to be are carried past and drawn away. For the substance of things is like a river in unbroken flow, the activities are in continual changes, the causes in countless turns, and almost nothing stands still, even what is close at hand. And the boundlessness of the past and of the future is a yawning gulf in which all things vanish. How then is a person not a fool who, among these things, is puffed up, or torn about, or complains bitterly, as though at something that troubled them for a time, and a long one?

Remember the whole substance of things, of which you have the smallest share; and the whole of time, of which a brief, momentary interval has been marked off for you; and fate, of which you are how small a part?

Someone else does me some wrong? That is for them to see to: they have their own disposition, their own activity. I now have what the common nature wants me to have now, and I do what my own nature wants me to do now.

5.26  Let the ruling and governing part of your soul not be turned by the smooth or rough motion in the flesh, and let it not be blended in, but let it draw a line around itself and confine those feelings within the parts of the body. But when they pass up into the mind by the other shared feeling, as in a body that is one, then one should not try to go against the sensation, which is natural; but let the ruling part not add, from itself, the judgment that it is something good or bad.

5.27  “To live with the gods.” And the one who lives with the gods is the one who constantly shows them their own soul, content with what is allotted and doing all that the spirit wants, the spirit that Zeus has given to each person as protector and leader, a fragment of himself. And this spirit is each person’s mind and reason.

5.28a  Surely you are not angry with the person who stinks like a goat? Surely you are not angry with the one whose breath smells? What do you want them to do? The person has that kind of mouth, has that kind of armpits; from such things such a smell must come. “But a human being has reason,” someone says, “and can work out, by stopping to think, where they are at fault.” Good for you! Well then, you have reason too: stir a rational disposition with a rational disposition; show them, remind them. For if they listen, you will cure them, and there is no need of anger.

5.28b–29  Neither a tragic actor nor a prostitute.

The way you have in mind to live once you have gone out, you can live that way here. But if they do not allow it, then go out of life as well, yet as one who is suffering nothing bad. There is smoke, and I leave. Why do you think it anything much? But as long as nothing of that kind leads me out, I stay, free, and no one will prevent me from doing what I want. And I want what accords with the nature of the rational and social living creature.

5.30  The mind of the whole is social. At any rate it has made the worse things for the sake of the better, and has fitted the better to one another. You see how it has ranked things one below another, one beside another, and has given to each according to its worth, and has brought the best things to be of one mind with one another.

5.31  How have you behaved up to now toward the gods, your parents, your brothers, your wife, your children, your teachers, those who brought you up, your friends, those close to you, your household slaves? Does this hold of you up to now, toward all of them: “to have done nothing wrong to anyone, nor said anything wrong”? Call to mind, too, what things you have passed through and what things you have been strong enough to endure; and that the story of your life is now full and your service complete; and how many fine things you have seen; and how many pleasures and pains you have looked down on, how many honours you have looked past, and to how many inconsiderate people you have been considerate.

5.32  Why do unskilled and ignorant souls throw into confusion one that has skill and understanding? Which soul, then, has skill and understanding? The one that knows beginning and end, and the reason that runs through the whole of substance and, through all of time, manages the sum of things in appointed periods.

5.33  Any moment now, ash or a skeleton, and either a name or not even a name; and the name is a noise and an echo. The things highly prized in life are empty and rotten and small, little dogs biting one another, quarrelsome children laughing and then at once crying. But good faith, a sense of shame, justice and truth: “to Olympus from the earth of the wide ways.” What is there, then, that still holds one here, seeing that the things the senses perceive change easily and do not stand still, the organs of sense are dim and easily take false imprints, the little soul itself is a vapor rising from blood, and a good name among such people is empty? What then? You wait cheerfully for what is either an extinguishing or a move elsewhere. And until the right time for that is at hand, what is enough? What else but to revere the gods and speak well of them, to do good to human beings, to bear with them and to hold back; and, as for everything inside the limits of the bit of flesh and the little breath, to remember that it is neither yours nor up to you.

5.34  You can always have your life flow well, if indeed you can also travel a good road, if indeed you can also judge and act step by step, as along a road. These two things are common to the soul of god, and of a human being, and of every rational living being: not to be hindered by anything else; and to have its good in a just disposition and just action, and to have its desire stop there.

5.35–36a  If this is neither badness of mine nor an activity that comes from badness of mine, and the community is not harmed, why am I upset over it? And what harm is there to the community?

Do not be swept off wholesale by the impression, but help as far as you are able and as far as is deserved, even if it is in the middle things that they are coming off worse; do not, however, form the impression that it is a harm, for that is a bad habit. But as the old man, on leaving, asked for his foster-child’s top, remembering that it was a top, so too in this case. Otherwise you become someone calling out on the Rostra. Man, have you forgotten what these things were? “Yes, but they are much sought after by these people.” And is that a reason for you to become a fool as well? I once was,

5.36b  … wherever caught, a person whose lot is good. And “whose lot is good” means having assigned yourself a good lot; and good lots are good turnings of the soul, good impulses, good actions.



BOOK 6

6.1  The substance of the whole is obedient and ready, and the reason that governs it has in itself no cause for doing anything bad. For it has no badness, and it treats nothing badly, and nothing is harmed by it. And all things come about and are carried through in accordance with it.

6.2  Let it make no difference to you whether you do what is fitting shivering or warm, and whether drowsy or having slept enough, and whether spoken ill of or praised, and whether dying or doing some other kind of act. For this too is one of the acts of life, the one in which we die. So in this too it is enough to manage well what is at hand.

6.3  Look inside. With any thing, do not let either its own quality or its worth run past you.

6.4–5  All existing things will very quickly change, and either they will go up in vapour, if substance is indeed a unity, or they will be scattered.

The reason that governs knows in what condition it acts, what it does, and on what material.

6.6  The best way to fight back is to not become like them.

6.7  Take pleasure in one thing and rest in it: going from one social act to another social act, with god in mind.

6.8  The ruling part is what wakes itself and turns itself; it makes itself whatever it wants to be, and it makes everything that happens appear to itself the way it itself wants.

6.9–10  Each thing is carried through in accordance with the nature of the whole. For it is certainly not in accordance with any other nature, whether one that surrounds from outside, or one contained within, or one that stands apart outside.

Either a stirred brew and a mutual entanglement and a scattering, or unity and order and providence. Now if it is the first, why do I even want to spend my time in a random compound and a jumble of that kind? And why do I even care about anything other than how, one way or another, to “become earth”? And why am I even troubled? For the scattering will come upon me, whatever I do. But if it is the second, I feel reverence, I stand firm, and I take heart, relying on the one that governs.

6.11  When circumstances force you to be, as it were, thrown into confusion, go back quickly into yourself, and do not step out of the rhythm more than you are forced to. For you will have a firmer hold on the harmony by continually going back to it.

6.12  If you had a stepmother and a mother at the same time, you would wait on the stepmother, and still you would be continually going back to your mother. That is what the court and philosophy are to you now. Go back here often and rest in philosophy, because of which the things there seem bearable to you, and you seem bearable among them.

6.13a  Just as, over cooked dishes and other such things to eat, one takes the impression that this is the dead body of a fish, and this the dead body of a bird or a pig; and again, that Falernian wine is a little juice from a little bunch of grapes, and the purple-bordered robe is little hairs of a little sheep soaked in a little blood from a shellfish; and, over what belongs to sex, that it is the rubbing of a bit of gut and, with a kind of spasm, the discharge of a little slime: just as these impressions reach the things themselves and go right through them, so that one sees what they really are, so one must do through the whole of life, and where things give the impression of being very trustworthy, strip them bare, see their cheapness plainly, and strip off the story on which they pride themselves. For conceit is a formidable cheat, and it is when you think you are most occupied with serious things that you are most bewitched.

6.13b  See, at any rate, what Crates says about Xenocrates himself.

6.14  Most of the things the crowd admires come down to the most general kind, the things held together by cohesion or by natural growth: stones, timber, fig trees, vines, olive trees. The things admired by people of a slightly better sort come down to the things held together by soul, such as flocks and herds. The things admired by people more refined still come down to the things held together by rational soul; not, however, rational soul as such, but in so far as it is skilled in a craft or adept in some other way, or merely in terms of owning a great number of slaves. But whoever honours rational soul as such, and as civic, no longer pays attention to any of the other things, but before everything else preserves their own soul rational and social in its condition and its movement, and works together with whoever is of the same kind toward this.

6.15  Some things are hurrying to come to be, others are hurrying to have come to be, and of what is coming to be some part has already been quenched. Flows and alterations renew the universe continually, just as the unbroken movement of time keeps boundless eternity always new. In this river, then, which of these things running past would anyone prize, a thing it is not possible to stand on? It is as if someone began to love one of the little sparrows flying past, and it has already gone out of sight. Each person’s life itself, too, is something of the kind: like the vapour that rises from blood and the breath drawn in from the air. For drawing in the air once and giving it back, which we do every moment, is the same kind of thing as giving back the whole power of breathing, which you acquired yesterday and the day before when you were born, to the place from which you first drew it.

6.16  It is not breathing through the surface as plants do that is of value, nor breathing as cattle and wild beasts do, nor being stamped by an impression, nor being pulled on strings by impulse, nor herding together, nor taking in food; for this last is like passing out the waste from the food. What then is of value? Being clapped? No. Then not being clapped by tongues either; for the praises of the many are a clapping of tongues. So you have let petty fame go as well. What is left that is of value? I think it is this: to move and to hold back in keeping with the way one is oneself made, which is also where tending and the crafts lead. For every craft aims at this, that the thing made should be fit for the work it was made for. The planter and the one who tends the vine, the breaker of colts and the one who tends the dog are after this. And the upbringing of children, and teaching: what are they pressing towards? Here, then, is what is of value. And if this is in a good state, you will not be acquiring any of the other things for yourself. Will you not stop valuing many other things as well? Then you will be neither free nor sufficient to yourself nor without passion. For you are bound to envy, to be jealous, to look with suspicion on those who can take those things away, to plot against those who have what you value. In short, anyone who is in need of any of those things is bound to be in confusion, and what is more to find much fault even with the gods. But respect for your own mind, and the value you set on it, will make you pleasing to yourself, well fitted to your fellows and in tune with the gods, that is, praising all that they deal out and have arranged.

6.17  Up, down, round in a circle: such are the motions of the elements. The movement of virtue, though, is in none of these, but is something more divine, and going forward by a road hard to make out it travels well.

6.18  What a thing it is that they do. The people who live at the same time as them and alongside them they are unwilling to speak well of, yet they set great store by being spoken well of themselves by those who come after, whom they have never seen and never will see. This is close to what it would be to be pained that those who came before did not also speak well of you.

6.19  Do not, if something is hard for you yourself to carry out, judge it impossible for a human being; but if something is possible for a human being and proper to them, consider it within your own reach too.

6.20  In the gymnasium someone has scratched us with their nails, and butted us with their head and landed a blow; but we do not mark them down, nor take offence, nor suspect them afterwards of plotting against us. We are on our guard, certainly, yet not as against an enemy, nor with suspicion, but with a kindly stepping aside. Let something like this happen in the other parts of life too: let us overlook many things in those who are, as it were, our training partners. For it is possible, as I said, to step aside, and neither to suspect nor to hate.

6.21  If anyone can refute me and make it plain to me that I am not judging or acting rightly, I will gladly change. For I am looking for the truth, by which no one was ever harmed; the one who is harmed is the one who persists in their own delusion and ignorance.

6.22–23  I do my own duty; the other things do not distract me. For they are either without life, or without reason, or have gone astray and do not know the road.

Animals without reason, and in general things and objects, treat with a large mind and freely, as one who has reason treats what has none. Human beings, since they have reason, treat socially. And in everything call on the gods. And do not trouble yourself about how long you will be doing this: for even three hours of this kind are enough.

6.24  Alexander of Macedon and his mule driver, once dead, came to the same state: for either they were taken back into the same seed-reasons of the universe, or they were scattered alike into atoms.

6.25  Consider how many things come about at once in each of us in the same instant of time, things of the body and things of the soul together. And then you will not be surprised if many more things, or rather all the things that come about, exist at once within the one and entire thing, the very thing we call the universe.

6.26  If someone sets you the question how the name Antoninus is written, surely you will not strain as you bring out each of its letters? What then if they get angry: surely you will not get angry back? Will you not gently go on counting off each of the letters? So here too, remember that every duty is made complete out of certain counted parts. These you must keep to, and, without being thrown into confusion and without answering those who are annoyed with annoyance of your own, carry through step by step what lies before you.

6.27  How cruel it is not to let people go after the things that appear to them to suit them and to be good for them. And yet in a way you do not allow them to do this, whenever you are indignant that they do wrong. For they are certainly being carried toward things as suiting them and good for them. “But it is not so.” Then teach them and show them, without being indignant.

6.28  Death is a rest from the impacts on the senses, from the tugging of impulse on the puppet strings, from the step-by-step course of thought, and from service to the flesh.

6.29–30a  It is shameful that, in a life in which your body does not give out, your soul should give out first.

Take care that you are not turned into a Caesar, that you are not dyed: for it happens. So keep yourself simple, good, unspoiled, dignified, unpretentious, a friend of what is just, reverent toward the gods, kindly, warmly affectionate, strong for the work that is fitting. Struggle to remain the kind of person philosophy wanted to make you. Stand in awe of the gods, keep people safe. Life is short. There is one fruit of living on earth: a pious disposition and social actions.

6.30b  Everything as a pupil of Antoninus: his vigour on behalf of what is done according to reason, his evenness everywhere, his piety, the calm of his face, his gentleness, his freedom from vanity, and his ambition to grasp matters; and how he would not let anything at all go by without first looking into it very well and understanding it clearly; and how he bore with those who blamed him unjustly, without blaming them in return; and how he hurried toward nothing; and how he did not accept slanders; and how exact an examiner he was of characters and actions, and not one to reproach, not one to jump at a noise, not suspicious, not a sophist; and how he was content with little, for example in housing, bedding, clothing, food and service; and how hard-working he was and how patient; and that he was the kind of man who could stay at the same thing until evening, not even needing, because of his plain diet, to pass his waste except at the usual hour; and his being firm and always the same in his friendships; and his putting up with those who openly opposed his opinions, and being glad if anyone showed him something better; and how he was reverent toward the gods without superstition; so that your last hour may come upon you with a conscience as good as his.

6.31  Sober up and call yourself back. And when you have woken up again and understood that it was dreams that were troubling you, then, awake again, look at these things as you looked at those.

6.32  I am made of a little body and a soul. Now to the little body all things are indifferent, for it cannot tell a difference. To the mind, whatever is not its own activity is indifferent; and whatever is its own activity, all of that is in its power. Even of these, though, it is concerned only with the present, for its future and past activities are themselves already indifferent as well.

6.33–34  Pain is not against nature for the hand or for the foot, so long as the foot is doing the foot’s work and the hand the hand’s. So too for a human being, as a human being, pain is not against nature, so long as they are doing a human being’s work. And if it is not against nature for them, it is not bad for them either.

What great pleasures robbers, catamites, father-beaters, tyrants have enjoyed!

6.35  Do you not see how craftsmen in the manual trades adapt themselves to laymen up to a point, but all the same hold on to the reason of their craft and will not agree to abandon it? Is it not shocking if the master builder and the doctor are to have more respect for the reason of their own craft than a human being has for their own reason, which a human being shares with the gods?

6.36  Asia, Europe: corners of the universe. Every sea: a drop of the universe. Athos: a small clod of the universe. All of present time: a point of eternity. All things are small, easily changed, vanishing.

All things come from there: they have set out from that common ruling part, or they come as a consequence. So the gaping jaws of the lion too, and poison, and every kind of harm, like a thorn, like mud, are by-products of those revered and beautiful things. So do not picture them as foreign to what you revere, but consider the source of all things.

6.37–38  Whoever has seen what is now has seen everything: all that has come to be from the everlasting past and all that will be without end. For all things are of the same stock and of the same kind.

Reflect often on the linking together of all things in the universe and their relation to one another. For in a way all things are interwoven with one another, and all are on that account dear to one another. For this thing is next in order to another, through the movement of tension, the breathing together and the unity of substance.

6.39–40  Fit yourself to the things your lot has joined you to, and love the people your lot has put you among, but truly.

Every instrument, tool or utensil is as it should be if it does what it was made for; and yet in that case the one who made it is away. But in things held together by nature, the power that made them is inside and stays there. For that reason you should respect it all the more, and consider that if you keep to its will and live by it, everything is as your mind would have it. And in the same way, for the All too, what is its own is as its mind would have it.

6.41  Whatever you set up for yourself as good or bad among the things that are not yours to choose, it must follow that when you meet with such a bad thing or miss such a good one, you blame the gods, and hate people too: those who are the cause, or are suspected of being about to be the cause, of your missing the one or meeting with the other. And indeed we do many wrongs because of the difference these things make to us. But if we judge only the things that are up to us to be good and bad, no cause is left either for accusing a god or for taking an enemy’s stand against a person.

6.42  We are all working together towards one finished result, some of us knowingly and with awareness, others without attending to it, just as Heraclitus, I think, says that even those who are asleep are workers and fellow workers in the things that happen in the universe. One works together in one way, another in another, and, into the bargain, so does even the one who finds fault and the one who tries to stand against the things that happen and do away with them, for the universe had need of such a person too. What remains, then, is for you to understand among which of them you are placing yourself. For the one who governs the whole will in any case use you well, and will take you in as some part of the fellow workers and of those who help the work along. But you, do not become the kind of part that the cheap and ridiculous line is in the play, the one Chrysippus mentions.

6.43  Surely the sun does not think it should do the rain’s part? Or Asclepius the part of the fruit-bearing goddess? And what of each of the stars? Are they not different, yet working together toward the same thing?

6.44  If, then, the gods deliberated about me and about the things that had to happen to me, they deliberated well. For a god without deliberation is not easy even to imagine; and for what cause would they have had an impulse to do me harm? For what would they, or the community, which is what they take most thought for, have gained from it? But if they did not deliberate about me individually, they certainly did deliberate about the affairs of the community, at least, and these things too, which happen as a consequence of those, I ought to welcome and hold dear. But if in fact they deliberate about nothing (to believe that is not pious; or else let us not sacrifice, or pray, or swear oaths, or do the other things that we do at every turn towards the gods as present and living with us), if in fact they deliberate about nothing that concerns us, then I am free to deliberate about myself, and my inquiry is into what is of benefit. What benefits each is what accords with its own make-up and nature, and my nature is rational and civic. My city and fatherland, as Antoninus, is Rome; as a human being, the universe. So the things that are useful to these cities are the only things that are good for me.

6.45  Whatever happens to each is good for the whole. That would be enough. But if you watch you will also see this, as a general rule: whatever happens to one person is good for other people too. Here, though, let “good for” be taken in the more ordinary sense, as applied to things that are neither good nor bad.

6.46  Just as what goes on in the amphitheatre and places of that kind repels you, since the same things are always being seen, and the sameness makes the spectacle cloying, feel the same about the whole of life too. For all things, up and down, are the same and from the same. How long, then?

6.47  Keep in mind continually that people of every kind, of every kind of occupation and from every sort of nation, are dead, so that this thought comes down as far as Philistion and Phoebus and Origanion. Now pass on to the other classes. That is the place we must move to, where there are so many formidable orators, so many grave philosophers, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, Socrates, so many heroes earlier, so many generals and tyrants later; and besides these Eudoxus, Hipparchus, Archimedes, other natures that were sharp, great-minded, hard-working, unscrupulous, self-willed, mockers of human life itself, perishable and lasting a day, such as Menippus and all of his kind. About all of these, keep in mind that they have long been lying dead. What, then, is terrible in this for them? And what for those who are not even named at all? One thing here is worth a great deal: to live life through with truth and justice, kindly towards the liars and the unjust.

6.48  When you want to cheer yourself, think of the strong points of the people you live with: the energy of one, for example, the modesty of another, the generosity of a third, and something else in someone else. For nothing cheers so much as the likenesses of the virtues showing in the characters of the people you live with, and coming together, as many at once as possible. And so they must be kept ready to hand.

6.49  You are not upset, are you, that you weigh so many pounds and not three hundred? So too, then, with having to live to so many years and no more. For just as you are content with the amount of matter that has been marked off for you, so too with time.

6.50  Try to persuade them, but act even against their will whenever the reasoning of justice leads that way. If, however, someone uses force and stands in the way, change over to being content and free of distress, and use the hindrance for another virtue; and remember that your impulse was with a reservation, and that you were not desiring what is impossible. What were you desiring, then? An impulse of some such kind. And this you attain: the things we were led forward for, these come about.

6.51  The lover of fame takes their own good to be another person’s activity; the lover of pleasure, their own feeling; the person who has sense, their own action.

6.52  One is free to form no judgment about this and not to be troubled in one’s soul; for things themselves do not have a nature that produces our verdicts.

6.53  Accustom yourself to be with what another person is saying, without inattention, and, as far as possible, be in the soul of the one who speaks.

6.54  What is not good for the hive is not good for the bee either.

6.55  If the sailors were speaking ill of me while I was steering, or the sick while I was their doctor, would I be attending to anything other than how I myself might bring about what keeps those on board safe, or what makes those under treatment healthy?

6.56  How many of those with whom I came into the world have already gone away.

6.57  To people with jaundice honey appears bitter, and to those bitten by a mad dog water appears frightening, and to small children their little ball appears beautiful. Why, then, am I angry? Or do you think that a false belief has less power than the bile has over the person with jaundice and the poison over the one bitten by a mad dog?

6.58  No one will prevent you from living according to the reason of your own nature. Nothing will happen to you contrary to the reason of the common nature.

6.59  What sort of people those are whom they want to please, and for what sort of gains, and through what sort of activities. How quickly eternity will cover everything, and how much it has covered already.



BOOK 7

7.1  What is wickedness? This is what you have often seen. And with everything that happens, keep this at hand: this is what you have often seen. In general, high and low, you will find the same things. The histories are full of them, the ancient ones, those in between and the recent ones; cities and households are full of them now. Nothing is new. Everything is both familiar and short-lived.

7.2a  How can the principles be killed, unless the impressions that answer to them are put out, the impressions it is up to you to keep fanning back into flame? I am able to form the judgment I ought about this. If I am able, why am I disturbed? The things outside my mind are nothing at all to my mind. Learn this and you are upright.

7.2b–3  You can come back to life. See things again as you used to see them: for in this lies the coming back to life.

The empty seriousness of a procession, plays on a stage, flocks, herds, spear fights, a little bone thrown to little dogs, a scrap of bread into the fish tanks, the toils and load-carrying of ants, the scurrying of frightened little mice, puppets pulled on strings. Among these things, then, one must stand kindly and without snorting at them, and yet keep in view that each person is worth just as much as the things they have taken seriously are worth.

7.4  One must follow what is said word by word, and what is done impulse by impulse; and in the latter case see at once what aim it is directed at, and in the former watch closely for what is meant.

7.5–7  Is my mind enough for this or not? If it is enough, I use it for the work as a tool given by the nature of the whole. If it is not enough, either I step back from the work in favour of someone who can carry it out better, unless for some other reason it is my duty, or I do it as best I can, taking on to help me someone who, by making use of my ruling part as well, can do what is timely and useful for the community now. For whatever I do, by myself or with another, must be directed to this alone: what is useful to the community and fits well with it.

How many who were much sung of have already been handed over to forgetting, and how many who sang of them are long since out of the way.

Do not be ashamed of being helped. For what is set before you is to do what falls to you, like a soldier at the storming of a wall. What then, if you are lame and cannot climb up onto the battlement alone, but with another it is possible?

7.8–9  Do not let what is to come trouble you. For you will come to it, if you have to, carrying the same reason that you now use on what is present.

All things are interwoven with one another, and the bond is sacred, and hardly anything is foreign to anything else. For they have been arranged together, and together they give order to the same universe. For there is one universe, made of all things, and one god, through all things, and one substance, and one law, the reason common to all living beings that have mind, and one truth, if indeed there is also one perfection for living beings that are of the same kind and share in the same reason.

7.10  Everything material very quickly vanishes into the substance of the whole, and every cause is very quickly taken back up into the reason of the whole, and the memory of everything is very quickly buried in eternity.

7.11  For the rational animal, the same action is in accordance with nature and in accordance with reason.

7.12  Upright, not held upright.

7.13  The relation that the limbs of the body have in things that are united is the relation that rational beings have in things that stand apart, being built to do some one work together. The thought of this will strike you more if you often say to yourself: “I am a limb [melos] of the whole made up of rational beings.” But if, by using the letter rho, you say that you are a part [meros], you do not yet love people from the heart; doing good does not yet delight you fully; you still do it as no more than the proper thing, not yet as doing good to yourself.

7.14  Let whatever likes fall from outside on the things that are able to be affected by this falling. For those things, once affected, will complain if they like. But I, unless I judge that what has happened is bad, have not yet been harmed. And it is possible for me not to judge so.

7.15  Whatever anyone does or says, I must be good; just as if gold or emerald or purple were always saying this: “Whatever anyone does or says, I must be emerald and have my own color.”

7.16  The ruling part does not make trouble for itself; I mean, for example, it does not frighten itself into desire. But if anyone else can frighten or distress it, let them do so; for it will not, by its own judging, turn itself in such directions. Let the little body itself take care that it suffers nothing, if it can, and let it say so if it suffers anything. But the little soul, the thing that fears, that is distressed, that in general forms judgments about these things, will suffer nothing at all; for you will not lead it to such a verdict. The ruling part, so far as depends on itself, is in need of nothing, unless it makes a need for itself; and in the same way it is undisturbed and unhindered, unless it disturbs and hinders itself.

7.17  Happiness is a good spirit, or a good ruling part. So what are you doing here, impression? Go away, by the gods, as you came: for I do not need you. But you have come out of old habit. I am not angry with you. Only go away.

7.18  Is someone afraid of change? Why, what can come about without change? And what is dearer to the nature of the whole, or more its own? Can you yourself take a bath unless the firewood changes? Can you be fed unless the food changes? Can anything else that is useful be accomplished without change? Do you not see, then, that your own changing too is the same kind of thing, and in the same way necessary to the nature of the whole?

7.19–22  All bodies pass through and out of the substance of the whole, as through a winter torrent, grown together with the whole and working with it, as our limbs do with one another. How many a Chrysippus, how many a Socrates, how many an Epictetus has eternity already swallowed down. Let the same thought strike you about any human being whatever and any thing.

Only one thing troubles me: that I myself might do something that the constitution of a human being does not want, or in a way it does not want, or that it does not want now.

Your forgetting of all is near, and near is the forgetting of you by all.

It is proper to a human being to love even those who stumble. This happens if it strikes you at the same moment that they are your kin, that they go wrong through ignorance and against their will, that in a little while both of you will be dead, and above all that the person has not harmed you, for that person has not made your ruling part worse than it was before.

7.23  The nature of the whole, out of the whole of substance as if out of wax, at one moment shaped a little horse; then it melted that down and used its material for a little tree, then for a little human, then for something else. Each of these existed for the briefest time. But there is nothing terrible for a little box in being taken apart, just as there is nothing good in its being put together.

7.24–25  A scowl on the face is very much against nature. When it is often there, the look of the face dies in it, or in the end has been put out, so that it cannot be kindled at all. Try to grasp at least this very point, that it is against reason. For if even the awareness of doing wrong is going to go, what cause is there still for living?

Everything you see, the nature that governs the whole will change in almost no time, and will make other things out of their substance, and again others out of the substance of those, so that the universe may always be young.

7.26  Whenever someone does you some wrong, consider at once what they judged to be good or bad when they did the wrong. For once you have seen this you will pity them, and will be neither surprised nor angry. For either you yourself still judge the same thing to be good as they do, or another thing of the same kind; so you must forgive. But if you no longer judge such things to be good and bad, you will more easily be kind to one who fails to see.

7.27–28  Not to think of absent things as already there, but to reckon up the best of the things that are present, and on their account to remind yourself how they would be sought after if they were not present. At the same time, though, be on your guard that through being so glad of them you do not accustom yourself to prize them so much that, if ever they are not present, you will be troubled.

Draw in upon yourself. It is the nature of the rational ruling part to be content with itself when it acts justly and by that very fact has calm.

7.29–31a  Wipe out the impression. Stop the pulling of the puppet strings. Draw a line around the present time. Recognise what is happening, whether to you or to another. Take the thing before you apart and divide it into what causes and what is matter. Think of the last hour. The wrong that person did, leave it there, where the wrong came to be.

To stretch thinking out alongside what is being said. To have the mind get inside the things that come about and the things that act.

Brighten yourself with simplicity and modesty and with indifference towards what lies between virtue and vice. Love the human race. Follow god.

7.31b–34  He says, “All things are by convention, and in reality only the elements.” But it is enough to remember that all things are by convention. Already very few.

On death: either a scattering, if there are atoms; or, if there is a unity, either a snuffing out or a move elsewhere.

On pain: what cannot be borne carries one off, and what lasts can be borne. And the mind keeps its own calm by separation, and the ruling part has not become worse. As for the parts that are harmed by the pain, let them give their verdict on it, if they can.

On fame: look at their minds, what they are like, and what kind of things they run from and what kind of things they chase. And see that, as heaps of sand piled one on another hide the earlier ones, so in life the earlier things are very quickly covered by those piled on after.

7.35  “‘So, a mind that has greatness and a view of all time and of all being: do you think human life seems anything great to the one who has it?’ ‘Impossible,’ he said. ‘Then such a person will count death, too, as nothing terrible?’ ‘Not in the least.’”

7.36–37  “It is kingly to act well and yet be spoken ill of.”

It is shameful that the face should be obedient, and be shaped and put in order as the mind commands, while the mind itself is not shaped and put in order by itself.

7.38–43  “For one must not be angry with things, for they do not care at all.”

“May you give joys to the immortal gods and to us.”

“To reap life like a ripe ear of grain, and for one to be and another not.”

“But if I and my two children have been neglected by the gods, this too has its reason.”

“For what is right is with me, and what is just.”

No joining in the wailing, no racing pulse.

7.44–48  “But I would have a just answer to give this man: ‘What you say is not right, fellow, if you think that a man who is of even a little use must take into account the risk of living or dying, rather than look at this alone whenever he acts: whether he is doing what is just or unjust, and the deeds of a good man or of a bad one.’”

“For this is how it truly is, men of Athens. Wherever a person has taken up a post, judging it to be best, or has been posted by a commander, there, as it seems to me, they must stay and run the risk, taking nothing into account, neither death nor anything else, ahead of disgrace.”

“But, my dear man, see whether what is noble and what is good may not be something other than saving and being saved. For perhaps this, living for whatever length of time, is what one who is truly a man must let go, and he must not cling to life, but, leaving these matters to god and trusting the women who say that not even one person could escape what is fated, must consider what comes next: in what way he might live the time he is going to live as well as possible.”

Look round at the courses of the stars as if running round with them, and think continually of the changes of the elements into one another. For the impressions of these things wash away the dirt of life on the ground.

A fine saying of Plato’s. And indeed whoever is talking about human beings must also look down at the things on earth as if from somewhere above: herds, armies, farms, marriages, divorces, births, deaths, the din of law courts, empty lands, the varied peoples of the barbarians, festivals, laments, markets, the mixture of every kind and the order put together out of opposites.

7.49–51  Look back over what has gone before: so many changes of ruling powers. It is possible to foresee what will be as well. For it will certainly be of the same kind, and it cannot step out of the rhythm of what is happening now. So to observe human life for forty years is equal to observing it for ten thousand. For what more will you see?

And: “What grew from earth goes back to earth, and what sprouted from a seed of the upper air, back to the vault of heaven.” Or else this is a dissolution of the interlockings among the atoms, and some such scattering of elements that cannot be affected.

And: “Turning the channel aside with food and drink and spells, so as not to die.” … “But the sailing wind that blows from the gods must be borne, in toils without lament.”

7.52  Better at throwing an opponent, but not more social, nor more modest, nor better disciplined in the face of what happens, nor kinder toward the oversights of those nearby.

7.53–54  Where a piece of work can be completed in accordance with the reason that gods and human beings have in common, there is nothing to fear there. For where it is possible to gain benefit through an activity that travels a good road and goes forward in accordance with the way we are made, no harm is to be suspected there.

Everywhere and without a break it is in your power to be well content, in reverence for the gods, with the present turn of events, to behave justly towards the people present, and to work with a craftsman’s care on the present impression, so that nothing slips in that has not been grasped.

7.55  Do not look around at other people’s ruling parts, but look straight ahead to where nature is leading you, both the nature of the whole, through the things that happen to you, and your own nature, through the things you have to do. What each being has to do is what follows from its make-up. Now everything else has been made for the sake of the rational beings, just as in every other case the inferior things are for the sake of the superior, and the rational beings have been made for the sake of one another. So what comes first in the human make-up is being social. Second is not giving way to what the body undergoes; for it is proper to movement that is rational and of mind to draw a boundary around itself and never to be beaten by the movement of the senses or the movement of impulse. For both of those are of the animal kind, while the movement of mind wants to hold first place and not to be overpowered by them. And rightly so, for it is by nature fitted to make use of all of those. Third in the rational make-up is not being hasty and not being deceived. So let the ruling part hold to these and carry its straight road through, and it has what is its own.

7.56–59  As someone who has died and whose life has been lived up to now, one must live what is left, as a surplus, according to nature.

To love only what happens to oneself and is spun into one’s own thread. For what fits better?

At each thing that happens, to keep before one’s eyes the people to whom the same things happened, and who then were vexed, found it strange, complained. So where are they now? Nowhere. Well then, do you too want to be like them? Do you not rather want to leave the turns that belong to others to those who turn and are turned, and yourself be wholly taken up with how to use the things that happen? For you will use them well, and they will be material for you. Only pay attention, and want to be honorable in your own eyes in everything you do; and remember both things, for what the action is done on also makes a difference.

Dig within. Within is the spring of the good, and it can always well up, if you always dig.

7.60  The body too must be firmly set, and not thrown about, either in movement or in posture. For what the mind provides in the face, keeping it composed and graceful, something of that kind must be required in the whole body as well. But all this must be kept up without affectation.

7.61  The art of living is more like the art of wrestling than the art of dancing, in that it stands ready for the things that fall on it and are not foreseen, and does not fall.

7.62–63  Keep turning your attention to who these people are whose testimony in your favour you want, and what ruling parts they have. For you will neither blame those who stumble against their will nor need their testimony, if you look into the springs of their judgment and impulse.

“Every soul,” he says, “is deprived of truth against its will.” So too, then, of justice and self-control and kindness and everything of that kind. It is most necessary to remember this continually, for you will be gentler toward everyone.

7.64  At every pain, have this ready to hand: that it is not shameful and does not make the mind at the helm any worse, for it does not ruin it either in so far as it is rational or in so far as it is social. With most pains, though, let Epicurus’s saying help you as well: that it is neither unbearable nor everlasting, if you remember the limits and do not add to it by opinion. And remember this too: that many things that are the same as pain go unnoticed as such, though we resent them, such as being drowsy, being overheated and having no appetite. So when you are displeased at any of these, say to yourself: “You are giving in to pain.”

7.65  See that you never come to feel toward the inhuman anything like what the inhuman feel toward human beings.

7.66–67a  How do we know that Telauges was not better than Socrates in his disposition? For it is not enough that Socrates died a more glorious death, and argued more nimbly with the sophists, and showed more endurance in spending the night out in the frost, and, when ordered to bring in the man from Salamis, was thought to have stood against it more nobly, and “swaggered in the streets”, a point one might well take note of most of all, if indeed it was true. But what has to be looked at is this: what kind of soul Socrates had, and whether he could find it enough to be just in his dealings with human beings and devout in his dealings with the gods, neither getting idly angry at vice nor yet being a slave to anyone’s ignorance, neither receiving anything allotted from the whole as something foreign or enduring it as something unbearable, nor handing his mind over to feel with the feelings of the poor flesh.

Nature did not blend the mind with the compound in such a way that it is not allowed to draw a boundary round itself and to bring what is its own under its own control.

9.1  Whoever does injustice is impious. For since the nature of the whole has made rational animals for the sake of one another, to help one another as each deserves and in no way to harm one another, whoever goes against its will is clearly impious towards the eldest of the gods. And whoever says what is false is also impious, towards the same goddess. For the nature of the whole is the nature of the things that are, and the things that are stand in close kinship to all that is the case. Further, this nature is also named Truth, and it is the first cause of all things that are true. So whoever says what is false willingly is impious in so far as they do injustice by deceiving; and whoever does so unwillingly, in so far as they are out of tune with the nature of the whole, and in so far as they are disorderly, fighting against the nature of the universe. For whoever is carried by their own doing towards what is opposite to the things that are true is fighting; for they had received starting points from nature beforehand, and by neglecting them they are now unable to tell false things from true. And again, whoever pursues pleasures as goods and runs from pains as evils is impious. For such a person must often find fault with the common nature, as allotting something to the worthless and the worthy against what they deserve, because often the worthless live among pleasures and get the things that produce them, while the worthy fall into pain and the things that produce it. Further, whoever fears pains will at some time also fear something that is going to be in the universe, and that is already impious. And whoever pursues pleasures will not hold back from doing injustice, and that is plainly impious. Now towards the things to which the common nature is equally disposed (for it would not make both if it were not equally disposed to both), those who want to follow nature, being of one mind with it, must also be equally disposed. So whoever is not, for their own part, equally disposed towards pain and pleasure, or death and life, or good repute and ill repute, which the nature of the whole uses equally, is clearly impious. When I say that the common nature uses these equally, I mean that they happen equally, in sequence, to the things that come to be and the things that come after them, by a certain original impulse of providence, by which from a certain origin it set its impulse towards this ordering of the universe, having conceived certain principles of the things that were to be and marked off powers to generate such existences, changes and successions.

9.8–9  One soul has been divided among the animals without reason, and one soul that has mind has been portioned out among the rational ones, just as there is one earth for all earthy things, and we see by one light and breathe one air, all of us that have sight and a soul.

So too, then, everything that shares in a common nature of mind hurries toward its kin in the same way, or even more. For the better it is compared with the rest, the readier it is to mix with what is its own and to be poured together with it. At any rate, right from the start, among the animals without reason there were found swarms and herds and the rearing of nestlings and what one might call loves; for here there were already souls, and the thing that draws together was found growing stronger in what is better, of a kind that did not exist in plants, or in stones or timber. Among the rational animals there were states and friendships and households and gatherings, and in wars treaties and truces. Among those better still, even out of things that stand apart there came to be, in a way, a union, such as there is among the stars. In this way the climb toward the better was able to produce a shared feeling even in things that stand apart. See, then, what is happening now. For only the beings that have mind have now forgotten their hurrying toward one another and their leaning together, and only here is the flowing together not to be seen. And yet, though they run away, they are overtaken and hemmed in all the same; for nature prevails. You will see what I mean if you watch closely. At any rate, one would sooner find something earthy touching nothing earthy than a human being split off from a human being.

9.10  A human being bears fruit, and so does god, and so does the universe; each bears it in its own seasons. But if usage has made the phrase well worn, in its strict sense, for the vine and things like it, that makes no difference. And reason has fruit, both shared and its own, and from it come other things of much the same kind as reason itself.

9.11  If you can, teach them otherwise; and if not, remember that kindness has been given to you for this. The gods too are kind to such people, and toward some things they even work with them, toward health, toward wealth, toward reputation: so good are they. You may be so too; or tell me, who is stopping you?

9.12  Toil, not as someone miserable, and not as someone who wants to be pitied or admired, but want one thing only: to move and to hold back as civic reason thinks right.

9.13  Today I got out of all that hemmed me in, or rather I threw out all that hemmed me in; for it was not outside but inside, in my judgments.

9.14  All these things are familiar in experience, lasting a day in time, and filthy in their matter. Everything now is as it was in the time of those we have buried.

9.15  Things stand outside the doors, themselves by themselves, knowing nothing about themselves and pronouncing nothing. What, then, pronounces on them? The ruling part.

9.16  The bad and the good of the rational and civic animal lie not in what it undergoes but in what it does, just as its virtue and its vice lie not in what it undergoes but in what it does.

9.17  For a stone that has been thrown upward, it is nothing bad to be carried down, and nothing good to be carried up.

9.18  Pass inside, right inside, into their ruling parts, and you will see what judges you are afraid of, and what sort of judges they are of themselves as well.

9.19  All things are in change. You yourself too are in continual alteration and, in a certain respect, in perishing, and so is the whole universe.

9.20  Another person’s wrongdoing must be left there.

9.21  The ending of an activity, the stopping and, as it were, the death of an impulse, of a judgment: nothing bad. Now pass on to the ages of life: childhood, for example, adolescence, youth, old age. For every change of these too is a death. Surely that is nothing terrible? Now pass on to your life under your grandfather, then your life under your mother, then your life under your father; and as you find many other things perishing and changing and ending, ask yourself: “Surely that is nothing terrible?” In the same way, then, neither is the ending and stopping and change of your whole life.

9.22  Run to your own ruling part, and to that of the whole, and to this person’s. To your own, so that you make it a just mind. To that of the whole, so that you remember along with it what you are a part of. To this person’s, so that you take note whether there is ignorance there or intent, and at the same time reckon that it is akin.

9.23  Just as you yourself help to complete a civic whole, so too let every action of yours help to complete a civic life. So any action of yours that does not refer, whether directly or at a distance, to the social goal tears your life apart and does not let it be one, and it is seditious, like the person in a citizen body who, for their own part, stands apart from such harmony.

9.24  Children’s tantrums and toys, and “little breaths carrying corpses”, so that the scene of the Calling Up of the Dead strikes home more vividly.

9.25  Go to the quality of the cause; mark the cause off from the material and look at it. Then also set the limit of the time: the longest that this thing, with its own particular quality, can by nature last.

9.26  You have endured countless things because you were not content with your ruling part doing the kind of things it is made to do. But enough.

9.27  When someone else blames you or hates you, or people voice things of that kind, go to their little souls, pass inside and see what sort of people they are. You will see that you need not be torn apart so that these people may think this or that of you. You must wish them well, however; for by nature they are friends, and the gods too help them in every kind of way, through dreams, through oracles, yet toward the things those people care about.

9.28a  The cycles of the universe are the same, up and down, from age to age. And either the mind of the whole has an impulse towards each thing, and if that is so, accept what its impulse is towards; or it had an impulse once, and the rest follows as a consequence, and why do you strain? For in a way: atoms, or things without parts. In sum: if it is god, all is well; if it is randomness, do not act at random yourself as well.

9.28b–29a  Any moment now earth will cover us all. Then earth itself will change, and those things too will change without end, and those again without end. For a person who reflects on wave after wave of changes and alterations, and on their speed, will look down on everything mortal.

The substance of the whole is a torrent: it carries everything along.

9.29b  And how cheap, too, are these little people who are busy with public affairs and, as they suppose, practical in a philosophic way: full of snot. Man, what then? Do what nature now demands. Set out, if it is given to you, and do not look around to see whether anyone will know. Do not hope for Plato’s republic, but be content if the smallest thing goes forward, and think of the outcome of even this as a small thing. For who will change a principle of theirs? And without a change of principles, what is there but the slavery of people who groan and pretend to be persuaded? Go on now and tell me of Alexander and Philip and Demetrius of Phalerum. It is for them to see whether they saw what the common nature wanted and schooled themselves; but if they played the tragic hero, no one has condemned me to imitate them. The work of philosophy is simple and modest: do not lead me off into solemn conceit.

9.30–31  To look down from above on countless herds and countless rites, on every kind of voyage in storms and in calms, and on the differences among those coming to be, coming together, and passing away. Think too of the life lived long ago by others, the life that will be lived after you, and the life being lived now among the barbarian peoples; and how many do not even know your name, how many will very quickly forget it, how many who perhaps praise you now will very quickly blame you; and that neither being remembered is of any account, nor reputation, nor anything else at all.

Freedom from disturbance about the things that happen from the cause outside; justice in the things done through the cause that comes from you. That is, impulse and action ending in just this, acting socially, as being what is according to nature for you.

9.32a  You can strip away many needless things among those that trouble you, things that rest wholly on your judgment, and you will at once make plenty of open room for yourself.

9.32b–33  By having encompassed the whole universe in thought, and by surveying everlasting eternity, and by thinking of the swift change of each particular thing: how short is the stretch from coming to be to dissolution, and how gaping the time before the coming to be, just as the time after the dissolution is likewise boundless.

Everything you see will very quickly perish, and those who have looked on as it perished will very quickly perish themselves, and whoever dies in extreme old age will be brought level with the one who died before their time.

9.34  What the ruling parts of these people are, what sort of things they are in earnest about, and for what sort of things they love and honour. Think of yourself as looking at their little souls naked. When they suppose that they do harm by blaming or do good by singing praises, how much conceit.

9.35  Loss is nothing other than change. And in this the nature of the whole delights, the nature in accordance with which all things come about well, and from eternity have come about in the same kind of way, and without end there will be others like them. Why then do you say that all things have come about badly and all things will always go badly, and that among so many gods no power, it seems, has ever been found to set these things right, but the universe has been condemned to be held fast in bad things without a break?

9.36  The rottenness of the matter that underlies each thing: water, dust, little bones, a goatish reek. Or again: marble is calluses of the earth, gold and silver are sediment, clothing is little hairs, and purple dye is blood, and all the rest is of this kind. And the little breath too is another thing of this kind, changing out of these things and into these.

9.37a  Enough of the wretched life, the grumbling, the aping. Why are you troubled? What in this is new? What puts you beside yourself? The cause? Look at it. Or the material? Look at it. Beyond these there is nothing. But toward the gods too, now at last, become simpler and better.

9.37b  To have observed these things for a hundred years or for three comes to the same.

9.38  If the person did wrong, the evil is there. But perhaps they did not do wrong.

9.39a  Either everything happens, one thing upon another, as to one body, from one source that has mind, and the part must not find fault with what comes about for the sake of the whole; or there are atoms, and nothing else but a stirred brew and a scattering. Why, then, are you troubled?

9.39b  Do you say to the ruling part: “You are dead, you are ruined, you have turned into a wild beast, you are playing a part, you are running with the herd, you are grazing”?

9.40  Either the gods can do nothing or they can. Now if they cannot, why do you pray? But if they can, why do you not rather pray that they grant you not to fear any of these things, not to desire any of them, not to be pained at any of them, rather than that any of them be absent or be present? For certainly, if they can work with people, they can work with them toward these things too. But perhaps you will say: “The gods have left these things up to me.” Then is it not better to use what is up to you in freedom than to let what is not up to you make a difference to you, in slavery and degradation? And who told you that the gods do not also lend a hand with the things that are up to us? Begin, at any rate, to pray about these, and you will see. This man prays: “How may I sleep with that woman?” You: “How may I not desire to sleep with that woman?” Another: “How may I be rid of that one?” You: “How may I not want to be rid of that one?” Another: “How may I not lose my little child?” You: “How may I not be afraid of losing it?” In short, turn your prayers round like this and watch what happens.

9.41a  Epicurus says: “In my illness my conversations were not about the sufferings of the little body, nor,” he says, “did I talk about such things to those who came in. Instead I went on inquiring into the leading questions of nature, and stayed on this very point: how the mind, though it has its share in such motions in the bit of flesh, stays untroubled, keeping its own good. Nor did I give the doctors the chance,” he says, “to put on airs as though they were doing something. Rather, life went on well and nobly.” So do the same as he did, in illness, if you are ill, and in any other circumstance. For not to stand away from philosophy in anything whatever that befalls, and not to chatter along with someone who is a layman and has not studied nature,

9.41b  … is common to every school. To be occupied only with what is being done now, and with the instrument through which you act.

9.42  Whenever you take offence at someone’s shamelessness, ask yourself at once: “Is it possible, then, for there to be no shameless people in the universe?” It is not possible. Then do not demand the impossible. For this person too is one of those shameless people who must exist in the universe. Have the same thought ready to hand for the scoundrel, and for the faithless person, and for everyone who does wrong in any way whatever. For as soon as you are reminded that it is impossible for this kind of people not to exist, you will be kinder toward each one of them. It is useful too to think at once of this: what virtue nature has given to a human being to meet this wrong. For it has given, as an antidote, gentleness against the unfeeling person, and against another some other power. And in general you are free to teach the one who has gone astray to think otherwise; for everyone who does wrong misses what they were aiming at and has gone astray. And what harm have you in fact suffered? For you will find that none of those you are provoked at has done anything of a kind that was going to make your mind worse; and it is there that what is bad for you, and what harms you, has its whole existence. And what new or strange thing has happened, if the untaught person does what an untaught person does? See whether you ought not rather to blame yourself, because you did not expect that this person would do this wrong. For you did have from reason the means to reflect that this person was likely to do this wrong, and yet you forgot it and are surprised that they have done wrong. Above all, whenever you blame someone as faithless or ungrateful, turn to yourself. For plainly the wrong is yours, whether you trusted that a person with such a disposition would keep faith, or whether, in doing a favour, you did not do it as something finished in itself, nor so as to have received the whole fruit at once from the act itself. For what more do you want when you have done good to a person? Is it not enough that you have done something in accord with your own nature, but you look for a wage for it? As if the eye demanded a return because it sees, or the feet because they walk. For just as these have come into being for one particular thing, and in doing it according to their own build they receive in full what is their own, so too a human being, who is by nature a doer of good, whenever they do some good, or in some other way work together toward things of the middle kind, has done what they were built for, and has what is their own.



BOOK 10

10.1  Will you ever, soul, be good and simple and one and bare, plainer to see than the body wrapped round you? Will you ever taste the loving and affectionate disposition? Will you ever be full and in need of nothing, longing for nothing and desiring nothing, whether living or lifeless, for the enjoyment of pleasures? Nor time in which to enjoy them longer? Nor a place or a country or a mild climate? Nor people who suit you well? But will you be content with your present state and take pleasure in all that is present, and persuade yourself that all things are present to you, from the gods, that all things are well with you and will be well, all that pleases them and all that they are going to give for the preservation of the perfect living being, the good and just and beautiful one, which begets all things and holds them together and surrounds them and takes them into itself as they are dissolved for the birth of others like them? Will you ever be such as to live as a fellow citizen with gods and human beings in such a way that you neither find any fault with them nor are condemned by them?

10.2  Watch what your nature asks for, taking yourself as something governed by nature only. Then do it and accept it, unless your nature as an animal is going to be put in a worse state. Next you must watch what your nature as an animal asks for, and all of this is to be taken up, unless your nature as a rational animal is going to be put in a worse state. And what is rational is by that very fact also civic. Using these rules, then, do not busy yourself with anything further.

10.3  Everything that happens either happens in such a way that you are fitted by nature to bear it, or in such a way that you are not fitted by nature to bear it. So if it happens to you in a way you are fitted by nature to bear, do not resent it, but bear it as you are fitted by nature to do. And if in a way you are not fitted by nature to bear, do not resent it: for it will perish once it has used you up. Remember, though, that you are fitted by nature to bear everything that it rests with your own judgment to make bearable and endurable, through the impression that it is good for you, or is your duty, to do so.

10.4  If someone is mistaken, teach them kindly and show them what they are failing to see. But if you cannot, blame yourself, or not even yourself.

10.5  Whatever happens to you was being made ready for you beforehand, from all time, and the interweaving of causes was spinning together, from eternity, both your existence and the happening of this.

10.6  Whether it is atoms or nature, let this first be laid down: that I am a part of the whole, which is governed by nature. Next, that I stand in a kind of kinship to the parts that are of the same kind as I am. For if I remember these things, then insofar as I am a part, I will not be displeased with anything that is allotted from the whole. For nothing that is good for the whole is harmful to the part. For the whole has nothing that is not good for itself. All natures have this in common, but the nature of the universe has this in addition, that it is not even forced by any cause from outside to produce anything harmful to itself. So by remembering that I am a part of a whole of this kind, I will be well pleased with everything that comes about. And insofar as I stand in a kind of kinship to the parts of the same kind, I will do nothing unsocial. Rather I will make those of my own kind my aim, and I will lead my every impulse toward what is good for all in common and lead it away from the opposite. When these things are carried through in this way, life must flow well, just as you would think that a citizen’s life flows well when that citizen goes forward through actions that profit the citizens, and welcomes whatever the city allots.

10.7  The parts of the whole, all that are by nature contained by the universe, must perish; but let “perish” be said in the sense of “be altered”. Now if this is by nature both bad and necessary for them, the whole would not be well managed, with its parts going into alteration and built in various ways for perishing. For did nature itself set out to treat its own parts badly, and to make them both liable to fall into what is bad and bound by necessity to fall into what is bad, or did such things come about without nature noticing? For both are unbelievable. And if someone were to leave nature out of it and explain these things by the way they naturally are, even so it would be ridiculous to say that the parts of the whole are naturally such as to change, and at the same time to be surprised or annoyed as though at something happening against nature, especially when the dissolution is into the things out of which each is put together. For it is either a scattering of the elements out of which it was compounded, or a turning of the solid into the earthy and of the breath-like into the airy, so that these too are taken back up into the reason of the whole, whether the whole is burned up in fire at each cycle or renewed by everlasting exchanges. And do not picture the solid and the breath-like as the ones that date from the first birth. For all of this took its inflow yesterday and the day before, from food and from the air drawn in. So what changes is this that it took in, not what your mother bore. But suppose that this binds you very closely to that individually qualified thing, which, I think, has no bearing on what is being said now.

10.8  Having given yourself these names: good, modest, truthful, clear-minded, like-minded, high-minded, take care that you never come to bear other names; and if you do lose these names, go back to them quickly. Remember that “clear-minded” was to mean for you a discerning attention to each thing, and not letting things go unheeded; “like-minded”, the willing acceptance of what the common nature assigns; and “high-minded”, the lifting high of the part that has mind, above the smooth or rough movement of the flesh, above petty reputation, above death and everything of that kind. So if you keep yourself in these names, without craving to have others call you by them, you will be a different person and will enter a different life. For to go on being such as you have been up to now, and to be torn and soiled in a life like this, is the mark of someone thoroughly senseless and clinging to life, someone like the half-eaten beast-fighters who, full of wounds and gore, still beg to be kept until tomorrow, to be thrown, just as they are, to the same claws and bites. So put yourself aboard these few names, and if you can stay on them, stay, like someone who has moved to some Islands of the Blessed. But if you sense that you are falling away and are not prevailing, go away with a good heart to some corner where you will prevail, or even leave life altogether, not in anger, but simply, freely and modestly, having at least done this one thing, and only this, in your life: to leave it in this way. Toward remembering the names, though, it will help you greatly to remember the gods, and that what they want is not to be flattered, but for all rational beings to become like them, and for the fig tree to do the work of a fig tree, the dog the work of a dog, the bee the work of a bee, and the human being the work of a human being.

10.9  Farce, war, panic, numbness, slavery: day by day those sacred principles of yours will be wiped out, all those that you picture without study of nature and let go by. But everything must be looked at and done in such a way that at one and the same time the practical side is carried out and the contemplative side is at work, and that the self-assurance which comes from knowledge of each thing is kept, unnoticed but not hidden. For when will you enjoy simplicity? When dignity? When the recognition, in each case, of what the thing is in its substance, what place it has in the universe, how long it is in its nature to last, what it is put together from, who can have it, and who can give it and take it away?

10.10  A little spider thinks a great deal of itself for having hunted down a fly; someone else for a little hare, another for a sprat in a hand net, another for little boars, another for bears, another for Sarmatians. For are these not robbers, if you examine their principles?

10.11a  How all things change into one another: get yourself a method for contemplating it, attend to it constantly, and train yourself in this field. For nothing so produces greatness of mind.

10.11b  Such a person has stripped off the body and, having taken in that very soon they will have to leave all these things behind as they go from among people, has given their whole self over: to justice in what they do themselves, and to the nature of the whole in the other things, those that happen. What anyone will say or think about them, or do against them, they do not even let into their mind, being content with these two things: if they themselves act justly in what is being done now, and if they love what is being allotted to them now. They have let go of all busyness and all eager pursuits, and want nothing else than to keep a straight course right through, by way of the law, and to follow god, who keeps a straight course right through.

10.12a  What need is there of guessing, when you are free to look at what has to be done, and, if you see it clearly, to go that way with good will and without turning back; and if you do not see it clearly, to hold off and use the best advisers; and if some other things stand in the way of this, to go forward as present means allow, in a considered way, holding to what appears just? For to reach this is best, since failure, at any rate, is falling away from this.

10.12b  Someone who follows reason in everything is something at once unhurried and quick to move, at once cheerful and composed.

10.13  Ask yourself as soon as you come out of sleep: will it make any difference to you if what is just and right is blamed by someone else? It will not. Have you forgotten that these people who snort and prance in their praise and blame of others are like this in bed and like this at table, and what things they do, what they run from, what they chase, what they steal, what they seize, not with hands and feet but with the most precious part of themselves, the part that becomes, when it wants to, good faith, a sense of shame, truth, law, a good spirit?

10.14  To nature, which gives everything and takes everything back, the person who is educated and modest says: “Give what you want. Take back what you want.” And that person says this not in bold defiance, but only in obedience to it and good will toward it.

10.15  What is left now is little. Live as on a mountain. For it makes no difference whether there or here, if a person, wherever they are, lives in the universe as in a city. Let human beings see, let them study, a true human being living according to nature. If they do not bear it, let them kill that person. For that is better than to live that way.

10.16  No longer to discuss at all what kind of man the good man is, but to be such a man.

10.17–18  An impression, constantly, of the whole of eternity and the whole of substance, and that all particular things are, set against substance, a fig seed, and set against time, a turn of a drill.

Fixing attention on each object, think of it as already dissolving and in change, and as it were in the course of rotting or scattering, or in whatever way it is the nature of each thing, so to speak, to die.

10.19  What they are like when eating, sleeping, mating, emptying their bowels, and the rest. Then what they are like when lording it and giving themselves airs, or getting angry and scolding from a position of superiority. Yet a little while ago they were slaves, to how many, and for what sort of things. And in a little while they will be in the same sort of case.

10.20  What the nature of the whole brings to each is good for each, and it is good at the time when nature brings it.

10.21  “The earth loves the rain, and the holy sky loves,” and the universe loves to bring about whatever is to come. So I say to the universe: “I love along with you.” And is that, too, perhaps said in this sense: “this loves to happen”?

10.22  Either you live here, and by now you are used to it; or you are going out from here, and that is what you wanted; or you are dying, and you have completed your service. Besides these there is nothing. So be of good heart.

10.23–24  Let it always be clear that the country place over there is just like this, and how everything here is the same as on the top of the mountain or on the seashore or wherever you like. For you will find exactly what Plato says: “putting a sheepfold round himself on a mountain,” he says, “and milking the bleaters.”

What is my ruling part to me, and what kind of thing am I making it now, and what am I using it for now? Can it be empty of mind? Can it be cut loose and torn away from community? Can it be melted into the bit of flesh and blended with it, so that it turns as the flesh turns?

10.25  Whoever flees from their master is a runaway slave. And the law is master, so whoever breaks the law is a runaway slave too. But whoever is distressed or angry or afraid, too, does not want one of the things appointed by the one who governs all things to have happened, or to be happening, or to be going to happen; and the one who governs all things is law, as the one who deals out all that falls to each. So whoever is afraid or distressed or angry is a runaway slave.

10.26  A man lets seed go into a womb and goes away, and from then on another cause takes it over, works on it and brings a baby to completion. From what a thing, what a thing! Again: he lets food go down through the throat, and from then on another cause takes it over and makes perception and impulse and, in sum, life and strength and other things (how many, and what things!). So: to look at these things that come about under such a veil, and to see the power just as we see the power that presses downward and the power that rises upward, not with the eyes, but no less clearly.

10.27  Think continually how all things of the kind that happen now happened before as well, and think of them as going to happen. And set before your eyes whole plays and scenes of the same kind, all that you have come to know from your own experience or from older history: for example the whole court of Hadrian, and the whole court of Antoninus, and the whole court of Philip, of Alexander, of Croesus. For all of those were of this kind, only with other people.

10.28  Picture everyone who is pained or displeased at anything whatever as like the piglet that is being sacrificed and kicks and squeals. Like it too is the one who laments on their little bed, alone, in silence. Picture also how we are bound, and that to the rational living creature alone it has been given to follow willingly what happens, while simply to follow is a necessity for all.

10.29  At each of the things you do, one by one, stop and ask yourself whether death is something terrible because of being deprived of this.

10.30  Whenever you take offence at someone’s wrongdoing, turn at once to yourself and reckon up what wrong of a similar kind you do: for example, judging money to be a good, or pleasure, or a scrap of reputation, and so on, kind by kind. For when you put your mind to this you will quickly forget your anger, as it strikes you at the same time that they are being forced: for what are they to do? Or, if you can, take away from them what is forcing them.

10.31a  When you see Satyrion, picture Socraticus or Eutyches or Hymen; and when you see Euphrates, picture Eutychion or Silvanus; and with Alciphron picture Tropaeophorus; and when you see Severus, picture Crito or Xenophon; and when you look at yourself, picture one of the Caesars; and with each person the one who corresponds. Then let this come to you along with it: where, then, are those people? Nowhere, or wherever it may be. For in this way you will continually look on human things as smoke and nothing, especially if you remember along with it that what has once changed will not exist any more in boundless time. Why then do you strain? Why is it not enough for you to get across this short stretch in good order?

10.31b  What material, what a subject, you are running away from! For what are all these things but exercises for a reason that has looked exactly, and as a student of nature, at the things in life? Stay, then, until you have made these things too your own, as a strong stomach makes everything its own, as a bright fire makes flame and light out of whatever you throw into it.

10.32–33  Let it not be possible for anyone to say truthfully of you that you are not straightforward or that you are not good, but let anyone who is going to judge any of these things about you be mistaken. All of this is in your power: for who prevents you from being good and straightforward? You have only to decide not to go on living unless you are going to be such a person, for reason does not require you to live either, if you are not such.

With this material, what is it that can be done or said in the soundest way? For whatever it is, it is possible to do it or say it, and do not make the excuse that you are being prevented. You will not stop groaning until this has come over you: that what luxury is to those who live for pleasure, that is what it is for you to do, with the material that is supplied and comes to hand, the things that belong to the way a human being is made. For one must judge everything that it is possible to do in accordance with one’s own nature to be an enjoyment. And it is possible everywhere. Now a cylinder is not granted everywhere to be carried along in its own motion, nor is water, nor fire, nor the other things that are governed by nature or by a soul without reason: for the things that bar them and stand in their way are many. But mind and reason can make their way through everything that falls against them, in the way their nature is and in the way they wish. Set this ease before your eyes, the ease with which reason will be carried through all things as fire is carried up, as a stone down, as a cylinder down a slope, and look for nothing more. For the remaining obstacles either belong to the little body, the dead thing, or, without a judgment and the giving in of reason itself, they break no one and do nothing bad at all. Otherwise the one affected would themselves at once become bad. At any rate with all other made things, whatever bad thing happens to any of them, the thing affected itself becomes worse because of it. But here, if it must be said, the human being becomes both better and more praiseworthy by using rightly the things that befall them. In general, remember that nothing harms one who is by nature a citizen that does not harm the city, and nothing harms the city that does not harm the law. And of these so-called strokes of bad luck not one harms the law. So what does not harm the law harms neither city nor citizen.

10.34  For someone who has been steeped in the true principles, even the shortest saying, one that lies open to everybody, is enough as a reminder of freedom from grief and fear. For example: “Leaves, some the wind pours to the ground: so the generation of men.” Your little children are little leaves too; little leaves too are these that shout convincingly and applaud, or on the contrary curse, or quietly find fault and mock; and little leaves in the same way are those who will receive in succession the fame that comes after death. For all these “come on in the season of spring”. Then the wind has thrown them down; then the forest grows others in their place. Shortness of life is common to all, but you run from all things and chase all things as if they were going to last for ever. A little while and you will close your eyes, and already someone else will be mourning the one who carried you out.

10.35  The healthy eye must see everything that can be seen, and not say, “I want the green things”; for that belongs to someone whose eyes are diseased. And healthy hearing and healthy smell must be ready for everything that can be heard and smelled, and the healthy stomach must be the same toward all foods, as a mill is toward everything it was built to grind. So too the healthy mind must be ready for everything that happens. But the mind that says, “Let the little ones be kept safe,” and “Let everyone praise whatever I do,” is an eye that looks for the green things, or teeth that look for the soft things.

10.36  No one is so fortunate that, as they die, there will not be some people standing by who welcome the bad thing that is happening. Say the person was good and wise: may there not, at the very last, be someone who says to themselves: “Shall we at last get a breathing space from this minder? He was not hard on any of us, but I could feel that he was quietly condemning us.” That, then, in the case of the good person. But in our case how many other things there are on account of which many a person is longing to be rid of us! So you will think of this as you die, and you will go out more easily, reasoning: “I am leaving a life of this kind, in which my very partners, for whom I struggled so much, prayed, took thought, those very people want me to go, hoping perhaps to get some other relief out of it. Why, then, would anyone hold on to a longer stay here?” Still, do not for that reason go away less kindly toward them, but keeping to your own habit: friendly, well-disposed and gracious. And, on the other hand, not as one being torn away; rather, just as in a person who dies an easy death the little soul slips easily out of the body, such too must the withdrawal from these people be. For nature tied you to these people too and put you together with them, but now it unties. I am being untied, as from my own people, it is true, yet not pulled back but without force; for this too is one of the things that accord with nature.

10.37  Train yourself, as far as you can, with everything that anyone does, to ask in your own mind: “To what end is this person doing this?” But begin with yourself, and examine yourself first.

10.38  Remember that what pulls the strings is that thing hidden inside. That is oratory, that is life, that, if one must say it, is the human being. Never picture along with it the vessel-like thing that surrounds it and these little tools moulded around it. For they are like a carpenter’s axe, differing only in being grown on. For without the cause that moves them and holds them in check these parts are in fact of no more use than the weaver’s shuttle, the writer’s pen and the charioteer’s little whip.

11.1  What is the rational soul’s own: it sees itself, it analyses itself, it makes itself whatever it wishes, it gathers for itself the fruit it bears (for the fruits of plants, and what corresponds to them in animals, are gathered by others), and it reaches its own end wherever the limit of life comes upon it. It is not as with dancing and acting and things of that kind, where the whole action falls short of its end if something cuts in, but at every part, and wherever it is overtaken, it makes what was set before it full and in need of nothing more, for itself, so that it can say: “I have what is mine in full.” Further, it goes round the whole universe and the void around it and its shape, and it stretches out into the boundlessness of time, and it takes in the periodic rebirth of the whole and considers it from every side, and it observes that those after us will see nothing newer and that those before us saw nothing more remarkable, but that in a way a person of forty, if they have any mind at all, has seen all that has been and all that will be, in that it is all of one kind. Also the rational soul’s own are love of those near it, and truthfulness, and a sense of shame, and valuing nothing above itself, which is law’s own too. In this way, then, right reason and the reason of justice turn out not to differ at all.

11.2  You will look down on delightful singing, on dancing and on the pankration, if you divide the tuneful voice into each of its notes and ask yourself at each one whether this gets the better of you, for you will be ashamed; and with dancing, if you do the corresponding thing at each movement or pose; and the same with the pankration too. In general, then, except for virtue and what comes from virtue, remember to run to the parts, and by dividing them to come to look down on them; and carry the same thing over to the whole of life as well.

11.3  What a soul it is that is ready, if it must this moment be released from the body, and either be extinguished or be scattered or hold together. But this readiness should come from a judgment of one’s own, not by way of bare opposition, as with the Christians, but in a reasoned and dignified way, and in a way that could convince someone else too, without theatrics.

11.4–5  I have done something as a social being: then I have been benefited. See that this always comes to hand, and never stop.

What is your craft? To be good. And how does that come about well, except from points of theory, some about the nature of the whole, others about the make-up proper to the human being?

11.6  First, tragedies were brought on as reminders of the things that happen, and that it is in the nature of these things to happen this way, and that what charms you on the stage should not weigh on you on the larger stage. For you see that these things have to be carried through this way, and that even those who cry out “O Cithaeron!” bear them. And some things are said usefully by those who write the plays. This above all, for example: “But if I and my two children have been neglected by the gods, this too has its reason.” And again: “For one should not be angry with events.” And: “To reap life like a fruitful ear of grain.” And everything of that kind. After tragedy, the Old Comedy was brought on. It had a tutor’s frankness and, by its very plainness of speech, reminded people, not uselessly, of freedom from conceit. It was for some such purpose that Diogenes too took these things up. As for the Middle Comedy after it, and lastly the New, which little by little slipped down into cleverness at imitation, consider just what it has been taken up for. For it is not unknown that some useful things are said by these writers too; but the whole undertaking of this kind of poetry and play-making, just what aim did it look to?

11.7  How plainly it comes home that there is no other situation in life so suited to philosophy as this one in which you now happen to be.

11.8a  A branch cut off from the branch next to it cannot help being cut off from the whole plant as well. So too a human being split off from one human being has fallen away from the whole community. Now a branch is cut off by someone else; but it is the human being who separates themselves from their neighbour, by coming to hate and turning away, and they do not know that they have at the same time cut themselves away from the whole civic body as well. Yet there is this gift from Zeus, who put the community together: we are allowed to grow together again with the one next to us and again to help to complete the whole. But when this kind of division happens many times, it makes what withdraws hard to unite and hard to restore. And in general, the branch that sprouted together from the beginning and has stayed together, sharing one breath, is not like the one grafted in again after being cut off, whatever it is that the gardeners say.

11.8b–9  To grow on the same stem, but not to hold the same principles.

Just as those who stand in your way as you go forward in accord with right reason will not be able to turn you aside from sound action, so let them not knock you out of your kindness toward them either; but keep watch over yourself on both counts alike: not only in steady judgment and action, but also in gentleness toward those who try to hinder you or are otherwise annoyed. For this too is weakness, to be harsh with them, just as it is to abandon the action and give in from fright. For both are equally deserters from their post: the one who has flinched, and the one who has become estranged from a person who is by nature kin and friend.

11.10  “No nature is inferior to art”; for the arts imitate the natures. If that is so, the nature that is the most complete of all natures and embraces the most would not fall short of the ingenuity of art. Now all the arts make the inferior things for the sake of the superior; so the common nature does too. And it is from this that justice is born, and from justice that the rest of the virtues have their existence; for what is just will not be kept if we either care about the things in between, or are easily deceived, hasty and changeable.

11.11  The things whose pursuit and avoidance throw you into turmoil do not come to you, but in a way you yourself go to them. At least let your judgment about them be quiet, and they will stay unmoving, and you will be seen neither pursuing nor avoiding.

11.12  The sphere of the soul is true to its own form when it neither stretches out toward anything nor runs together inward, neither coils up nor sinks in on itself, but shines with a light by which it sees the truth, the truth of all things and the truth within itself.

11.13  Will someone despise me? That is for them to see to. But I will see to it that I am not found doing or saying anything that deserves to be despised. Will they hate me? That is for them to see to. But I am kind and well-disposed to everyone, and ready to show this very person what they are failing to see, not with reproach, nor as if making a show of putting up with them, but genuinely and good-heartedly, like the famous Phocion, if indeed he was not pretending. For what is within must be like this, and a human being must be seen by the gods to be one who is not disposed to be indignant at anything and makes a grievance of nothing. For what is bad for you, if you yourself are now doing what is proper to your nature, and are accepting what is now in season for the nature of the whole, a human being straining toward this: that what is good for all in common should come about, by whatever means?

11.14  Despising one another, they fawn on one another; and wanting to be above one another, they take the lower place beneath one another.

11.15  How rotten and counterfeit is the person who says: “I have chosen to be straightforward with you.” What are you doing, man? This is not a thing to announce beforehand. It will show of itself. It ought to be written on the forehead. At once the voice has that ring, at once it stands out in the eyes, just as the one who is loved at once knows everything in the look of the lovers. In short, the straightforward and good person must be like someone who stinks like a goat, so that whoever stands near notices as soon as they come up, whether they want to or not. But a studied straightforwardness is a dagger. Nothing is more shameful than a wolf’s friendship: shun this above all things. The good and straightforward and kind person has these things in the eyes, and it does not escape notice.

11.16  To live life through in the finest way: this power is in the soul, if a person is indifferent toward the things that are indifferent. And a person will be indifferent if they look at each of these things taken apart and as a whole, and remember that none of them puts a judgment about itself into us, nor comes to us, but the things stay still, and it is we who produce the verdicts about them and, as it were, write them in ourselves, when it is in our power not to write them, and in our power, even if one somehow escapes notice, to wipe it out at once; and remember that this kind of attention will be for a short time, and after that life will be over. But what is hard in it, if these things are otherwise? For if they are according to nature, rejoice in them and let them be easy for you. And if they are contrary to nature, look for what there is for you that is according to your own nature and hurry toward that, even if it brings no reputation. For there is pardon for everyone who looks for their own good.

11.17–18  Where each thing has come from, and what underlying materials each is made of, and what it is changing into, and what it will be like when it has changed, and that it will suffer nothing bad.

And first: what my relation to them is, and that we have come into being for one another’s sake; and that, on another account, I have come into being to stand at their head, as a ram stands at the head of a flock or a bull of a herd. But go back further and start from this: if not atoms, then nature is what governs the whole; if that is so, the worse things are for the sake of the better, and the better for the sake of one another. Second: what sort of people they are at table, in bed, and so on; and above all, what compulsions their principles have laid on them; and these very things, with what conceit they do them. Third: that if they do these things rightly, one should not be annoyed; and if not rightly, then plainly they do them against their will and in ignorance. For every soul is deprived against its will, as of the truth, so too of dealing with each person as they deserve. At any rate they are aggrieved to hear themselves called unjust and unfeeling and greedy and, in a word, prone to do wrong to their neighbours. Fourth: that you yourself also do many wrongs and are another of the same kind; and if you do hold back from certain wrongs, you still have the disposition that brings them on, even if it is through cowardice or hunger for reputation or some such bad thing that you hold back from wrongs like theirs. Fifth: that you have not even grasped for certain whether they are doing wrong; for many things are done with some further purpose in view, and in general one must first learn many things in order to pronounce on another’s action with a sure grasp. Sixth: that, whenever you are extremely indignant or even take it hard, human life is a moment long, and in a little while we are all laid out. Seventh: that it is not their actions that trouble us, for those are in their ruling parts, but our own judgments. At any rate, take away the verdict that this is something terrible, and be willing to let it go, and the anger is gone. How, then, will you take it away? By reasoning that it is not shameful. For if what is shameful is not the only bad thing, you too must do many wrongs and become a robber and anything at all. Eighth: how much harder are the things that our fits of anger and distress over such things bring on than the things themselves are at which we are angry and distressed. Ninth: that kindness is unbeatable, if it is genuine and not a grin or play-acting. For what will the most insolent person do to you, if you go on being kind to them and, if the chance comes, gently advise them and teach them otherwise, at your ease, at the very moment when they are trying to do you harm: “No, child. We are made by nature for something else. I shall certainly not be harmed; it is you who are being harmed, child.” And show, with a light touch and in general terms, that this is so: that not even bees do it, nor any of the creatures that are by nature herd animals. And it must be done neither with irony nor with reproach, but with affection and with no bite in your soul; and not as in a lecture room, nor so that someone else standing by may admire, but to that person alone, even if some others are standing around. Remember these nine headings as gifts you have received from the Muses, and begin at last to be a human being, while you live. One must guard against flattering them just as much as against being angry with them, for both are unsocial and lead to harm. And in your fits of anger have this ready to hand: that it is not flying into a rage that is manly, but that what is gentle and mild, as it is more human, is also more masculine; and that it is this that has a share of strength and sinew and manly courage, not the one who is indignant and discontented. For the closer this is to freedom from passion, the closer it is to power. And just as distress belongs to a weak person, so does anger; for both have been wounded and have given in. And if you like, take a tenth gift too, from the Leader of the Muses: that to require bad people not to do wrong is madness, for it aims at the impossible. And to allow them to be such to others, but to require that they not do wrong to you, is unfeeling and tyrannical.

11.19  Four turns that the ruling part takes must above all be guarded against without a break and, whenever you catch them, wiped away, saying over each one as follows: “This imagining is not necessary.” “This one dissolves community.” “This that you are about to say is not from yourself.” For count it among the most absurd things not to speak from yourself. The fourth is the one for which you will reproach yourself: that this is the mark of the more divine part in you being beaten and lying down under the less worthy and mortal portion, that of the body, and under its rough or smooth movements.

So even the elements heed the whole: once they have been posted somewhere by force, they stay until, from there in turn, the signal for dissolution is given. Is it not terrible, then, that only the part of you that is of mind is disobedient and resents its own post? And yet nothing forced is ordered of it, only the things that are in accordance with its nature. Still it does not bear them, but is carried the opposite way. For the movement towards acts of injustice and acts of self-indulgence, towards angers and griefs and fears, is nothing other than the movement of one who is deserting nature. And whenever the ruling part is discontented with any of the things that happen, then too it leaves its own post. For it has been made for holiness and reverence for the gods no less than for justice. For these too come under good fellowship, or rather they come before acts of justice.

For whoever directs all their private impulses toward this aim will make all their actions alike, and in this way will always be the same.

12.1  All those things you pray to reach by a roundabout road you can have already, if you do not begrudge yourself. That is: if you leave all the past behind, hand the future over to providence, and steer only the present toward piety and justice. Piety, so that you love what is allotted, for nature brought it to you and you to it. Justice, so that, freely and without winding about, you speak the truth and do what accords with law and with what each deserves. And do not be hindered by another person’s badness, or judgment, or voice, or even by the sensation of the bit of flesh that has grown up around you, for the part that suffers will see to that. So if, whenever it is that you come close to your exit, you leave everything else behind and honor only your ruling part and what is divine in you, and if what you fear is not that you will some day stop living but rather never beginning to live according to nature, then you will be a human being worthy of the universe that gave you birth. You will stop being a stranger in your own country, stop wondering at what happens every day as if it were unexpected, and stop hanging on this and that.

12.2–3  God sees all ruling parts bare of their material vessels and husks and refuse. For with only the part of himself that is of mind he touches only what has flowed out of himself into these and been channelled off. If you too make a habit of doing this, you will strip away most of your distraction. For someone who does not look at the surrounding scraps of flesh is hardly likely to busy themselves with gazing at clothing and a house and reputation and that kind of wrapping and stage scenery.

There are three things of which you are made up: a little body, a little breath, mind. Of these the other two are yours so far as you must take care of them, but only the third is properly yours. So if you separate from yourself, that is, from your thought, all that others do or say, or all that you yourself have done or said, and all that troubles you as something to come, and all that belongs to you, without your choosing it, from the little body that surrounds you or the little breath born with you, and all that the whirl streaming round outside sets spinning, so that the power of mind, lifted clear of what is bound up with it by fate, and clean, lives set loose by itself, doing what is just and willing what happens and saying what is true; if, I say, you separate from this ruling part the things fastened onto it by attachment, and what of time lies ahead or has gone by, and make yourself like the sphere of Empedocles, “rounded, rejoicing in its encircling solitude”, and practise living only what you are living, that is, the present: then what is left until you die, at least, you will be able to live through untroubled, kindly, and gracious toward your own guardian spirit.

12.4  I have often wondered how it is that each person loves themselves more than everyone else, yet sets less value on their own judgment about themselves than on that of others. At any rate, if a god appears beside someone, or a sensible teacher, and tells them to turn over nothing in their mind and think nothing to themselves that they will not at the same moment bring out, crying it aloud, they will not put up with this for even one day. So it is that we feel more shame before those near us, at what they will think of us, than before ourselves.

12.5  How can it be that the gods, who arranged all things well and with love for humankind, overlooked this one thing only: that some people, and very good ones, who had made a great many contracts, so to speak, with the divine, and who through holy deeds and sacred rites had come to be on the closest terms with the divine, once they have died, do not come to be again any more, but are completely extinguished? But if this really is so, be sure that if it ought to have been otherwise, they would have made it otherwise. For if it were just, it would also be possible, and if it were in accord with nature, nature would have brought it about. So from its not being so, if indeed it is not so, be assured that it ought not to have come about so. For you see for yourself that in prying into this you are arguing a case of justice with god. And we would not be reasoning with the gods like this unless they are best and most just. And if that is so, they would not have let anything in the order of things be neglected unjustly and against reason.

12.6  Practise even the things you have given up on. For in fact the left hand, though for lack of practice it is no good at anything else, holds the bridle more firmly than the right, because this is what it has had practice in.

12.7–9  What a person ought to be like, in body and in soul, when death catches them. The shortness of life. The gaping expanse of time behind and ahead. The weakness of all matter.

To look at what is causal, bare of its husks. The aims of actions. What pain is. What pleasure is. What death is. What reputation is. Who is the cause of a person’s own busyness. How no one is hindered by another. That everything is judgment.

And in the use of principles one must be like the all-in fighter, not the gladiator. For the gladiator puts down the sword he uses and picks it up; but the other always has his hand, and needs only to clench it.

12.10  See things as being of this kind, dividing them into matter, cause, purpose.

12.11a  How great a power a human being has: to do nothing other than what god is going to praise, and to accept everything god assigns to them, whatever it is.

12.11b–12  What follows in order on nature.

The gods are not to be blamed, for they go wrong in nothing, willingly or unwillingly. Nor are people, for they go wrong in nothing except unwillingly. So no one is to be blamed.

12.13  How ridiculous, and what a stranger, is the person who is surprised at anything at all that happens in life.

12.14–15  Either a necessity of fate and an order that cannot be overstepped, or a providence that can be appeased, or a jumble of randomness with no one in charge. If, then, it is a necessity that cannot be overstepped, why do you strain against it? If it is a providence that allows itself to be appeased, make yourself worthy of the help that comes from the divine. And if it is a jumble with nothing ruling it, be glad that in such a rough sea you yourself have in yourself a mind that rules. And if the sea carries you away, let it carry away the poor flesh, the bit of breath, the rest; for the mind it will not carry away.

Or does the light of a lamp shine until it goes out, and not lose its brightness, while the truth and justice and self-control in you will go out first?

12.16  In the case of someone who has given the impression of having done wrong: “But how do I know whether this is a wrong?” And if they did do wrong, that they have condemned themselves, and so this is like tearing one’s own face. That whoever does not want the bad person to do wrong is like someone who does not want the fig tree to bear sap in its figs, or babies to cry, or the horse to neigh, or any of the other things that have to be. For what are they to do, having a disposition like that? So if you are vigorous, cure that disposition.

12.17a  If it is not fitting, do not do it; if it is not true, do not say it. For let your impulse be …

12.17b  Always see in full what that thing itself is which produces the impression in you, and open it out, dividing it into the cause, into the material, into the purpose, into the time within which it will have to be over.

12.19–21  Notice at last that you have in yourself something stronger and more divine than the things that produce the passions and that, in a word, pull you about on strings. What is my thought at this moment? Can it be fear? Suspicion? Desire? Anything else of that kind?

First, not at random and not without a point of reference. Second, to take as the point of reference nothing other than the social end.

That before long you will be no one and nowhere, nor will any of the things you now see be, nor any of the people now living. For it is the nature of all things to change, to turn and to perish, so that other things may come to be after them.

12.22  That all things are judgment, and this judgment is up to you. So take the judgment away when you wish, and, just as for someone who has rounded the headland, there is calm: all things steady, and a bay without a wave.

12.23  Any single activity, whichever it is, that stops at the right time suffers nothing bad in that it has stopped; nor has the one who did this action suffered anything bad just in this respect, that it has stopped. In the same way, then, the sum made up of all the actions, which is a life, if it stops at the right time, suffers nothing bad just in this respect, that it has stopped; nor has the one who brought this chain to a stop at the right time been put in a bad state. The right time and the limit are given by nature: sometimes by one’s own nature too, when the stopping comes in old age, but in every case by the nature of the whole, with the changing of whose parts the entire universe stays always young and in its prime. And everything that benefits the whole is always fine and in season. So for each person the stopping of life is not a bad thing, since it is not a shameful thing either, if indeed it is both outside one’s choice and not unsocial; and it is a good thing, if indeed it comes at the right time for the whole, benefits the whole and is carried along with the whole. For in this way a person is also carried by god: the one who is carried in the same way as god and is carried, in their purpose, toward the same things.

12.24  These three things must be kept ready to hand. About what you do: whether it is neither at random nor otherwise than Justice herself would have acted. About what happens from outside: that it comes either by chance or by providence, and that one must neither blame chance nor bring a charge against providence. Second: what each thing is like from seed to the receiving of a soul, and from the receiving of a soul to the giving back of the soul; and out of what kind of things it is put together and into what kind of things it is dissolved. Third: that if you were suddenly lifted high into the air and looked down on human affairs and their many turns, you will despise them, seeing at the same time how much there is that dwells all around, in the air and in the ether; and that however often you are lifted up, you will see the same things: how alike in kind, how short-lived. And over these things, the puffed-up pride.

12.25–26  Throw out the judgment: you are saved. Who then is stopping you from throwing it out?

Whenever you take something hard, you have forgotten that all things come about according to the nature of the whole; and that the wrong done is another’s; and, on top of these, that everything that comes about has always come about in this way, and will come about, and is coming about now everywhere; and how great is the kinship of a human being with the whole human race, for it is a sharing not of a little blood or a little seed but of mind. And you have forgotten too that each person’s mind is a god and has flowed from there; that nothing is anyone’s own, but even one’s little child and little body and the little soul itself have come from there; that everything is judgment; that each person lives only the present, and this is what they lose.

12.27  Go back continually over those who were exceedingly indignant at something, those who were at their height in the greatest fame or disasters or enmities or fortunes of whatever kind. Then stop and consider: where is all that now? Smoke and ash and a tale, or not even a tale. And let everything of this kind come to you along with it, for example: Fabius Catullinus in the country, and Lusius Lupus in the gardens, and Stertinius at Baiae, and Tiberius on Capri, and Velius Rufus, and in general the setting store by anything whatever, together with self-importance; and how cheap everything is that people strain after, and how much more philosophical it is, in the material one has been given, to show oneself just, self-controlled, following the gods, without affectation. For the vanity that smoulders under a cover of having none is the most troublesome of all.

12.28  To those who ask: “Well, where have you seen the gods, or from what have you grasped that they exist, that you revere them as you do?” First, they are visible even to the eye. Second, though, I have not seen my own soul either, and yet I honour it. So with the gods too: from what I experience of their power on every occasion, from that I both grasp that they exist and stand in awe of them.

12.29  What keeps a life safe: to see each thing through and through, what it is in itself, what in it is the material and what the cause; with the whole soul to do what is just and to say what is true. What is left but to enjoy living, joining one good to another so as to leave not even the shortest gap?

12.30  One light of the sun, even if it is divided by walls, by mountains, by countless other things. One common substance, even if it is divided by countless bodies, each with its own quality. One soul, even if it is divided by countless natures and individual outlines. One soul that has mind, even if it seems to be separated. Now the other parts of the things just named, such as breaths and the matter that underlies things, are without sensation and have no attachment to one another; and yet even those are held together by what makes them one and by the weight that bears toward the same place. But thought, in a way of its own, stretches toward what is of the same kind and joins with it, and the social feeling is not divided.

12.31  What are you looking for? To breathe? Or to have sensation? To have impulse? To grow? And then to stop? To use your voice? To think? Which of these seems to you worth longing for? But if each of them is easy to despise, go on to the last thing, following reason and god. But to value these things, to be vexed that by being dead a person will be deprived of them, conflicts with that.

12.32  How small a part of boundless, gaping eternity has been portioned out to each one: for it vanishes very quickly into the everlasting. And how small a part of the whole of substance, and how small a part of the whole of soul. And on how small a clod of the whole earth you crawl. Reflecting on all this, picture nothing as great except this: to act as your own nature leads, and to undergo things as the common nature brings them.

12.33  How is the ruling part using itself? For everything lies in that. As for the rest, it is either within choice or outside it: dead things and smoke.

12.34–36  What most rouses one to despise death is that even those who judge pleasure good and pain bad have despised it all the same.

A person for whom only what comes at the right time is good, for whom performing more actions in line with right reason is equal to performing fewer, and to whom it makes no difference whether they look on the universe for a longer or a shorter time: to that person not even death is a thing to fear.

Man, you have been a citizen in this great city. What difference does it make to you whether for five years or for three? For what accords with the laws is equal for each. So what is terrible if you are sent away from the city not by a tyrant, nor by an unjust judge, but by nature, which brought you in, just as if the magistrate who engaged a comic actor were to dismiss him from the stage? “But I have spoken not the five acts but the three.” Well said; in life, however, the three are the whole play. For what is complete is fixed by the one who was responsible then for the putting together and is responsible now for the dissolving; you are responsible for neither. So go away graciously, for the one who dismisses you is gracious too.
