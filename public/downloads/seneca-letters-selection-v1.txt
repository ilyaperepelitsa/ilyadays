SENECA, MORAL LETTERS TO LUCILIUS: A SELECTION
A new English translation from the Latin of the sections the Stoic Constitution quotes (seneca-v1)

Made 2026-11-23. Every section of Seneca’s Moral Letters that the Stoic Constitution quotes: 304 sections from 94 letters. Each was translated from the Latin alone by one model, checked against the Latin by a second, and each point was settled in writing by a third reading. None has been read by the owner or checked by a person who reads Latin.
How it was made:
- A first translator (Claude Fable 5.2) worked from the Latin alone, with a short glossary that keeps shared Stoic terms in the same English as the project’s Marcus, and wrote notes where the Latin is open.
- A second reader saw only the Latin and the English and listed every place where the English says more, less or other than the Latin.
- A third reading settled each point in writing (Accepted, Partly accepted or Declined, with the reason from the Latin) and gave the final English.
- No reader saw any published translation of Seneca. Gummere’s 1917–25 English is kept privately and used only to measure similarity (as the 1634 English is for Marcus); none of its words are published.
- Letter titles are the project’s own, written from each letter’s Latin.
What it cannot promise:
- The Latin is an unattributed older text, not a modern critical edition; doubtful readings are noted where a reader saw them.
- All three readings were made by language models; no person who reads Latin has checked the English.
- Only the quoted sections are translated, so a section may begin in the middle of an argument; its notes say so where it matters.
Source text: The Latin Library, Seneca, Epistulae morales ad Lucilium (https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/sen.html), an older public-domain text. The Latin is not included in this file.
Sections are numbered by letter and section as in the standard editions. Only the quoted sections are translated, so a section may begin in the middle of an argument. The letter titles are the project’s own.

LETTER 1: ON VALUING AND SAVING YOUR TIME

1.2  Who will you give me who sets any price on time, who puts a value on a day, who understands that he is dying every day? For we are deceived in this, that we look ahead to death: a great part of it has already gone by; whatever of our lifetime lies behind us, death holds. So do, my Lucilius, what you write you are doing: take hold of every hour. In this way it will come about that you depend less on tomorrow, if you lay hands on today.



LETTER 4: ON OVERCOMING THE FEAR OF DEATH

4.5  Practise this every day, so that you can leave life with an even mind: life, which many clasp and hold on to in the way that people being swept away by rushing water clasp thorns and jagged edges. Most people are tossed about, wretched, between the fear of death and the torments of life, and they do not want to live, they do not know how to die.



LETTER 5: ON LIVING PHILOSOPHY WITHOUT SHOWING OFF

5.2  Rough dress, an uncut head of hair, a rather neglected beard, hatred declared on silver, a bed laid on the ground, and whatever else seeks attention by a perverse route: avoid them. The very name of philosophy, even if it is handled with restraint, draws enough ill will: what if we begin to withdraw ourselves from the customs of people? Inside, let everything be different; let our outward face fit in with the people.

5.3  Let the toga not gleam, nor be dirty either. Let us not own silver with engraved work of solid gold sunk into it, but let us not think it a sign of frugality to have gone without gold and silver. Let us make it our aim to follow a better life than the crowd, not a contrary one: otherwise we drive from us and turn away the people we want to see reformed. We also bring it about that they want to imitate nothing of ours, for fear that everything has to be imitated.

5.5  Just as it is a mark of luxury to long for refined things, so it is a mark of madness to run from things that are in ordinary use and can be had at no great price. Philosophy demands frugality, not punishment; and there can be a frugality that is not unkempt. This is the mean I favour: let life be balanced between good conduct and the conduct of people in general; let everyone look up to our life, but let them find it recognisable.

5.9  Wild animals run from the dangers they see, and when they have escaped they are free from care: we are tormented both by what is coming and by what is past. Many of our good things do us harm: for memory brings back the torment of fear, and foresight takes it ahead of time. No one is wretched from present things alone. Farewell.



LETTER 6: ON SHARING WISDOM BETWEEN FRIENDS

6.5  So I will send you the books themselves, and so that you do not spend much effort chasing here and there after what will be of use, I will put marks in them, so that you can go straight to the very things I approve of and admire. Yet both the living voice and a shared life will do you more good than a set discourse. You ought to come to the thing itself, on the spot, first because people trust their eyes more than their ears, and then because the road is long by way of precepts, short and effective by way of examples.



LETTER 7: ON AVOIDING THE CROWD

7.7  A single example of luxury or of greed does a great deal of harm: a self-indulgent housemate little by little weakens and softens, a rich neighbour stirs up craving, a spiteful companion rubs his own rust off on a man however spotless and straightforward. What do you think happens to a character on which the attack has been made by the people as a body?

7.8  You are bound either to imitate or to hate. But both are to be avoided: do not become like the bad because they are many, and do not become an enemy to many because they are unlike you. Withdraw into yourself as far as you can; keep company with those who will make you better, and let in those whom you in your turn can make better. This works both ways, and people learn while they teach.

7.11  That man too put it well, whoever he was (for it is disputed who said it): when he was asked what was the aim of such great care over an art that would reach very few, he said, “A few are enough for me, one is enough, none is enough.” Epicurus put this third one excellently, when he was writing to one of the partners in his studies: “I write these things,” he said, “not for many, but for you; for we are a large enough theatre, each for the other.”

7.12  These things, my Lucilius, are to be stored away in your mind, so that you may despise the pleasure that comes from the approval of the greater number. Many praise you: have you any reason to be pleased with yourself, if you are the kind of man whom many understand? Let your good things face inward. Farewell.



LETTER 9: ON SELF-SUFFICIENCY AND WHY THE WISE WANT FRIENDS

9.13  “The wise man is content with himself.” Most people, my dear Lucilius, interpret this wrongly: they push the wise man back on every side and force him inside his own skin. But a distinction must be drawn about what that saying promises and how far. The wise man is content with himself for living happily, not for living, since for living he has use for many things, and for living happily he has use only for a mind that is sound and held high and looks down on fortune.

9.14  I want to point out to you Chrysippus’s distinction as well. He says that the wise man is in want of nothing, and yet has use for many things: “on the other hand there is nothing the fool has use for, since he does not know how to use anything, but he is in want of everything.” The wise man has use for hands and eyes and many things necessary for daily use; he is in want of nothing. For to be in want is a matter of necessity; nothing is necessary to the wise man.

9.15  So, although he is content with himself, he has use for friends. He desires to have as many of them as possible, not in order to live happily, for he will live happily even without friends. The highest good does not look for equipment from outside; it is cultivated at home, it comes wholly from itself. It begins to be subject to fortune if it looks for any part of itself outside the house.

9.22  “What then?” you say. “If that man who is rich to his shame calls himself happy, and that one who is master of many but slave of more, will he become happy by what he himself thinks?” What matters is not what he says but what he thinks, and not what he thinks on one day but what he thinks constantly. But there is no reason for you to fear that so great a thing may come to someone unworthy: what is his own pleases no one but the wise man; all folly suffers from disgust with itself. Farewell.



LETTER 10: ON BEING ALONE WITH YOURSELF

10.5  But, to send this letter with some small gift as is my habit, what I found in Athenodorus is true: “Know that you are released from all desires at the point when you have come to ask god for nothing except what you can ask for openly.” For as things are, how great is the madness of people! They whisper the most shameful prayers to the gods; if anyone brings his ear close, they will fall silent, and what they do not want a human being to know they tell to god. See, then, whether this might not be a wholesome precept to give: live with people just as if god saw, speak with god just as if people heard. Farewell.



LETTER 11: ON BLUSHING AND CHOOSING A ROLE MODEL

11.8  Now the letter calls for its close. Take one, and a useful and wholesome one at that, which I want you to fix in your mind: “We must hold some good man dear and keep him always before our eyes, so that we live just as if he were looking on and do everything as if he saw it.”

11.9  This, my dear Lucilius, is Epicurus’s precept. He gave us a guardian and a minder, and not without reason: a great part of wrongdoing is removed if a witness stands beside those who are about to do wrong. Let the mind have someone to revere, by whose authority it may make even its privacy purer. How fortunate is the man who corrects not only when he is present but even when he is thought of! How fortunate is the man who can revere someone in such a way that he composes and orders himself even at the memory of him! One who can revere someone in this way will soon be someone to be revered.

11.10  So choose Cato. If he seems to you too stiff, choose Laelius, a man of a more relaxed mind. Choose the man whose life and speech and very face, which carries his mind in plain view, have pleased you. Show him to yourself always, either as guardian or as example. Someone is needed, I say, against whom our character may measure itself: you will not straighten what is crooked except against a ruler. Farewell.



LETTER 12: ON OLD AGE AND LIVING EACH DAY FULLY

12.4  I owe this to my country place near the city: my old age showed itself to me wherever I turned. Let us embrace it and love it; it is full of pleasure, if you know how to use it. Fruit is most welcome when it is slipping away; the beauty of boyhood is greatest at its close; those given over to wine are delighted by the last drink, the one that sinks them, the one that puts the finishing touch to drunkenness;

12.8  So every day must be ordered as if it were bringing up the rear of the column, and were completing and filling out life. Pacuvius, who made Syria his own by right of possession, whenever he had made the offerings for the dead to himself, with wine and those funeral feasts, used to be carried from dinner to his bedroom in such a way that, amid the applause of his kept men, this was sung to music: “Life has been lived, life has been lived.”

12.9  There was no day on which he did not carry himself out for burial. What he used to do from a bad conscience let us do from a good one, and as we go to sleep let us say, glad and cheerful: “I have lived, and I have finished the course that Fortune had given.” If god adds a tomorrow, let us receive it gladly. The happiest man, and the untroubled owner of himself, is the one who waits for tomorrow without anxiety; whoever has said “I have lived” rises every day to a gain.



LETTER 13: ON NEEDLESS FEARS ABOUT THE FUTURE

13.2  An athlete who has never been beaten black and blue cannot bring great courage to the contest. The one who has seen his own blood, whose teeth have rattled under a fist, the one who has been tripped and has taken his opponent on the whole of his body, who, thrown down, has not thrown away his spirit, who has got up more defiant every time he has fallen: he goes down to the fight with great hope.

13.5  So some things torment us more than they ought, some torment us before they ought, some torment us when they ought not to torment us at all; we either increase our pain, or anticipate it, or invent it. The first of these, since the matter is in dispute and we have a case formally before the court, should be put off for the present. What I call slight you will insist is very grave; I know that some people laugh under the lash and others groan at a blow of the fist. Later we will see whether those things are strong by their own strength or by our weakness.

13.6  Do this for me: whenever people stand around you trying to persuade you that you are wretched, think not about what you hear but about what you feel, and take counsel with your own power to endure, and be the one to question yourself, you who know your own affairs best: “Why is it that these people are lamenting over me? Why is it that they are alarmed, that they even fear infection from me, as if misfortune could jump across? Is there any evil in that, or is that thing ill spoken of rather than evil?” Be the one to question yourself: “Am I perhaps in torment and grieving without cause, and making an evil of what is not one?”

13.8  “But it is to come.” First look carefully at whether there are sure proofs of an evil to come. For most of the time we are troubled by suspicions, and we are made fools of by rumour, the thing that so often finishes a war, but finishes individuals far more. That is how it is, my Lucilius: we are quick to side with opinion. We do not refute the things that lead us into fear, nor do we search them, but we panic, and turn and run like those whom dust raised by a herd in flight has driven from their camp, or whom some tale spread about with no source has terrified.

13.13  So weigh hope and fear, and whenever everything is uncertain, favour yourself: believe what you prefer. If fear has more votes, lean rather to the other side all the same, and stop disturbing yourself, and keep turning this over in your mind: that the greater part of mortals, though they have nothing evil upon them and nothing evil that is certain to come, seethe and run this way and that. For no one resists himself once he has begun to be pushed, and no one brings his fear down to the truth; no one says “The source is worthless, worthless: he either made it up or believed it.” We give ourselves to the breeze to be carried; we take fright at doubtful things as though they were certain; we do not keep things in proportion: a slight unease at once turns into fear.

13.14  I am ashamed to be talking with you like this, and to be reviving you with such mild remedies. Let someone else say “Perhaps it will not come.” As for you, say: “And what if it does come? We will see which of the two wins. Perhaps it is coming on my side, and that death will bring honour to my life.” Hemlock made Socrates great. Wrest from Cato the sword that championed freedom: you will have taken away a great part of his glory.



LETTER 14: ON CARING FOR THE BODY AND STAYING SAFE

14.1  I admit that it is planted in us to hold our own body dear; I admit that we hold it in our guardianship. I do not deny that it should be indulged; I deny that we should be its slaves. For the man who is a slave to his body, who fears too much for it, who measures everything by it, will be a slave to many.

14.2  This is how we ought to conduct ourselves: not as though we ought to live for the sake of the body, but as though we could not live without the body. Too great a love of it unsettles us with fears, loads us with anxieties, exposes us to insults. What is honourable is cheap to the man to whom his body is too dear. Let it be cared for with the greatest diligence, but in such a way that, when reason demands it, when dignity demands it, when good faith demands it, it must be thrown into the flames.

14.16  Finally, in everything the wise man looks to the intention, not the outcome. The beginnings are in our power; on the result Fortune passes judgment, and I do not allow her a verdict on me. “But she will bring some harassment, some adversity.” A robber does not condemn when he kills.



LETTER 16: ON PHILOSOPHY AS THE GUIDE OF LIFE

16.1  I know that this is clear to you, Lucilius: that no one can live happily, or even tolerably, without the study of wisdom, and that the happy life is brought about by wisdom made complete, but a tolerable life even by wisdom begun. But this, which is clear, must be made firm and fixed deeper by daily practice: there is more work in keeping to what you have resolved than in resolving on what is honourable. One must persevere and add strength by constant study, until what is a good intention becomes a good mind.

16.5  Whichever of these is the case, Lucilius, or even if all of them are, one must do philosophy. Whether the fates bind us by an inexorable law, or god, the arbiter of the universe, has arranged everything, or chance drives and tosses human affairs about without order, philosophy ought to protect us. It will urge us to obey god willingly, and Fortune defiantly; it will teach you to follow god and bear chance.

16.6  But now is not the time to cross over into this discussion: what is ours to decide if providence is in command, or if the chain of the fates drags us along bound, or if the sudden and the unforeseen are the masters. I return now to this: to warn you and urge you not to let the impulse of your mind slip away and grow cold. Keep hold of it and set it firm, so that what is an impulse becomes a settled state of mind.



LETTER 17: ON NOT POSTPONING PHILOSOPHY FOR MONEY

17.5  If you want to have time for your mind, you must either be poor or be like a poor man. Wholesome study cannot be carried on without a concern for frugality; and frugality is voluntary poverty. So away with those excuses: “I do not yet have as much as is enough; if I reach that sum, then I will give myself wholly to philosophy.” And yet nothing should be acquired before this thing that you put off and acquire after everything else; one must begin with this. “I want,” you say, “to acquire something to live on.” At the same time learn to acquire as well: if something forbids you to live well, it does not forbid you to die well.

17.6  There is no reason for poverty to call us back from philosophy, or even destitution. For those who hurry towards this must bear even hunger. Some have borne it in sieges, and what other reward was there for that endurance than not falling into the conqueror’s power? How much greater is this that is promised: unending freedom, and fear of no one, neither man nor god. Should one not come to these things even hungry?



LETTER 18: ON PRACTISING POVERTY DURING THE HOLIDAYS

18.5  But I am so much in favour of testing the firmness of your mind that, following the precept of great men, I shall give a precept to you as well: set some days in between on which, content with the scantiest and cheapest food and with coarse and rough clothing, you say to yourself, “Is this what was feared?”

18.7  There is no reason now for you to think that I mean dinners in the style of Timon and poor men’s rooms and whatever else there is by which luxury plays, out of boredom with riches: let that pallet be a real one, and the rough cloak, and the bread hard and coarse. Bear this for three or four days, sometimes for more days, so that it is not play but a test: then, believe me, Lucilius, you will leap for joy, filled for two coppers, and you will understand that for freedom from care there is no need of Fortune; for this, which is enough for necessity, she will give even when angry.

18.15  This passion flares up against all persons; it is born of love as much as of hatred, no less in serious matters than in play and jokes; and what matters is not how great the cause it is born from but what kind of mind it reaches. So with fire: it does not matter how great it is but what it falls on; for solid things do not take even the greatest fire, while things that are dry and catch fire easily nurse even a spark all the way to a blaze. So it is, my Lucilius: the outcome of enormous anger is madness, and therefore anger is to be avoided not for the sake of moderation but for the sake of sanity. Farewell.



LETTER 20: ON MATCHING DEEDS TO WORDS

20.2  Those who declaim and angle for the approval of a ring of listeners have one purpose, those who hold the ears of young men and men of leisure with varied or rapid discussion have another: philosophy teaches doing, not saying, and it demands this, that each person live by his own law, that his life not be at odds with his speech, nor his life itself at odds with itself; let all actions be of one colour. This is both the greatest duty of wisdom and its greatest sign: that deeds agree with words, that the man himself be everywhere consistent with himself and one and the same. “Who will achieve this?” Few, but some all the same. For it is difficult; and I am not saying that the wise man will always go at one pace, but that he will go by one road.

20.9  Begrudge it if you like: even now Epicurus will gladly pay on my behalf. “Your talk, believe me, will seem grander on a pallet and in rags; for those things will not only be said but proved.” I at least hear in a different way what our Demetrius says when I have seen him naked, lying on something far less than straw bedding: he is not a teacher of the truth but a witness to it.



LETTER 21: ON LASTING FAME THROUGH PHILOSOPHY

21.5  A fathomless depth of time will come over us; few talents will lift their heads out and, though bound to go off some day into the same silence, will hold out against oblivion and will long assert their freedom. What Epicurus was able to promise his friend, this I promise you, Lucilius: I shall have favour with those who come after; I can bring out with me names that will last. Our Vergil promised everlasting memory to two men, and he is making it good: “Fortunate pair! If my songs have any power, no day shall ever take you out of time that remembers, so long as the house of Aeneas dwells beside the immovable rock of the Capitol and the Roman father holds the empire.”



LETTER 22: ON WITHDRAWING FROM A BUSY CAREER

22.15  “No one,” he says, “goes out of life otherwise than as he was born.” That is false: we die worse than we are born. That fault is ours, not nature’s. She ought to remonstrate with us and say: “What is this? I gave birth to you without desires, without fears, without superstition, without treachery and the other plagues: go out such as you came in.”



LETTER 23: ON FINDING TRUE JOY WITHIN

23.3  Do this before all else, my Lucilius: learn to feel joy. Do you now think that I am taking many pleasures away from you, I who clear away the things of chance, who think that hopes, the sweetest of delights, are to be avoided? No, on the contrary: I do not want gladness ever to be lacking to you. I want it to be born for you at home: it is born if only it comes about within yourself. The other kinds of cheerfulness do not fill the breast; they smooth the brow, they are slight, unless perhaps you judge that a man who laughs is feeling joy: the mind ought to be lively and confident and raised up above all things.

23.4  Believe me, true joy is a stern thing. Or do you think that anyone with a relaxed and, as those pampered people say, “cheery little” face despises death, opens his house to poverty, holds pleasures under the bridle, rehearses the endurance of pains? The man who turns these things over within himself is in great joy, but a joy with little charm. It is this joy that I want you to be in possession of: it will never fail, once you have found where it is to be got from.

23.6  Do, I beg you, dearest Lucilius, the one thing that can make a person happy: scatter and trample those things that shine on the outside, that are promised to you by someone else or from something else; look to the true good and take your joy from what is your own. But what is this “from what is your own”? From yourself, and from the best part of you. As for the little body too, even though nothing can be done without it, believe it to be a necessary thing rather than a great one; it supplies empty pleasures, short ones, ones to be regretted and, unless they are tempered with great moderation, bound to pass into their opposite. This is what I say: pleasure is on a steep slope; it tilts towards pain unless it has kept within bounds; but to keep within bounds is difficult in a thing that you have believed to be good: greed for the true good is safe.

23.7  Do you ask what that is, or where it comes up from? I will tell you: from a good conscience, from honourable plans, from right actions, from contempt for the things of chance, from the peaceful and unbroken course of a life that keeps to one road. For those who leap from one set of purposes to another, or do not even leap but are carried across by some accident, how can they have anything certain or lasting, when they are suspended and wandering?

23.11  There is no reason for you to think that these are few: they are nearly everyone. Some, indeed, begin at the time when they must stop. If you judge this a wonder, I will add something for you to wonder at more: some have stopped living before they began. Farewell.



LETTER 24: ON FACING FEARED TROUBLES AND DEATH

24.12  So, free from care, listen to your enemy’s threats; and although your own conscience gives you confidence, still, because many things outside the case carry weight, hope for what is fairest and also prepare yourself for what is most unfair. But above all remember this, to take the uproar away from things and to see what is in each thing: you will find that there is nothing terrible in those things except the fear itself.

24.13  What you see happen to children happens to us too, who are slightly bigger children: the people they love, the people they are used to, the people they play with, if they see them wearing masks, they take fright. The mask must be taken off not only people but things, and their own face given back to them.

24.14  Why do you show me swords and fires and a crowd of executioners roaring around you? Take away that parade you hide behind and frighten fools with: you are death, which a slave of mine recently scorned, which a slave girl scorned. And you in turn, why do you lay out whips and racks before me with great display? Why the separate devices fitted to separate joints to wrench them out, and a thousand other instruments for torturing a man piece by piece? Put away those things that stun us; order the groans to fall silent, and the cries, and the harshness of voices forced out while the body is being torn. You are pain, after all, which that man with gout scorns, which that man with a bad stomach endures in the very midst of his luxuries, which a girl endures to the end in childbirth. You are light if I can bear you; you are short if I cannot bear you.

24.15  Turn over in your mind these things, which you have often heard and often said; but whether you have truly heard them, whether you have truly said them, prove in action. For this charge that is commonly thrown at us is a most shameful one: that we handle the words of philosophy, not its deeds. What? Have you learned now for the first time that death hangs over you, now exile, now pain? You were born to these things. Whatever can happen, let us think of it as going to happen.

24.17  I will become poor: I will be among the greater number. I will become an exile: I will think myself born in the place I am sent to. I will be bound: what of it? Am I unbound now? Nature has bound me fast to this heavy weight of my body. I will die: what you are saying is this, I will cease to be able to be ill, I will cease to be able to be bound, I will cease to be able to die.

24.18  I am not so silly as to go through the Epicurean refrain at this point and say that the fears of the underworld are empty, that Ixion is not turned on a wheel, that no rock is pushed uphill by the shoulders of Sisyphus, that no one’s entrails can both grow back and be torn at every day. No one is so much a child as to fear Cerberus and the darkness and the ghostly look of those whose bare bones hang together. Death either consumes us or strips us bare. For those who are let out, better things remain, with the burden taken off; for those who are consumed, nothing remains: good things and bad alike have been removed.

24.20  We die every day; for every day some part of life is taken away, and even at the time when we are growing, life is growing less. We have lost infancy, then childhood, then youth. Right up to yesterday, whatever time has passed has perished; this very day that we are living we share with death. Just as it is not the last drop that empties a water clock but whatever has run out before, so the last hour, in which we cease to be, does not alone make death but alone completes it. That is when we come to it, but we have been coming for a long time.

24.22  I see what you are looking for: you are asking what I have packed into this letter, what spirited saying of someone’s, what useful precept. Something will be sent from this very subject, the one that has been in hand. Epicurus rebukes those who long for death no less than those who fear it, and says: “It is ridiculous to run to death out of weariness of life, when it is by your way of life that you have made it necessary to run to death.”

24.24  Whichever of these you handle, you will strengthen your mind to endure either death or life; for we need to be warned and strengthened in both directions, both not to love life too much and not to hate it too much. Even when reason advises making an end of oneself, the leap is not to be taken rashly or at a run.

24.25  A brave and wise man ought not to flee from life but to go out of it. And above all, that passion too should be avoided which has taken hold of many: the lust for dying. For there is, my Lucilius, as towards other things, so also towards dying, an unconsidered leaning of the mind, which often seizes men who are noble and of the keenest character, and often the spiritless and the downcast. The former scorn life; the latter find it a burden.

24.26  Some people are overcome by having had their fill of doing and seeing the same things, and by not hatred of life but distaste for it. We slide into this with philosophy itself pushing us, while we say: “How long the same things? Of course I will wake, I will sleep, I will be hungry, I will be cold, I will be hot. Nothing has an end, but all things are linked in a circle; they flee and they follow. Night presses on day, day on night; summer ends in autumn, winter bears down on autumn, and is checked by spring. All things pass in such a way that they come back. I do nothing new, I see nothing new: at some point there comes nausea at this too.” There are many who judge living to be not bitter but superfluous. Farewell.



LETTER 25: ON REFORMING FRIENDS AND IMAGINING A WATCHER

25.5  “Do everything,” he says, “as though Epicurus were watching.” It is without doubt useful to have set a guard over oneself and to have someone you look to, someone you judge to be present at your thoughts. This is indeed far grander, to live as though under the eyes of some good man who is always present; but I am content even with this, that you do whatever you do as though someone were watching. Solitude persuades us to every evil.

25.6  When you have made so much progress that you have respect even for yourself, you may dismiss your minder. Meanwhile guard yourself by the authority of certain men: let it be the famous Cato, or Scipio, or Laelius, or another at whose coming even depraved men would suppress their vices, while you are making yourself the man in whose company you would not dare to do wrong. When you have made yourself that, and have begun to have some standing in your own eyes, I will begin to allow you what the same Epicurus advises: “Withdraw into yourself most of all at the time when you are forced to be in a crowd.”

25.7  You need to become unlike the many, until it is safe for you to withdraw to yourself. Look around at people one by one: there is no one for whom it is not better to be with anyone at all than with himself. “Withdraw into yourself most of all at the time when you are forced to be in a crowd”: if you are a good man, if you are untroubled, if you are self-controlled. Otherwise you must withdraw from yourself into the crowd: there, with yourself, you are closer to a bad man. Farewell.



LETTER 26: ON OLD AGE AND DEATH AS THE TEST

26.2  Still, I thank myself in your presence: I do not feel the injury of age in my mind, though I feel it in my body. Only the vices and what waits on the vices have grown old: the mind is vigorous and is glad that it has not much to do with the body; it has laid down a great part of its burden. It exults and disputes with me about old age: it says that this is its flowering. Let us believe it: let it make use of its own good.

26.5  “What we have shown so far, whether in deeds or in words,” I say, “is nothing. Those are slight and deceptive guarantees of what the mind is, and wrapped in much flattering ornament. As to what progress I have made, I shall take death’s word. So it is not timidly that I am readying myself for that day on which, with the tricks and the paint set aside, I shall pass judgment on myself: whether I say brave things or mean them, whether all the defiant words I have flung at fortune were a pretence and a piece of acting.

26.6  Set aside the estimate people make: it is always uncertain and is split both ways. Set aside the studies you have pursued all your life: death is going to pronounce on you. This is what I say: debates, literary conversations, words collected from the teachings of the wise, and learned talk do not show the true strength of the mind; for even the most timid have bold speech. What you have done will be seen at the moment when you breathe your last. I accept the terms; I do not shrink from the judgment.”



LETTER 28: ON TRAVEL AS NO CURE FOR THE MIND

28.1  Do you think this has happened to you alone, and do you wonder at it as something new, that by so long a journey and so great a variety of places you have not shaken off the sadness and heaviness of your mind? It is your mind you must change, not your sky. Though you have crossed the vast sea, though, as our Vergil says, “lands and cities fall away,” your vices will follow you wherever you arrive.

28.2  To someone who made this same complaint Socrates said: “Why do you wonder that your journeys do you no good, when it is yourself you carry around? The same cause that drove you out is pressing on you.” What help can the newness of lands be? What help is getting to know cities or places? That tossing about of yours comes to nothing. Do you ask why that flight of yours does not help you? You flee with yourself. The burden of the mind must be laid down: until then no place will please you.

28.4  But once you have removed that ill, every change of place will become pleasant. Though you are driven out to the farthest lands, though you are set down in whatever corner of barbarian country, that dwelling, whatever it is like, will be hospitable to you. Who you are when you come matters more than where you come to, and for that reason we ought not to sign our mind over to any place. One must live with this conviction: “I was not born for one corner; this whole world is my country.”

28.6  Can anything be as turbulent as the forum? Even there one may live quietly, if it should be necessary. But if one might place oneself, I would flee far from even the sight and the neighbourhood of the forum; for just as oppressive places test even the strongest health, so for a good mind too, one that is not yet perfected and is recovering, there are some places that are not wholesome enough.



LETTER 29: ON GIVING ADVICE ONLY TO THE WILLING

29.1  You ask about our Marcellinus and want to know how he is doing. He seldom comes to us, for no other reason than that he is afraid to hear the truth; and that is a danger he is now clear of, for it should be spoken to no one unless he is going to hear it. That is why, about Diogenes and no less about the other Cynics who spoke freely to all alike and admonished whoever came their way, it is commonly doubted whether they ought to have done this. For what if someone were to rebuke the deaf, or those who are mute by nature or by illness?

29.3  This, my Lucilius, I do not think a great man should do: his authority is watered down and does not have enough weight with those whom it could set right if it were less cheapened. An archer ought not to hit now and then, but to miss now and then; that is not an art which comes to its result by chance. Wisdom is an art: let it aim at what is sure, let it choose those who will make progress, let it draw back from those of whom it has despaired, yet let it not abandon them quickly, and in despair itself let it try remedies of last resort.

29.11  “Who said this?” you ask, as if you did not know whom I order to pay. Epicurus. But all of them, from every house, will shout this same thing to you together: Peripatetics, Academics, Stoics, Cynics. For who can please the people if virtue pleases him? The favour of the people is sought by bad arts. You must make yourself like those people: they will not approve of you unless they recognise you. But what you seem to yourself matters far more than what you seem to others; the love of base people cannot be won except by base means.

29.12  What, then, will that philosophy deliver, which is praised and is to be preferred to all arts and all things? This, of course: that you would rather please yourself than the people, that you assess judgments and do not count them, that you live without fear of gods and men, that you either overcome evils or end them. But if I see you celebrated by the approving voices of the crowd, if when you come in there is a din of shouting and applause, the honours of a pantomime dancer, if throughout the whole city women and children praise you, why should I not pity you, since I know what road leads to that favour? Farewell.



LETTER 30: ON AN OLD MAN'S CALM BEFORE DEATH

30.4  This is a great thing, Lucilius, and one that has to be learned over a long time: to go away with an even mind when that hour which cannot be avoided draws near. Other kinds of death are mixed with hope: an illness comes to an end, a fire is put out, a falling building has set down the people it seemed about to crush; the sea has thrown out unharmed those it had swallowed, with the same force with which it was sucking them down; a soldier has drawn his sword back from the very neck of the man who was about to die. The man whom old age is leading to death has nothing to hope for; against this alone no one can step in. By no kind of death do people die more gently, but by none more slowly either.

30.10  You may count Bassus among these, a man who did not want us to be deceived. He says that a man who fears death is as much a fool as one who fears old age; for just as old age follows youth, so death follows old age. Whoever does not want to die did not want to live; for life was given with death as its proviso; it is towards death that one goes. To fear it is what a madman does, for this reason: what is certain is waited for, what is doubtful is feared.

30.11  Death has a necessity that is fair and cannot be overcome: who can complain of being in a condition that no one is not in? And the first part of fairness is equality. But at this point it is needless to plead the case of nature, who wanted our law to be no other than her own: whatever she has put together she takes apart, and whatever she has taken apart she puts together again.

30.12  And further, if it has been anyone’s lot that old age should let him go gently, not torn suddenly from life but drawn away little by little, oh, that man truly ought to give thanks to all the gods that, having had his fill, he has been brought through to a rest that is necessary for a human being and welcome to a tired one. You see some people wishing for death, and indeed more than life is usually begged for. I do not know which of the two I should think give us more courage, those who demand death or those who wait for it cheerful and quiet, since the first sometimes comes from frenzy and sudden indignation, while this calm comes from a settled judgment. Someone has come to death angry: no one has received death cheerfully when it came, except a man who had long made himself ready for it.

30.16  He used to say that this torment at least we feel by our own doing: that we tremble just when we believe death is near us. For who is there that it is not near, ready as it is in every place and at every moment? “But let us consider,” he said, “just when some cause of dying seems to be approaching, how much nearer other causes are that are not feared.”



LETTER 31: ON TRUSTING YOURSELF AND SCORNING POPULAR GOODS

31.5  Hard work feeds noble minds. So there is no reason for you to pick out, from that old prayer of your parents, what you would want to come to you, what you would wish for; and altogether, for a man who has already passed through the greatest things, it is shameful still to be wearying the gods. What need is there of prayers? Make yourself fortunate, you yourself; and you will, if you understand that those things are good which have virtue mixed into them, and those shameful which have wickedness joined to them. Just as nothing is bright without a mixture of light, and nothing is black unless it has darkness in it or has drawn something dark into itself; just as nothing is hot without the help of fire, and nothing cold without air; so it is partnership with virtue and with wickedness that makes things honourable and shameful.



LETTER 32: ON COMPLETING LIFE BEFORE DEATH

32.3  So hurry, dearest Lucilius, and think how much you would add to your speed if an enemy were bearing down on you from behind, if you suspected that cavalry were coming up and pressing on the tracks of those in flight. This is happening: you are being pressed. Speed up and get away, bring yourself through to safety, and consider again and again what a fine thing it is to complete one’s life before death, and then to wait free from care for the remaining part of one’s time, waiting for nothing for oneself, settled in possession of the happy life, which does not become happier if it is longer.

32.5  I wish for you to have yourself at your own disposal: that your mind, driven about by wandering thoughts, may at last come to a stand and be settled; that it may be content with itself and, once it has understood the true goods, which are possessed the moment they are understood, may have no need of added years. That man alone has got beyond necessities, and is discharged from service and free, who lives with his life completed.



LETTER 35: ON STEADINESS AND BECOMING A TRUE FRIEND

35.4  Hurry to me, but to yourself first. Make progress, and before all else take care of this: that you be consistent with yourself. Whenever you want to test whether anything has been achieved, watch whether you want the same things today as yesterday: a change in what one wants shows that the mind is adrift, turning up now in one place and now in another, as the wind has carried it. What is fixed and set on a foundation does not wander: that falls to the perfect wise man, and to some extent also to the one who is making progress and has advanced. What then is the difference? The latter is indeed shaken, yet he does not shift his ground, but sways in his own place; the former is not even shaken. Farewell.



LETTER 36: ON THE BENEFITS OF A QUIET LIFE

36.10  But if so great a desire for a longer life has hold of you, consider that none of the things that go out of sight and are put back into the nature of things, from which they came out and will soon come forth, is used up: these things stop, they do not perish, and death, which we dread and refuse, interrupts life, it does not snatch it away; a day will come again to put us back into the light, a day that many would refuse if it did not bring them back with their memory gone.

36.11  But later I will show more carefully that all the things that seem to perish are changed. A man who is going to come back ought to go out with an even mind. Watch the circle of things as they return upon themselves: you will see that nothing in this world is extinguished, but that things go down and rise by turns. Summer has gone, but another year will bring it; winter has fallen away, its own months will bring it back; night has buried the sun, but day will at once drive night itself away. That running about of the stars comes back to whatever it has passed; one part of the sky is continually being lifted, one part is being sunk.



LETTER 37: ON THE OATH TO PHILOSOPHY AND REASON

37.5  You will not show me anyone who knows how he began to want what he wants: he was not led to it by deliberation but flung into it by impulse. Fortune collides with us no less often than we with it. It is shameful not to go but to be carried, and suddenly, in the middle of the whirlwind of things, to ask in a daze, “How did I get here?” Farewell.



LETTER 41: ON THE GOD WITHIN US

41.1  You are doing a very good thing, and one that is healthy for you, if, as you write, you keep on making your way towards a sound mind. It is foolish to pray for this when you can obtain it from yourself. There is no need to lift hands to heaven, or to beg the temple-keeper to admit us to the ear of the statue, as if we could be heard better: god is near you, he is with you, he is within.

41.2  This is what I say, Lucilius: a sacred spirit sits within us, the observer and guardian of the bad and the good that are ours. As it has been treated by us, so it treats us in its turn. And indeed no one is a good man without god: or can anyone rise above fortune unless helped by him? It is he who gives advice that is grand and lofty. In each and every good man (which god is uncertain) a god lives.

41.8  Praise in him what can neither be snatched away nor given, what is man’s own. You ask what that is? The mind, and reason made perfect in the mind. For man is a reasoning animal; and so his good is brought to completion if he has fulfilled what he is born for.

41.9  And what is it that this reason demands of him? A very easy thing: to live according to his own nature. But the madness shared by all makes it hard: we push one another into vices. And how can people be called back to health when no one holds them back and the crowd drives them on? Farewell.



LETTER 44: ON TRUE NOBILITY COMING FROM THE MIND

44.3  Socrates was not a patrician. Cleanthes drew water and hired out his hands for watering a garden. Philosophy did not receive Plato as a noble; it made him one. What reason is there for you to despair of being able to become the equal of these men? All of these are your ancestors, if you conduct yourself in a way worthy of them; and you will, if you persuade yourself of this at once: that you are surpassed by no one in nobility.

44.5  Who is well born? The man who is well made by nature for virtue. This is the one thing to look at. Otherwise, if you go back to ancient times, there is no one who does not come from that point before which there is nothing. From the first beginning of the world down to this time we have been led all the way by a line in which the splendid and the lowly alternate. A hall full of smoke-darkened images of ancestors does not make a man noble. No one has lived for our glory, and what was before us is not ours. It is the mind that makes a man noble, and the mind may rise above fortune from any station whatever.



LETTER 47: ON TREATING SLAVES AS FELLOW HUMANS

47.17  “He is a slave.” But perhaps free in mind. “He is a slave.” Will that harm him? Show who is not one: one man is a slave to lust, another to greed, another to ambition, [gap in the Latin] all to fear. I will give you a man of consular rank who is a slave to a little old woman, I will give you a rich man who is a slave to a slave girl, I will show you young men of the noblest birth who are the bought slaves of pantomime dancers: no slavery is more shameful than voluntary slavery. So there is no reason why those disdainful people should deter you from showing yourself cheerful to your slaves, and superior without being arrogant: let them honour you rather than fear you.



LETTER 48: ON LOGIC PUZZLES VERSUS REAL HELP

48.2  Am I talking like an Epicurean again? But in fact the same thing is in my interest as is in yours: or else I am no friend, if whatever matter concerns you is not mine. Friendship makes between us a partnership in all things. Nothing favourable and nothing adverse belongs to one alone; life is lived in common. And no one can live happily who looks only at himself, who turns everything to his own advantage: you must live for another, if you want to live for yourself.

48.9  Say what nature has made necessary and what superfluous, how easy the [laws] are that it has laid down, how pleasant life is, how unencumbered, for those who follow them, how bitter and entangled is the life of those who have trusted opinion more than nature [gap in the Latin] if you have first shown what part of them they are going to relieve. Which of those things takes away desires? Which moderates them? I wish it were only that they do no good! They do harm. Whenever you like I will make this perfectly clear to you: that a well-born character is crushed and weakened when it is thrown into those quibbles.



LETTER 49: ON THE SHORTNESS OF LIFE AND WASTED CLEVERNESS

49.10  Teach me something against these things. Bring it about that I do not flee from death, and that life does not escape me. Encourage me against what is hard, against what cannot be avoided; loosen the narrow limits of my time. Teach me that the good of life lies not in its length but in its use; that it can happen, indeed very often does happen, that a man who has lived long has lived too little. Say to me when I am about to sleep, “You might not wake up.” Say to me when I have woken, “You might not sleep again.” Say to me as I go out, “You might not come back.” Say to me as I come back, “You might not go out.”



LETTER 51: ON LUXURIOUS RESORTS AND SOFT LIVING

51.4  We ought to choose a place that is healthy not only for the body but also for our character. Just as I would not want to live among torturers, so I would not want to live among taverns either. To see drunk people wandering along the shores, and the revels of people out sailing, and the lakes noisy with the music of bands, and the other offences that self-indulgence, as if set free from the laws, not only commits but makes public: what need is there of that?

51.9  Freedom is what has been set as the goal; it is for this prize that the toil is done. You ask what freedom is? To be a slave to no thing, to no necessity, to no accidents; to bring Fortune down to level ground. On the day I understand that she has more power, she will have no power: am I to put up with her, when death is in my hand?



LETTER 52: ON CHOOSING GUIDES AND APPLAUDING PHILOSOPHERS

52.2  “It is folly,” you say, “and for folly nothing holds firm, nothing pleases for long.” But how, or when, shall we tear ourselves away from it? No one is strong enough by himself to come up to the surface; someone must stretch out a hand, someone must draw him out.

52.3  Epicurus says that some people have come out to the truth without anyone’s help, that they themselves have made their own road; these he praises most, the ones whose drive came from themselves, who brought themselves forward. Others, he says, need another’s aid: they will not go if no one has gone ahead, but they will follow well. Metrodorus, he says, is one of these; this too is an outstanding talent, but of the second rank. We are not of that first grade; we are well treated if we are admitted to the second. Do not look down even on the man who can be safe through another’s good service; this too is a great deal, to want to be saved.

52.9  Nor would I, on that account, forbid you to listen also to those whose habit it is to admit the public and lecture, provided that they come out before the crowd with this purpose, to become better and to make others better, if they do not practise this to court popularity. For what is more shameful than philosophy hunting for shouts? Surely a sick man does not praise the doctor who is cutting him?

52.11  And how great is the madness of the man whom the shouts of the ignorant send away from the lecture hall cheerful! Why are you glad to have been praised by these people, whom you cannot yourself praise? Fabianus used to lecture to the public, but he was listened to with restraint; from time to time a great shout of praise would break out, but one that the greatness of what he was saying had called forth, not the sound of speech that had glided by without a stumble and softly.

52.12  Let there be some difference between the shouting of the theatre and that of the school: there is a certain good taste in praising too. Everything is a sign of everything, if it is watched, and one may take evidence of character even from the smallest things: the lewd man is shown by his walk, by a movement of his hand, sometimes by a single answer, by a finger brought to his head, by the turn of his eyes; the wicked man is given away by his laugh, the madman by his face and bearing. For those traits come out into the open through their marks: you will know what each person is like if you look at how he praises and how he is praised.



LETTER 53: ON HIDDEN FAULTS AND GIVING ALL TO PHILOSOPHY

53.8  Why does no one confess his vices? Because he is still in them: to tell a dream is for a man who is awake, and to confess one’s vices is a sign of health. Let us wake up, then, so that we can expose our errors. But only philosophy will rouse us, only philosophy will shake off the heavy sleep: dedicate yourself wholly to her. You are worthy of her, she is worthy of you: go into each other’s embrace. Refuse yourself to all other things, bravely, openly; there is no reason for you to do philosophy by someone else’s leave.

53.9  If you were ill, you would have set aside the care of your property, and your business in the forum would have dropped out of your mind, and you would not think anyone worth so much that you would go down as his advocate during a remission; with your whole mind you would work at this, to be freed from the illness as soon as possible. Well then? Will you not do the same now too? Let go of everything that hinders you and make yourself free for a sound mind: no one reaches it while he is busy. Philosophy exercises her own royal power; she gives out time, she does not accept it; she is not a thing for leftover hours; she is a thing for the regular hours, she is the mistress, she is present and gives orders.



LETTER 54: ON ASTHMA AND REHEARSING DEATH

54.5  I ask you, would you not call it most foolish if someone thought that a lamp is worse off when it has been put out than before it is lit? We too are both put out and lit: in the time between we go through something, but on either side there is deep freedom from care. For this, my Lucilius, unless I am mistaken, is where we go wrong: we judge that death follows, when it has both gone before and will follow. Whatever was before us is death; for what does it matter whether you do not begin or you cease, when the result of both is this, not to be?

54.7  Be assured of this about me: I shall not tremble at the end, I am already prepared, I give no thought to a whole day. As for you, praise and imitate the man who is not sorry to die though he is glad to live: for what virtue is there in going out when you are being thrown out? Yet there is virtue here too: I am indeed being thrown out, but as though I were going out. And the reason the wise man is never thrown out is that to be thrown out is to be driven from a place you leave unwillingly: the wise man does nothing unwillingly; he escapes necessity, because he wants what it is going to force on him. Farewell.



LETTER 55: ON HIDING VERSUS TRULY LIVING IN RETIREMENT

55.5  For the man who has run away from affairs and from people, whom the ill success of his own desires has banished, who could not bear to see others more successful, who has gone into hiding out of fear like a timid and sluggish animal: that man does not live for himself but, which is the most shameful thing, for his belly, for sleep, for lust; a man who lives for no one does not, just by that, live for himself. Yet steadiness, and persistence in one’s purpose, is so great a thing that even stubborn sluggishness carries weight.

55.8  But place does not contribute much to calm: it is the mind that makes everything agreeable to itself. I have seen people gloomy in a cheerful and pleasant country house, I have seen people in the middle of solitude who were like busy men. So there is no reason for you to think that you are not well enough composed because you are not in Campania. But why are you not? Send your thoughts all the way here.



LETTER 56: ON NOISE AND INNER QUIET

56.5  Besides, a sound that keeps breaking off is more of a nuisance to me than one that goes on without a break. But by now I have so hardened myself against all these things that I can listen even to a rowing-master giving the oarsmen their time in the harshest of voices. For I force my mind to be intent on itself and not to be called away to things outside. Let everything resound outside, so long as there is no uproar inside, so long as desire and fear do not brawl with each other, so long as greed and extravagance are not at odds and neither troubles the other. For what good is the silence of a whole district, if the passions are roaring?

56.12  It is a lightweight character, and one that has not yet withdrawn into itself, that starts up at a voice and at whatever happens. It has something of anxiety inside, and it has something of a dread that has formed in it, which makes the man watchful, as our Vergil says: “And I, who a little while ago was moved by no weapons hurled at me, nor by Greeks crowding out of the opposing ranks, am now frightened by every breeze; every sound startles me, on edge and afraid alike for my companion and for my burden.”

56.14  Whichever you pick of those fortunate people, who drag much along and carry much, you will see him “fearing for both his companion and his load”. Know, then, that you are settled at the point when no shouting concerns you, when no voice shakes you out of yourself, not if it coaxes, not if it threatens, not if it is an empty voice making a din all round you with hollow sound.

56.15  “What then? Is it not sometimes more comfortable to be without the racket as well?” I admit it; and so I shall move out of this place. I wanted to test myself and train myself. What need is there to be tormented any longer, when Ulysses found so easy a remedy for his comrades even against the Sirens? Farewell.



LETTER 57: ON INSTINCTIVE FEAR IN A DARK TUNNEL

57.3  Still, that darkness gave me something to think about: I felt a kind of blow to the mind, and a change, without fear, which the newness and at the same time the foulness of an unfamiliar thing had brought about. I am not speaking to you now about myself, who am a long way from a tolerable man, let alone a perfect one, but about the man over whom Fortune has lost her authority: his mind too will be struck, his colour will change.

57.4  For there are some things, my Lucilius, that no virtue can escape; nature reminds virtue of its own mortality. And so he will tighten his face at grim things, and shudder at sudden things, and his sight will go dim if he stands on the brink of a vast depth and looks down into it. This is not fear, but a natural feeling that reason cannot take by storm.



LETTER 58: ON BEING, PLATO'S IDEAS AND OLD AGE

58.22  The fifth kind is of the things that exist in the common way; these begin to concern us; here are all things: people, cattle, objects. The sixth kind is of the things that, so to speak, exist, such as the void, such as time. Whatever we see or touch, Plato does not count among the things that he thinks exist in the proper sense; for they flow, and are in constant loss and gain. None of us is the same in old age as he was when young; none of us is the same in the morning as he was the day before. Our bodies are swept along in the manner of rivers. Whatever you see runs with time; nothing of the things we see remains; I myself, while I say that these things are changing, have changed.

58.23  This is what Heraclitus says: “Into the same river we go down twice and do not go down.” For the name of the river remains the same; the water has passed on. This is plainer in a river than in a human being; but a course no less swift carries us past too, and so I wonder at our madness, that we love so much a thing so quick to flee, the body, and fear that some day we may die, when every moment is the death of the state before it. Come, do not fear that what happens every day may happen once!

58.24  I have spoken of man, matter that is flowing and perishable and at the mercy of every cause. The world too, an eternal and unconquerable thing, changes and does not remain the same. For although it has in itself all that it had, it has it otherwise than it had it: it changes its order.

58.28  For all things remain, not because they are eternal, but because they are defended by the care of the one who governs them: immortal things would need no guardian. These things the craftsman preserves, overcoming the frailty of matter by his own power. Let us despise all the things that are so far from precious that it is doubtful whether they exist at all.

58.35  Do not listen to me unwillingly, as though this pronouncement already applied to you; and weigh what I am saying. I shall not leave old age, if it keeps the whole of me for myself, the whole, that is, as regards that better part. But if it begins to shake my mind, if it begins to tear its parts loose, if it leaves me not life but breath, I shall leap out of a building that is rotten and falling down.



LETTER 59: ON PLEASURE, TRUE JOY AND SELF-FLATTERY

59.14  I shall now show you how you may understand that you are not a wise man. The wise man is full of joy, cheerful and peaceful, unshaken; he lives with the gods as an equal. Now consult yourself: if you are never sad, if no hope makes your mind anxious with expectation of the future, if by day and by night the course of your mind, lifted up and pleased with itself, is even and level, you have reached the height of human good. But if you go after pleasures, from everywhere and all of them, know that you fall as far short of wisdom as you do of joy. This is what you long to reach, but you go wrong in hoping that you will get there among riches, among honours, that is, in looking for joy among anxieties: those things, which you go after as though they would give gladness and pleasure, are causes of pain.



LETTER 60: ON HARMFUL PRAYERS AND HUMAN GREED

60.4  So let us count these people, who are, as Sallust says, “obedient to the belly”, as animals, not as human beings, and some of them not even as animals, but as the dead. The one who is of use to many is alive; the one who makes use of himself is alive. But those who lie hidden and inert are in their house as they would be in a tomb. You may carve their name on marble at the very threshold: they have gone ahead of their own death. Farewell.



LETTER 61: ON BEING READY AND WILLING TO DIE

61.1  Let us stop wanting what we have wanted. I at any rate am working at this: that as an old man I should not want the same things I wanted as a boy. To this one thing my days go, to this my nights; this is my work, this my thought: to put an end to old ills. I am working at this, that a day should be to me the equal of a whole life; and, by Hercules, I do not snatch at it as though it were my last, but I look at it as though it could even be my last.

61.2  I write you this letter in this frame of mind: as though death were going to call me away at the very moment of writing. I am ready to go, and I shall enjoy life for this reason, that I do not hang in too much suspense over how long this will last. Before old age I took care to live well; in old age I take care to die well. And to die well is to die willingly.

61.3  Take care that you never do anything unwillingly. Whatever is going to be necessary for one who resists is no necessity for one who wants it. This is what I mean: the man who takes orders willingly escapes the bitterest part of slavery: doing what he does not want to do. It is not the man who does something under orders who is wretched, but the man who does it unwillingly. So let us set our mind in order so that we want whatever the situation demands, and above all so that we think of our own end without sadness.

61.4  We must be prepared for death before we are prepared for life. Life is equipped well enough, but we are greedy for its equipment; something seems to us to be lacking, and always will seem so. That we have lived enough is brought about neither by years nor by days but by the mind. I have lived, dearest Lucilius, as much as was enough; having had my fill, I await death. Farewell.



LETTER 62: ON MAKING TIME FOR STUDY ANYWHERE

62.1  They are lying, those who want it to look as though a crowd of business stands between them and liberal studies: they feign occupations, they make more of them, and they are the ones who keep themselves occupied. I have time, Lucilius, I have time, and wherever I am, there I am my own. For I do not hand myself over to affairs but lend myself to them, and I do not go hunting for reasons to waste time. And in whatever place I have come to a stop, there I work over my own thoughts and turn something wholesome over in my mind.

62.2  When I have given myself to my friends, I still do not take myself away from myself, and I do not linger with those with whom some occasion has thrown me together, or some cause arising from a citizen’s duty, but I am with all the best men. To them, in whatever place, in whatever age they lived, I send my mind.



LETTER 63: ON KEEPING GRIEF FOR A FRIEND WITHIN BOUNDS

63.15  So let us think constantly about our own mortality as much as that of all those we love. I ought to have said then: “My Serenus is younger than I am: what has that to do with it? He ought to die after me, but he can die before me.” Because I did not, Fortune struck me suddenly and unprepared. Now I think this: all things are mortal, and mortal under an uncertain law. Whatever can happen at any time can happen today.



LETTER 65: ON CAUSES AND THE WORTH OF STUDYING NATURE

65.2  Talk took the place of the pen, and from it I shall bring you the part that is in dispute. We have named you as arbiter. You have more work on your hands than you think: the case is threefold. Our Stoics say, as you know, that there are two things in the nature of things from which everything is made: cause and matter. Matter lies inert, a thing ready for everything, which will stay idle if no one moves it. But cause, that is, reason, shapes matter and turns it wherever it wishes, and brings varied works out of it. So there must be that from which a thing is made, and then that by which it is made: the second is cause, the first is matter.

65.10  You ask what god’s purpose is? Goodness. So, at any rate, Plato says: “What cause did god have for making the world? He is good; one who is good begrudges nothing good; so he made it as good as he could.” Give your verdict, then, as judge, and declare who seems to you to say what is most like the truth, not who says what is truest; for that is as far above us as truth itself.

65.12  But what we are asking about now is the first and general cause. This must be simple, for matter too is simple. Are we asking what the cause is? Reason that makes, of course: that is, god. For all those things you people have listed are not many separate causes, but hang from one, from the one that makes.

65.22  Never will this flesh drive me to fear, never to a pretence unworthy of a good man; never will I lie to do honour to this little body. When it seems right, I shall break up my partnership with it. And even now, while we are stuck together, we shall still not be partners on equal shares: the mind will take all the rights for itself. Contempt for one’s own body is sure freedom.

65.24  The place that god holds in this world, the mind holds in a human being; what matter is there, the body is in us. So let the worse serve the better. Let us be brave against the things of chance; let us not tremble at wrongs, at wounds, at chains, at want. What is death? Either an end or a crossing. I am not afraid of ceasing, for it is the same as not having begun; nor of crossing over, because nowhere shall I be so cramped. Farewell.



LETTER 66: ON ALL GOODS BEING EQUAL

66.5  On the first day this was the question asked: how can goods be equal, if their condition is threefold? Some, as our people think, are first goods, such as joy, peace, the safety of one’s country. Some are second goods, shaped in unlucky material, such as endurance of torture and self-control in a serious illness. The former goods we shall wish for ourselves outright, the latter if it proves necessary. There are also third goods, such as a restrained way of walking, a composed and decent expression, and gestures that suit a man of good sense.

66.12  Mortal things dwindle and fall, are worn away and grow, are drained and filled; and so, in a lot so uncertain, there is inequality among them. Divine things have one nature. Now reason is nothing other than a part of the divine breath sunk into a human body. If reason is divine, and no good is without reason, then every good is divine. Further, there is no difference between divine things; so there is none between goods either. So joy and the brave, unyielding endurance of torture are equal; for in both there is the same greatness of mind, in the one slackened and loose, in the other fighting and taut.



LETTER 67: ON WHETHER ENDURING HARDSHIP IS DESIRABLE

67.4  Draw distinctions among these things, my Lucilius, and you will understand that there is something in them to be wished for. I would want torture to be far from me; but if it has to be endured, I shall wish to carry myself in it bravely, honourably, with spirit. Why would I not prefer that war not break out? But if it does break out, I shall wish to bear wounds, to bear hunger and everything that the necessity of war brings, nobly. I am not so mad as to desire to be ill; but if I have to be ill, I shall wish to do nothing without self-control, nothing womanish. So it is not the hardships that are to be wished for, but the virtue by which the hardships are borne.



LETTER 68: ON RETIRING WITHOUT MAKING A SHOW

68.14  “And when,” you say, “will what you are learning on your way out be of use to you, or for what purpose?” For this one: so that I may go out better. But you have no reason to think that any age is better suited to a good mind than the age that has tamed itself by many experiences, by long and frequent regret over what it has done, and has come, with its passions softened, to what heals. This is the time for this good: whoever has reached wisdom as an old man has reached it by his years. Farewell.



LETTER 70: ON CHOOSING THE RIGHT TIME TO DIE

70.3  We think that end is a rock, utter madmen that we are: it is a harbour, sometimes to be steered for, never to be refused, and if anyone has been carried into it within his first years, he should no more complain than a man who has made a quick voyage. For, as you know, sluggish winds toy with one man and hold him back and tire him out with the tedium of a very slow calm, while a persistent breeze carries another through very quickly.

70.4  Consider that the same happens to us: life has brought some very quickly to the place that even those who hang back had to come to, and others it has soaked and stewed. Life, as you know, is not always to be held on to. For it is not living that is a good, but living well. So the wise man will live as long as he ought, not as long as he can.

70.6  To die sooner or later does not matter; to die well or badly does matter. And to die well is to escape the danger of living badly. So I consider most unmanly the saying of that man of Rhodes who, when he had been thrown into a cage by a tyrant and was being fed like some wild animal, said to someone who advised him to refuse food: “A human being should hope for everything while he lives.”



LETTER 71: ON VIRTUE AS THE ONLY GOOD

71.2  Whenever you want to know what is to be avoided or what is to be aimed at, look to the highest good, the purpose set before your whole life. For whatever we do ought to agree with it: no one will put the particulars in order unless he already has the whole of his life set before him. No one, though he has his colours ready, will produce a likeness unless it is already settled what he wants to paint. The reason we go wrong is that we all deliberate about the parts of life, and no one deliberates about the whole.

71.3  The man who wants to shoot an arrow must know what he is aiming at, and then direct and guide the shaft with his hand: our plans go astray because they have nothing to be directed at; no wind is favourable to one who does not know what harbour he is aiming for. Chance is bound to have great power in our life, because we live by chance.

71.7  Socrates, who called the whole of philosophy back to conduct and said that this is the highest wisdom, to distinguish goods from evils, says: “Follow them, if I have any authority with you, so that you may be happy, and let yourself seem a fool to someone. Let anyone who wishes insult you and wrong you; you will still suffer nothing, so long as virtue is with you. If you want to be happy,” he says, “if you want to be truly a good man, let someone despise you.” No one will achieve this unless he has first despised all things himself, unless he has put all goods on one level, because there is no good without what is honourable, and what is honourable is equal in all.

71.13  All things move through fixed periods: they must be born, grow, be extinguished. Whatever you see running above us, and these things on which we lean and have been set as though they were the most solid of things, will be worn away and will cease. There is nothing that does not have its own old age. Nature sends these things off to the same place after unequal spans: whatever is will not be, yet it will not perish but will be dissolved.

71.15  And so, like Marcus Cato, when he has run through the ages in his mind, he will say: “The whole human race, both the part that is and the part that will be, is condemned to death. All the cities that anywhere hold supreme power, and all those that are the great ornaments of empires not their own: one day it will be asked where they were, and they will be done away with by one kind of destruction or another. Wars will pull some down; others will be eaten away by idleness and peace turned to sloth, and by a thing destructive to great wealth, luxury. All these fertile plains will be hidden by a sudden flood of the sea, or carried off into a sudden chasm by the slipping of the ground as it sinks. What reason is there, then, for me to be indignant or to grieve, if I go a brief moment ahead of the common fate?”

71.16  Let a great soul obey god and undergo without hesitation whatever the law of the universe commands. Either it is sent out into a better life, to remain among divine things in greater light and greater calm, or at least it will be without any trouble, if it is mixed back into nature and returns into the whole. So Marcus Cato’s honourable life is not a greater good than his honourable death, since virtue cannot be stretched any further. Socrates used to say that truth and virtue are the same thing. As truth does not grow, so virtue does not either: it has its full complement, it is full.

71.27  I do not take the wise man out of the number of human beings, nor do I move pains away from him as from some rock that lets in no feeling. I remember that he is made up of two parts: one is without reason, and this part is bitten, burned, feels pain; the other has reason, and this part holds unshaken beliefs, is fearless and unconquered. In this part is placed that highest good of a human being. Before that good is brought to fullness, there is an unsure wavering of the mind; but when it has been made complete, the mind has a stability that does not move.

71.28  And so the man who is only begun and is moving toward the heights, one who cultivates virtue, even if he is coming near to the complete good but has not yet put the finishing touch to it, will from time to time go backward and relax something of the exertion of his mind; for he has not yet crossed over the unsure ground and is still on slippery footing. But the happy man, whose virtue is perfected, loves himself most when he has put himself to the test most bravely, and the things the rest fear, if they are the price of some honourable duty, he not only bears but embraces, and he much prefers to hear “so much the better man” than “so much the more fortunate”.

71.29  I come now to the point to which your expectation calls me. So that our virtue may not seem to wander outside the nature of things: the wise man will tremble, and feel pain, and turn pale, for all these are feelings of the body. Where, then, is the disaster, where is that true evil? There, of course: if these things drag the mind down, if they bring it to a confession of slavery, if they make it dissatisfied with itself.

71.30  The wise man, it is true, conquers fortune by virtue, but many who have professed wisdom have at times been terrified by the slightest threats. Here the fault is ours, who demand the same thing of the wise man and of the man who is making progress. I am still urging on myself the things I praise; I have not yet convinced myself. Even if I had convinced myself, I would not yet have them so ready or so practised that they would run out to meet whatever happens.

71.31  Just as wool takes on some colours in one go, and others it does not drink in fully unless it has been soaked and boiled again several times, so there are other branches of learning that intellects make good on at once, as soon as they have received them: this one, unless it has gone down deep and settled for a long time and dyed the mind rather than coloured it, makes good on nothing of what it had promised.

71.35  Things that are incomplete are bound to totter, and now go forward, now slip down or collapse. And they will slip down, unless they keep on going and striving; if they slacken anything of their zeal and faithful exertion, they have to go backward. No one finds his progress where he had left it.

71.36  So let us press on and persevere. More remains than we have got through, but a great part of progress is wanting to make progress. This I know of myself: I want to, and I want to with my whole mind. I see that you too have been roused and are hurrying with great force toward the finest things. Let us hurry: only so will life be a gift. Otherwise it is a delay, and a shameful one at that, for those who live among foul things. Let us see to it that all time is ours; but it will not be, unless we have first begun to be our own.



LETTER 72: ON NOT LETTING BUSINESS POSTPONE PHILOSOPHY

72.3  It is not when you have time free that you are to do philosophy, but you are to make time free so that you may do philosophy. Everything else is to be neglected so that we may sit in attendance on philosophy, for which no time is long enough, even if life is drawn out from boyhood to the farthest limits of a human lifetime. It does not matter much whether you give philosophy up or leave it off for a time; for it does not stay where it was interrupted, but, like things stretched tight that spring apart, it runs back all the way to its beginnings, because it has left off being continuous. We must resist the things that keep us busy: they are not to be untangled but pushed out of the way. There is in fact no time that is ill suited to a study that brings health; and yet many do not study in the midst of the things on account of which one ought to study.

72.4  “Something will come up to get in the way.” Not, at any rate, in the way of the man whose mind is glad and lively in every piece of business. In those who are still imperfect, gladness is cut apart; but the wise man’s joy is woven in one piece: it is torn by no cause, by no fortune. He is calm always and everywhere. For he does not hang on what is not his own, and he does not wait for the favour of fortune or of a man. His happiness is home-grown: it would go out of the mind if it came into it. It is born there.



LETTER 74: ON VIRTUE AS THE ONLY SAFE GOOD

74.10  Whoever resolves to be happy should think that the only good is what is honourable. For if he considers anything else a good, first, he passes an unfavourable judgment on providence, because many troubles happen to just men, and because whatever it has given us is short and slight, if you compare it with the lifetime of the whole world.

74.18  Let all those things come to us as additions and not stick fast to us, so that, if they are led away, they may leave without tearing us at all. Let us use them, not boast of them, and let us use them sparingly, as things deposited with us that are going to leave. Whoever has possessed them without the use of reason has not kept them long; for good fortune itself, unless it is kept within measure, bears itself down. If he has trusted in goods that are the quickest to run away, he is soon deserted, and, in order to be deserted, he is struck down. Few have been allowed to lay their good fortune down gently: the rest fall together with the things among which they stood high, and the very things that had lifted them up weigh them down.

74.20  Let him not be indignant at anything that happens to him, and let him know that the very things by which he seems to be harmed serve to preserve the universe and are among those that bring the course of the world and its task to completion. Let whatever has pleased god please man. For this very reason let him admire himself and what is his: that he cannot be conquered, that he holds evils themselves beneath him, that by reason, and nothing is stronger than reason, he subdues chance and pain and wrong.



LETTER 75: ON PLAIN WRITING AND STAGES OF PROGRESS

75.15  Let us give some thought to this rank: it will go well with us if we are admitted to this group. The second step is won through great good fortune in one’s nature and through great and unremitting effort in study; but not even this third colour is to be despised. Think how much evil you see around you; look how there is no outrage without a precedent, how much progress wickedness makes every day, how much wrong is done in public and in private: you will understand that we attain enough if we are not among the worst.



LETTER 76: ON LEARNING IN OLD AGE AND THE ONLY GOOD

76.4  But I am ashamed of the human race every time I go into the school. As you know, anyone making for Metronax’s house has to pass right by the theatre of the Neapolitans. That place, to be sure, is packed, and with enormous eagerness judgment is passed on who is a good piper; a Greek trumpeter and a herald draw a crowd as well. But in the place where a good man is what is inquired into, where a good man is what is learned, very few sit, and to most people these seem to have no good business to be about; they are called silly and idle. May that mockery fall to me: the abuse of the ignorant must be heard with a calm mind, and one who is heading for what is honourable must hold contempt itself in contempt.

76.5  Keep going, Lucilius, and hurry, so that what has happened to me does not happen to you: to be learning as an old man. Or rather, hurry all the more for this reason, that you have now taken on something you could hardly learn through to the end as an old man. “How much progress shall I make?” you say. As much as you attempt.

76.8  Every thing rests on its own good. What recommends the vine is its fruitfulness and the flavour of its wine; what recommends the stag is speed. Of pack animals, whose one use is this, to carry a load, you ask how strong they are in the back. In a dog the first thing is keenness of scent if it has to track wild animals, running if it has to catch them, boldness if it has to bite and attack. In each thing, what ought to be best is that for which it is born, that by which it is rated.

76.9  What is best in a human being? Reason: by this he goes ahead of the animals and comes next after the gods. So perfected reason is the good peculiar to him; everything else he has in common with animals and plants. He is strong: so are lions. He is handsome: so are peacocks. He is swift: so are horses. I say nothing of his being outdone in all these; I am not asking what he has in him that is greatest, but what is his own. He has a body: so do trees. He has impulse and voluntary movement: so do beasts and worms. He has a voice: but how much louder is the dogs’, how much shriller the eagles’, deeper the bulls’, sweeter and more supple the nightingales’?

76.10  What is peculiar to a human being? Reason: when this is right and brought to its full measure, it has made a human being’s happiness complete. So if every thing, when it has perfected its own good, is praiseworthy and has reached the goal of its nature, and if a human being’s own good is reason, then if he has perfected this he is praiseworthy and has touched the goal of his nature. This perfected reason is called virtue, and it is also what is honourable.

76.23  You must grant that the good man is a man of the highest piety towards the gods. So whatever happens to him he will bear with a calm mind; for he will know that it has happened by the divine law by which all things go forward. If that is so, the one good for him will be what is honourable; for in this lies obeying the gods, and not flaring up at sudden events or bewailing his lot, but patiently accepting fate and doing what is commanded.

76.29  But even the man from whom this joy too is snatched away, the joy that the handling of his greatest and last work gives, will leap down into death without any hesitation, content to act rightly and dutifully. Go further and set against him many things to dissuade him; say: “Your deed will be followed by early oblivion and by a less than grateful verdict from your fellow citizens.” He will answer you: “All those things lie outside my work; I am looking at the work itself. I know that this is honourable; so wherever it leads and calls, I come.”

76.31  None of those whom you see dressed in purple is happy, any more than any of those to whom the plays assign a sceptre and a cloak on the stage: when they have walked before the people with a broad swagger and in high boots, the moment they have gone off, their boots are taken off and they return to their own stature. None of those whom riches and offices set at a greater height is great. Why then does he seem great? You are measuring him together with his pedestal. A dwarf is not great though he has taken his stand on a mountain; a colossus will keep its greatness even if it stands in a well.

76.32  This is the error we suffer from, this is how we are taken in: we value no one by what he is, but add to him the things he is decked out with as well. Yet when you want to make a true valuation of a human being and to know what he is like, examine him naked; let him lay aside his estate, let him lay aside his offices and the other lies of fortune, let him strip off even his body: look at his mind, what it is like and how great, whether it is great by what is another’s or by what is its own.

76.34  When an evil has been thought out beforehand, its blow comes soft. But to fools and to those who trust fortune every face that events show seems new and unexpected; and for the inexperienced a great part of an evil is its newness. So that you may know this: the things they had thought harsh they bear more bravely once they have grown used to them.



LETTER 77: ON A FRIEND'S CALM CHOSEN DEATH

77.4  A journey will be incomplete if you stop in the middle of it or short of the place you were making for. A life is not incomplete if it is honourable; wherever you leave off, if you leave off well, it is whole. But often one must leave off both bravely and for reasons that are not the greatest; for the reasons that hold us are not the greatest either.

77.6  Our Stoic friend, an outstanding person and, to praise him in the words in which he deserves to be praised, a brave and energetic man, seems to me to have urged him on best. For he began like this: “Do not torment yourself, my Marcellinus, as if you were deliberating about a great matter. Living is not a great matter: all your slaves live, all animals do. The great thing is to die honourably, sensibly, bravely. Think how long you have now been doing the same thing: food, sleep, lust. This is the circle one runs round. Not only a sensible man or a brave one or a wretched one can want to die, but also one who is fed up.”

77.19  “But I,” he says, “want to live, I who do many things honourably; it is against my will that I am leaving the duties of life, which I carry out faithfully and diligently.” What? Do you not know that to die is also one of the duties of life? You are leaving no duty; for no fixed number is laid down that you must complete.



LETTER 78: ON BEARING ILLNESS AND PAIN

78.2  I often had the impulse to break off my life: the old age of my most loving father held me back. For I thought not of how bravely I could die, but of how far he was from being able to miss me bravely. So I ordered myself to live; for sometimes even to live is to act bravely.

78.7  Now let us go back to that trouble which is illness’s own: illness has great torments, but intervals make them bearable. For the intensity of pain at its height finds an end; no one can be in pain both severely and for long; nature, who loves us most dearly, has so arranged us as to make pain either bearable or short.

78.10  So this is the consolation for immense pain: that you are bound to stop feeling it if you feel it too much. But what troubles the untrained when the body is in distress is this: they have not become used to being content with the mind; they have had much to do with the body. That is why a great and prudent man draws his mind apart from the body and spends much time with the better and divine part; with this complaining and fragile one, as much as is necessary.

78.13  Do not make your own ills heavier for yourself and load yourself with complaints: pain is light if opinion has added nothing to it. If, on the other hand, you begin to encourage yourself and to say, “It is nothing, or at least it is slight; let us hold out; it will stop soon”, you will make it light in thinking it so. Everything hangs on opinion; it is not only ambition and extravagance and greed that look to it: we feel pain according to opinion.

78.14  Everyone is as wretched as he has believed himself to be. I think complaints about past pains should be done away with, and these words too: “No one has ever had it worse. What torments, what great ills I have gone through! No one thought I would get up again. How many times my own people gave me up for lost, how many times the doctors abandoned me! Men put on the rack are not pulled apart like this.” Even if those things are true, they are over: what good does it do to go back over past pains and to be wretched because you have been? And what of the fact that there is no one who does not add a great deal to his own ills and lie to himself? Then again, what was bitter to bear is pleasant to have borne: it is natural to be glad at the end of one’s own ill. So two things must be cut away, both the fear of what is to come and the memory of old trouble: the old trouble no longer concerns me, what is to come does not yet.

78.15  Set in the very midst of difficulties, let him say, “Perhaps one day it will be pleasant to remember even these things.” Let him fight back with his whole mind; he will be conquered if he gives way, he will conquer if he braces himself against his pain. As things are, what most people do is this: they pull down on themselves the collapse they ought to be standing against. That thing which presses, which hangs overhead, which bears down: if you begin to draw yourself out from under it, it will follow and weigh more heavily; if you stand against it and choose to push back, it will be driven back.

78.18  This too will help: to turn the mind away to other thoughts and to step away from the pain. Think of what you have done honourably, what you have done bravely; go over the good parts of your life in your own mind; scatter your memory across the things you have most admired; then let all the bravest men, the conquerors of pain, come before you: the man who went on reading a book while he offered his varicose veins to be cut out, the man who did not stop laughing while the torturers, angered by that very thing, tried every instrument of their cruelty. Will pain not be conquered by reason, when it has been conquered by laughter?

78.20  “But illness,” he says, “lets me do nothing; it has taken me away from all my duties.” Ill health holds your body, not your mind as well. So it slows the feet of a runner, it hampers the hands of a cobbler or a smith: if your mind is what you are used to using, you will advise and teach, listen and learn, inquire and remember. And another thing: do you believe you are doing nothing if you are self-controlled in your sickness? You will show that illness can be overcome, or at least borne.

78.26  For a life that is surveying so many varied, great, divine things cannot be overtaken by the sense of having had its fill of itself: what usually brings it to hatred of itself is sluggish leisure. To one who ranges through the nature of things the truth will never become distasteful: it is false things that he will have his fill of.

78.29  Meanwhile hold on to this, grip this with your teeth: not to give in to adversity, not to trust prosperity, to keep before your eyes all that Fortune is free to do, as though she were going to do whatever she can do. Whatever has been long expected is lighter when it comes. Farewell.



LETTER 79: ON ETNA, VIRTUE AND LATE FAME

79.17  No virtue lies hidden, and to have lain hidden is not virtue’s own loss: a day will come to make public the virtue that was buried and held down by the spite of its own age. The man who thinks of the people of his own time is born for few. Many thousands of years, many thousands of peoples will come after: look to those. Even if envy has imposed silence on all who are alive with you, there will come those who judge without ill will, without favour. If virtue has any reward from fame, this does not perish either. The talk of those who come after will indeed not concern us at all; yet it will honour us and keep returning to us even though we do not feel it.



LETTER 80: ON TRAINING THE MIND AND FALSE HAPPINESS

80.3  What I turn over most in my own thoughts is this: if the body can be brought by training to this endurance, by which it bears the fists and equally the kicks of more than one man, by which someone holding out under the most blazing sun in the most scorching dust, and drenched in his own blood, lasts out the day, how much more easily the mind can be strengthened so that it takes the blows of Fortune unconquered, so that thrown down, so that trampled on, it rises up. For the body needs many things in order to be in good condition: the mind grows from itself, feeds itself, trains itself. Those men need much food, much drink, much oil, and long labour besides: virtue will come to you without equipment, without expense. Whatever can make you good is with you.

80.10  Do you see that king of Scythia or Sarmatia, adorned with the mark of rank on his head? If you want to value him and to know him whole, what he is like, undo the band: much that is bad lies hidden under it. Why do I speak of others? If you want to weigh yourself up, set aside money, house, standing; look at yourself within, for yourself: as it is, you take the word of others for what you are. Farewell.



LETTER 81: ON GRATITUDE AND REPAYING FAVOURS

81.1  You complain that you have run into an ungrateful man. If this is now the first time, give thanks either to fortune or to your own carefulness. But in this matter carefulness can do nothing except make you grudging; for if you want to avoid this risk, you will not give benefits; and in this way, so that they are not lost in someone else’s hands, they will be lost in yours. Let them fail to bring a return rather than fail to be given: even after a bad harvest one must sow. Often whatever had been lost through the persistent barrenness of a poor soil has been restored by the richness of a single year.

81.19  We must do everything to be as grateful as we can. For this good is our own, in the same way that justice is not, as is commonly believed, something that has to do with others: a great part of it comes back to itself. There is no one who, when he does good to another, has not done good to himself. I do not say this for the reason that the man who has been helped will want to help and the man who has been defended will want to protect, or that a good example comes back round in a circle to the one who sets it (just as bad examples fall back on their authors, and no pity comes to those who suffer wrongs that they taught could be done, by doing them), but because the reward of all the virtues is in the virtues themselves. For they are not practised for a prize: the pay for a right deed is to have done it.

81.22  So let us avoid being ungrateful, not for another’s sake but for our own. The smallest and lightest part of wickedness spills over onto others: what is worst in it and, so to speak, thickest stays at home and presses on the one who has it, as our Attalus used to say: “Vice itself drinks the greatest part of its own poison.” That poison which snakes bring out for another’s destruction, and hold without destruction to themselves, is not like this one: this one is worst for those who have it.

81.24  Bad men have one pleasure, and that a short one, while they are receiving benefits; from those benefits a long joy, one that does not fail, stays with the wise man. For what delights him is not receiving but having received, which is undying and constant. He scorns the things that have hurt him, and he forgets not through carelessness but by choice.

81.25  He does not turn everything to the worse, nor does he look for someone to blame for an accident, and he puts men’s wrongdoings down to fortune instead. He does not put a bad meaning on words or looks; whatever happens, he lightens it by interpreting it kindly. He does not remember an offence rather than a service; as far as he can he keeps himself in the earlier and better memory, and his mind does not change toward those who have done well by him unless the bad deeds far outstrip the good and the difference is plain even to one who shuts his eyes; and even then only this far, that after the greater wrong he is such as he was before the benefit. For when the wrong is equal to the benefit, some goodwill remains in the mind.

81.26  Just as a defendant is acquitted when the votes are equal, and humane feeling always tips whatever is doubtful toward the better side, so the mind of the wise man, when the services are equal to the misdeeds, does indeed cease to owe, but does not cease to want to owe, and does what those do who pay after a cancellation of debts.



LETTER 82: ON FACING DEATH WITHOUT LOGICAL TRICKS

82.5  Philosophy must be set around us, a wall that cannot be stormed, which Fortune does not get past, though she has assailed it with many siege engines. The mind that has abandoned external things stands in a place that cannot be scaled, and claims its freedom in its own citadel; every missile falls below it. Fortune does not have the long hands we think she has: she seizes no one except the one who clings to her.

82.15  Among these things too, Lucilius, which we call middle, there is a great difference. For death is not a matter of indifference in the same way as whether you have an even number of hairs: death is among those things that are not indeed evils, yet have the look of an evil. There is love of self, and an inborn will to last and to preserve oneself, and a shrinking from dissolution, [gap in the Latin text] because it seems to snatch many goods away from us and to lead us out of this plenty of things to which we have grown used. This too estranges us from death, that we already know the things here, while we do not know what the things are like to which we are going to cross over, and we shudder at the unknown. Besides, there is a natural fear of the dark, into which, it is believed, death will lead us.

82.16  So even if death is a matter of indifference, it is still not a thing that can easily be disregarded: the mind must be hardened by much training to bear the sight of it and its approach. Death ought to be scorned more than it usually is; for we have believed many things about it; the talents of many have competed to increase its bad name; a prison in the underworld has been described, and a region weighed down by everlasting night, in which the huge doorkeeper of Orcus, lying on half-eaten bones in his bloody cave, barking for ever, frightens the bloodless shades. Even when you have persuaded people that these are stories and that nothing is left for the dead to fear, another fear comes up: for they are equally afraid of being in the underworld and of being nowhere.



LETTER 83: ON A DAILY ROUTINE AND DRUNKENNESS

83.1  You tell me to report to you each of my days, and the whole of each: you judge well of me if you think there is nothing in them that I would hide. Certainly we must live as if we were living in plain sight, and think as if someone could look into the inmost heart: and someone can. For what is the use of something being kept secret from a human being? Nothing is shut to god; he is present in our minds and comes into the midst of our thoughts. I say “comes into” as if he ever went away.

83.2  So I will do what you tell me, and I will gladly write to you what I do and in what order. I will watch myself at once and, what is most useful, I will review my day. This is what makes us very bad, that no one looks back at his own life; we think about what we are going to do, and that rarely; we do not think about what we have done; and yet a plan for the future comes from the past.



LETTER 84: ON READING AND DIGESTING WHAT YOU READ

84.11  “How,” you say, “will it be possible to bring this about?” By constant attention: if we do nothing except at reason’s urging and avoid nothing except at reason’s urging. If you are willing to listen to reason, it will say to you: leave at once those things that people run about after; leave riches, either a danger or a burden to those who own them; leave the pleasures of body and of mind, they soften and weaken; leave ambition, it is a swollen thing, empty, windy, it has no limit, it is as anxious not to see anyone ahead of it as not to see anyone level with it, it suffers from envy, and a double envy at that. And you see how wretched a man is if he who is envied also envies.

84.13  Whatever things seem to stand high in human affairs, though they are tiny and stand out by comparison with the lowest, are still reached by hard and steep paths. The road to the peak of rank is rough; but if you care to climb this summit, the one before which Fortune has lowered herself, you will indeed see beneath you everything that is counted as loftiest, and yet you will come to the top over level ground. Farewell.



LETTER 85: ON REJECTING EVEN MODERATE PASSIONS

85.11  Next, if you grant any rights to sadness, to fear, to desire, to the other wrong movements, they will not be in our power. Why? Because the things that provoke them are outside us; and so they will grow according to whether the causes that stir them up are great or lesser. Fear will be greater if it has seen more to terrify it, or seen it nearer; desire will be keener to the degree that the hope of a larger thing has called it out.

85.13  But what madness this is: to believe that where the beginnings of things are placed outside our control, their limits are within our control! How am I strong enough to set a limit to a thing that I was not strong enough to prevent, when it is easier to shut things out than to hold them down once they have been let in?

85.33  I judge that a different answer should be given: that neither the helmsman’s art nor the actual practice of the art is made worse by any storm. The helmsman did not promise you good luck, but useful effort and the knowledge of how to steer a ship; this knowledge is all the more visible, the more some chance force has stood in his way. Anyone who has been able to say this, “Neptune, never this ship except on a straight course”, has done enough for his art: a storm hinders not the helmsman’s work but his success.

85.36  Next, the helmsman’s art is a good that belongs to others: it is for those he carries, as the doctor’s is for those he treats. [The wise man’s] is a shared good: it is both the good of those he lives with and his own. And so the helmsman may perhaps be harmed, whose service, promised to others, is hindered by a storm.

85.37  The wise man is not harmed by poverty, nor by pain, nor by the other storms of life. For not all his work is prevented, but only the work that is for others: he himself is always in action, greatest in what he achieves at the time when fortune has set itself against him; for then he is doing the business of wisdom itself, which we said is both a good that belongs to others and his own.

85.38  Besides, he is not prevented from being of use even to others at the time when some constraints press on him. Because of poverty he is prevented from teaching how the state should be handled, but he teaches this: how poverty should be handled. His work stretches through the whole of life. So no fortune, no circumstance shuts out the wise man’s actions; for he is busy with the very thing that prevents him from being busy with other things. He is fitted for either turn of events: he steers good things, he conquers bad ones.

85.40  You think he is pressed by bad things? He uses them. It was not only out of ivory that Phidias knew how to make statues; he made them out of bronze. If you had offered him marble, if you had offered him a still cheaper material, he would have made the best that could be made out of it. In the same way the wise man will display virtue in riches if he is allowed, and if not, in poverty; in his own country if he can, and if not, in exile; as a general if he can, and if not, as a soldier; able-bodied if he can, and if not, disabled. Whatever fortune he receives, he will make out of it something worth remembering.



LETTER 88: ON THE LIMITS OF LIBERAL STUDIES

88.19  For what is there that is liberal, I ask you, about those people who vomit on an empty stomach, whose bodies are stuffed fat and whose minds are thin and sluggish? Or do we believe that this is a liberal study for our youth, whom our ancestors trained upright, to throw javelins, to hurl a stake, to ride a horse, to handle weapons? They taught their children nothing that had to be learned lying down. But neither these arts nor those teach or nourish virtue; for what use is it to control a horse and moderate its pace with the bridle, yet be dragged away by the most unbridled passions? What use is it to beat many at wrestling or boxing, yet be beaten by a hot temper?



LETTER 89: ON THE DIVISIONS OF PHILOSOPHY

89.3  The people is distributed into tribes, the army into centuries; whatever has grown large is more easily made out if it has separated into parts, which, as I said, ought not to be countless and tiny. For too much division has the same fault as none: whatever has been cut all the way down to dust is like a jumble.

89.14  So, since philosophy has three parts, let us begin by setting out its moral part first. It has been held that this in turn is divided into three: the first is the examination that assigns to each thing its own and assesses how much each is worth, useful in the highest degree (for what is so necessary as putting prices on things?); the second is about impulse, the third about actions. For the first thing is that you judge how much each thing is worth; the second, that you take up toward those things an impulse that is ordered and moderated; the third, that your impulse and your action fit together, so that in all of these you agree with yourself.

89.15  Whichever of the three is missing throws the rest into disorder as well. For what use is it to have everything assessed, if you go too far in impulse? What use is it to have checked your impulses and to have your desires in your own power, if in the action itself you are ignorant of the timing and do not know when each thing should be done, and where, and how? For it is one thing to know the worth and the prices of things, another to know the critical moments, another to rein in impulses and to go to what has to be done, not rush at it. So a life is in harmony with itself when action does not desert impulse, and impulse takes its start from the worth of each thing, slack [or] keener in proportion as that thing is worth seeking.

89.19  “I was the one who ought to be saying ‘How long will you keep doing the same wrong things?’ Do you want the remedies to stop before the vices do? I, for my part, will say it all the more, and because you refuse I will keep on; medicine begins to do good at the point when, in a body that has lost its feeling, a touch has forced out pain. I will say things that will help even the unwilling. Let some voice that does not flatter reach you at last, and because you are not willing to hear the truth each on your own, hear it all together.



LETTER 90: ON PHILOSOPHY AND THE INVENTION OF CRAFTS

90.29  Then it went back to the beginnings of things, to the eternal reason set within the whole, and to the force of all seeds, which shapes each single thing in its own way. Then it began to inquire about the soul: where it comes from, where it is, how long it lasts, into how many members it is divided. Then it moved from bodies to things without body, and examined truth and its proofs thoroughly; after that, how the ambiguities of life or of speech were to be told apart; for in both, false things are mixed in with true.

90.44  But although their life was outstanding and free of deceit, they were not wise men, since that name now belongs to the greatest of works. Still, I would not deny that they were men of lofty spirit and, so to speak, fresh from the gods; for there is no doubt that the world brought forth better things when it was not yet worn out with bearing. But while all of them had an inborn character that was stronger and readier for toil, their natural abilities were not, in all of them, brought to completion. For nature does not give virtue: to become good is an art.

90.46  What then? They were innocent through ignorance of things; but it makes a great difference whether someone does not want to do wrong or does not know how. They lacked justice, they lacked prudence, they lacked self-control and courage. Their unschooled life had certain things that resembled all these virtues: virtue does not come to a mind unless that mind has been trained and taught thoroughly and brought to the highest point by constant exercise. We are born for this, it is true, but without it, and even in the best people, before you school them, there is the material of virtue, not virtue. Farewell.



LETTER 91: ON THE BURNING OF LYON AND EXPECTING DISASTER

91.3  All these things bend the spirit of our friend Liberalis, a spirit firm and unbowed against his own troubles. And he has not been shaken without cause: what is unexpected weighs more heavily; newness adds weight to disasters, and no mortal has failed to feel more pain at what also took him by surprise.

91.4  That is why nothing ought to be unforeseen by us; the mind must be sent ahead to everything, and what must be thought of is not whatever usually happens but whatever can happen. For what is there that Fortune, when she has wished it, does not drag down from its fullest flowering? What is there that she does not attack and shake all the more, the more splendidly it shines? What is an uphill climb for her, or what is hard?

91.19  Still, there is no reason for you to believe those people who make a din all around you: none of these things is an evil, none is unbearable or hard. Their fear comes from general agreement. You fear death in the same way that you fear what people say: but what is more foolish than a man who is afraid of words? Our friend Demetrius has a neat way of saying that the voices of the ignorant count for the same with him as the noises of the belly breaking wind. “For what is it to me,” he says, “whether those people make their sound up above or down below?”



LETTER 92: ON REASON AND VIRTUE SUFFICING FOR HAPPINESS

92.1  I think you and I will agree that external things are acquired for the body, that the body is cared for in honour of the mind, that in the mind there are serving parts, through which we move and are nourished, given to us for the sake of the ruling part itself. In this ruling part there is something without reason, and there is also something with reason; the first serves the second, and the second is the one thing that is not referred to anything else but refers everything to itself. For that divine reason too is set over all things and is itself under nothing; and this reason of ours, which comes from that one, is the same.

92.2  If we agree on this, it follows that we also agree on that: the happy life rests on this one thing, that reason in us should be perfect. For it alone does not let the mind sink; it stands against fortune; in whatever state things are, it keeps [the next word is corrupt in the Latin]. And that alone is good which is never broken off. That man, I say, is happy whom nothing makes smaller; he holds the heights, and leans on no one at all except himself; for a man who is held up by some support can fall. If it is otherwise, things that are not ours will begin to have great power in us. But who wants to owe his standing to fortune, or what sensible man admires himself for things that are not his own?

92.11  “What then?” he says. “If good health and rest and freedom from pains are not going to hinder virtue in any way, will you not seek them?” Why would I not seek them? Not because they are goods, but because they are according to nature, and because they will be taken by me with good judgment. What then will be good in them? This one thing: being well chosen. For when I take the kind of clothing that is fitting, when I walk as I ought, when I dine as I should, it is not the dinner or the walk or the clothing that is a good, but my purpose in them, the purpose of one who keeps, in each thing, the measure that agrees with reason.

92.12  I will add this as well: the choice of clean clothing is something a human being should seek; for a human being is by nature a clean animal and one that chooses with care. And so clean clothing is not a good in itself, but the choice of clean clothing is, because the good is not in the thing but in a choice of a certain kind; it is our actions that are honourable, not the things themselves that are done.

92.18  In the same way, the things set in the way of virtue take nothing away from it: it is not smaller, but it shines less. To us, perhaps, it does not show and gleam as much; to itself it is the same, and like the sun when it is obscured it exerts its force out of sight. And so disasters and losses and wrongs have the same power against virtue that a mist has against the sun.

92.33  When it has raised itself to this height, it is, towards the body too, as towards a necessary burden, not a lover but a manager, and it does not put itself under the thing it has been set over. No one is free who is a slave to the body; for, even if you pass over the other masters that too much anxiety on the body’s behalf finds, the body’s own rule is hard to please and fussy.



LETTER 93: ON THE QUALITY NOT LENGTH OF LIFE

93.2  Which, I beg you, do you judge the fairer: that you should obey nature, or that nature should obey you? And what does it matter how soon you leave a place that must be left in any case? What we must take care of is not that we live long but that we live enough; for to live long you need fate, to live enough you need the mind. A life is long if it is full; and it is made full when the mind has given back to itself its own good and has transferred to itself the power over itself.

93.6  I would not on that account refuse to have more years added to me; yet I will say that I lacked nothing for the happy life if its span is cut short; for I have not made myself ready for the day that greedy hope had promised me as my last, but there is no day I have not looked on as if it were my last. Why do you ask me when I was born, or whether I am still registered among the younger men? I have what is mine.

93.7  Just as a person can be complete in a smaller build of body, so too a life can be complete in a smaller measure of time. Years are among the external things. How long I am to be is not mine: to be, for as long as I will be, is mine. Demand this of me: that I do not travel the whole length of an obscure lifetime as if through darkness, that I lead my life, not that I am carried past it.



LETTER 94: ON THE USEFULNESS OF PRACTICAL ADVICE

94.26  You know that friendships are to be cultivated religiously, but you do not do it. You know that a man is shameless who demands chastity of his wife while he himself is a seducer of other men’s wives; you know that, just as she ought to have nothing to do with an adulterer, so you ought to have nothing to do with a mistress, and you do not do it. So you must be brought back to remembering again and again; for these things ought not to be stored away but at hand. Whatever is wholesome ought to be often stirred, often turned over, so that it is not only known to us but also ready. Add now that even plain things usually become plainer.

94.55  So let there be some guardian, and let him tug at our ear again and again, and drive off the common talk, and shout back against the praise of the crowds. For you are wrong if you think that vices are born with us: they have come upon us, they have been forced into us. So let the opinions that sound all around us be driven back by frequent reminders.

94.61  Many are found who carry fire into cities, who lay low what the centuries could not storm and what had been safe through several generations, who raise a siege mound level with citadels, and with rams and engines batter walls that had been built up to an astonishing height. There are many who drive armies before them and press hard on the backs of the enemy and come to the great sea drenched in the slaughter of nations; but these too, in order to conquer the enemy, were conquered by desire. No one withstood them as they came, but neither had they themselves withstood ambition and cruelty; at the time when they seemed to be driving others, they were being driven.

94.62  Wretched Alexander was driven by a madness for laying waste what belonged to others, and it sent him off to unknown lands. Or do you think a man sane who begins first with the disasters of Greece, where he was educated? Who snatches from each the best thing it has: orders Sparta to be a slave, Athens to be silent? Not content with the overthrow of so many states, which Philip had either conquered or bought, he throws down others, some in one place and some in another, and carries his arms around the whole world; and his cruelty, like savage beasts that bite more than hunger demands, nowhere tires and comes to a halt.

94.64  Gnaeus Pompeius, too, was urged to wars abroad and at home not by virtue or reason but by an insane love of false greatness. Now he was going into Spain and against the arms of Sertorius, now to bind the pirates and pacify the seas: these were the causes held out as a cover, in order to keep his power going without a break.

94.65  What dragged him into Africa, what into the north, what against Mithridates and into Armenia and every corner of Asia? Plainly a boundless desire to grow, since to himself alone he seemed not great enough. What sent Gaius Caesar headlong into his own doom and the state’s alike? Glory and ambition and no limit to standing out above the rest. He could not bear one man ahead of him, though the state was bearing two above it.



LETTER 95: ON WHY ADVICE NEEDS UNDERLYING PRINCIPLES

95.8  All those arts are occupied with the equipment of life, not with the whole of life; and so many things check them from outside and hinder them: hope, desire, fear. But this one, which professes the art of life, cannot be prevented by anything from exercising itself, for it shatters hindrances and tosses aside what stands in its way. Do you want to know how unlike the condition of the other arts is to the condition of this one? In them it is more excusable to go wrong on purpose than by accident; in this one the greatest fault is to do wrong of one’s own accord.

95.9  What I mean is this. A grammarian will not blush at a mistake in grammar if he made it knowingly; he will blush if he made it unknowingly. A doctor, if he does not realise that his patient is failing, goes wrong more, as far as his art is concerned, than if he pretends not to realise it. But in this art of living the fault of those who go wrong willingly is the more shameful. Add now the fact that most arts too, or rather the most liberal arts of them all, have their own doctrines, not only precepts, as medicine does; and so the school of Hippocrates is one, that of Asclepiades another, that of Themison another.

95.45  Marcus Brutus, in the book he titled “Peri kathekontos” (“On Duty”), gives many precepts to parents and to children and to brothers. No one will carry these out as he ought unless he has something to refer them to. We must set before us the goal of the highest good, towards which we may strive, towards which every deed and word of ours may look, just as those who sail must direct their course towards some star.

95.47  It is usual to give precepts on how the gods are to be worshipped. Let us forbid anyone to light lamps on the sabbath, since the gods have no need of light and not even human beings take pleasure in soot. Let us forbid the performing of morning greetings and sitting at the doors of temples: it is human vanity that is won by such attentions; whoever knows god worships him. Let us forbid the bringing of towels and bath scrapers to Jupiter and the holding of a mirror for Juno: god does not look for servants. Why would he? He himself serves the human race; he is at hand everywhere and for everyone.

95.49  What cause do the gods have for doing good? Nature. Anyone who thinks they are unwilling to do harm is mistaken: they are not able to. They can neither suffer a wrong nor do one, for to hurt and to be hurt are joined together. Those whom that nature, the highest and most beautiful of all, has taken out of danger it has not made dangerous either.

95.50  The first way to worship the gods is to believe in the gods; next, to give them the majesty that is theirs, to give them the goodness without which there is no majesty; to know that it is they who preside over the world, who govern all things by their power, who have the guardianship of the human race, sometimes without concern for individuals. They neither give evil nor have it; yet they chastise some people and restrain them and impose punishments, and sometimes they punish by what looks like a good. Do you want to win the favour of the gods? Be good. Whoever has imitated them has worshipped them enough.

95.52  All this that you see, in which things divine and human are enclosed, is one; we are limbs of a great body. Nature brought us forth as kin, since she begot us from the same things and for the same things. She planted in us love for one another and made us fit for fellowship. She established what is fair and just; by her arrangement it is more wretched to harm than to be hurt; by her command let our hands be ready for those who need help.

95.53  Let that verse be both in the heart and on the lips: “I am human: I count nothing human foreign to me.” Let us have it in common: we have been born. Our fellowship is very like an arch of stones, which would fall if the stones did not block one another, and is held up by that very thing.

95.54  After gods and human beings, let us look into how things are to be used. We shall be tossing out precepts to no purpose unless this has come first: what opinion we ought to hold about each thing, about poverty, about riches, about glory, about disgrace, about homeland, about exile. Let us assess each thing one by one with its reputation set aside, and ask what they are, not what they are called.



LETTER 96: ON ACCEPTING HARDSHIP WITHOUT COMPLAINT

96.1  Yet you are indignant at something, or complain, and do not understand that there is nothing evil in these things except this one thing, that you are indignant and complain? If you ask me, I think nothing is wretched for a man except that there is anything in nature which he thinks wretched. I shall not put up with myself on the day when I am not able to put up with something. I am in poor health: it is part of fate. The slaves have taken to their beds, the interest has gone wrong, the house has cracked, losses, wounds, hardships, fears have come rushing in: it commonly happens. That says too little: it had to happen.

96.2  These things are decreed; they are not accidents. If you trust me at all, I am at this very moment uncovering my inmost feelings to you: in all the things that seem adverse and hard, this is how I have been formed: I do not obey god but agree with him; I follow him from the heart, not because it is necessary. Nothing will ever befall me that I shall receive gloomily, that I shall receive with a sour face; I shall pay no tax unwillingly. And all the things at which we groan, at which we take fright, are the taxes of life: from these, my Lucilius, do not hope for exemption and do not ask for it.



LETTER 97: ON VICE IN EVERY AGE AND GUILTY CONSCIENCE

97.11  A helmsman does not rejoice when his ship has been overturned, a doctor does not rejoice when his patient has been carried out for burial, an orator does not rejoice if the defendant has lost through the advocate’s fault; but on the other hand to everyone his own offence is a pleasure. One man delights in an adultery to which he was provoked by its very difficulty; another delights in cheating and theft, and the fault does not displease him until the fault’s fortune does. This comes about through perverse habit.

97.12  Apart from that, so that you may know that a sense of the good lies underneath even in minds that have been drawn away into the worst things, and that what is shameful is not unknown but disregarded: all people conceal their wrongdoings and, however luckily these have turned out, enjoy the fruit of them while keeping the deeds themselves out of sight. But a good conscience wants to come forward and be seen; wickedness is afraid even of the darkness.

97.14  I do not judge that this conflicts with our school, if it is explained in this way. Why? Because the first and greatest punishment of those who do wrong is to have done wrong, and no crime, even if Fortune adorns it with her gifts, even if she protects and defends it, is unpunished, since the penalty of crime is in the crime. But nonetheless these second punishments too press hard upon that first one and follow it: always to be afraid, to take fright, and to have no trust in one’s own security. Why should I free wickedness from this penalty? Why should I not leave it always in suspense?



LETTER 98: ON NOT DEPENDING ON FORTUNE'S GIFTS

98.2  All the things that Fortune looks on become fruitful and pleasant on this condition: that the one who has them also has himself and is not in the power of his own things. For those people are wrong, Lucilius, who judge that Fortune gives us anything good or anything evil: she gives the material for goods and evils, and the beginnings of things that, in our keeping, will turn out for evil or for good. For the mind is stronger than any fortune, and itself leads its own things in either direction, and is for itself the cause of a happy life and of a wretched one.

98.5  Or rather, by Hercules, so that I may reach for a line that is braver and more just, one with which you may prop up your mind more, say this whenever something has turned out otherwise than you had reckoned: “The gods decided better.” Nothing will happen to one so settled. And he will be so settled if he has reckoned, before he has felt it, with what the changeableness of human affairs can do, if he has children and wife and property as one who will not necessarily have them always, and as one who will not be more wretched on that account if he stops having them.

98.9  So I think Metrodorus put it excellently in the letter in which he consoles his sister after the loss of her son, who was of the finest promise: “Every good of mortals is mortal.” He is speaking of these goods that people rush to in crowds. For that true good does not die; it is sure and everlasting: wisdom and virtue. This is the one immortal thing that falls to mortals.

98.10  But they are so shameless, and so forgetful of where they are going, of where each single day is pushing them, that they are surprised to lose anything themselves, though on one day they are going to lose everything. Whatever it is that you are written down as owner of is in your keeping; it is not yours. Nothing is firm for one who is not firm, nothing eternal and unconquerable for one who is fragile. It is as necessary to perish as to lose, and this very fact, if we understand it, is a comfort. Lose with an even mind: one must perish.

98.11  What help, then, do we find against these losses? This: that we hold in memory the things lost, and do not let the fruit we gathered from them drop away along with the things themselves. Having is snatched away; having had, never. It is a thoroughly ungrateful man who, when he has lost a thing, owes nothing for having received it. Chance snatches the thing from us; the use and the fruit of it chance leaves with us, and this we ourselves have lost by the unfairness of our longing.

98.14  Why have we given up? Why do we despair? Whatever it has been possible to do, it is possible to do. Only let us cleanse our mind and follow nature: one who strays from her must desire and fear and be a slave to the things of chance. We may return to the road, we may be restored to our original condition. Let us be restored, so that we can bear pains to the end, in whatever way they invade the body, and say to Fortune: “It is a man you have to deal with. Look for someone you can conquer.”

98.17  This, my Lucilius, is learning philosophy in action and training on the real thing: seeing how much spirit a man of good sense has against death, against pain, when the one is coming near and the other is pressing on him. What is to be done must be learned from one who is doing it.



LETTER 99: ON EXCESSIVE GRIEF FOR A LOST CHILD

99.4  But most people do not reckon up how much they have received, how much they have rejoiced. Among its other evils that grief has this one: it is not only needless but ungrateful. So then, that you had such a friend: is it wasted effort? In so many years, in so close a sharing of life, in so intimate a partnership of pursuits, was nothing achieved? Do you carry the friendship out to burial along with the friend? And why do you grieve at having lost him, if having had him does you no good? Believe me, a great part of those we have loved stays with us, even though chance has taken the people themselves away. The time that has gone by is ours, and nothing is in a safer place than what has been.

99.5  We are ungrateful for what we have received, out of hope for what is to come, as if what is to come, if it does turn out well for us, were not going to pass quickly into what is past. Whoever is glad only at what is present sets narrow bounds to the fruits of things. Both what is to come and what is past give delight, the one by expectation, the other by memory; but the one is in suspense and may not come about, while the other cannot not have been. What madness is it, then, to lose hold of what is surest? Let us rest in the things we have already drawn in, provided that we were not drawing them in with a mind full of holes, one that let through whatever it had received.

99.6  There are countless examples of men who carried out their children, young men, to burial without tears, who went back from the pyre to the senate or to some public duty and at once turned to other business. And not without reason. For first, it is needless to grieve if you gain nothing by grieving. Next, it is unfair to complain of a thing that has happened to one and is waiting for all. Next, the complaint that longing makes is foolish where very little lies between the one lost and the one who longs for him. So our mind ought to be all the more even, because we are following those we have lost.

99.10  “But he died a boy.” I am not yet saying that the one who is done with life is better off. Let us pass to the one who has grown old: by how little he beats the infant! Set before yourself the vastness of deep time and take in the whole; then compare this thing we call a human lifetime with the immeasurable. You will see how small a thing it is that we wish for, that we stretch out.

99.31  Let us also say this to one who weeps and longs for someone snatched away in his earliest years: all of us, as far as shortness of lifetime goes, if you compare it with the whole of time, both young and old, are on a level. For less comes to us out of all time than what anyone would call the least, since the least is at any rate some part. This span that we live is next to nothing; and yet, oh our madness, it is planned out on a wide scale.



LETTER 101: ON SUDDEN DEATH AND LIVING EACH DAY

101.7  But what is more foolish than to be surprised that something has happened on any one day which can happen on every day? A boundary does stand for us, where the necessity of the fates, which no plea can move, has fixed it; but none of us knows how near he is to the boundary. So let us shape our mind as though the end had been reached. Let us put nothing off. Let us balance our account with life every day.

101.8  The greatest fault of life is that it is always unfinished, that some part of it is put off. The man who has put the finishing touch to his life every day is not in need of time. But from this need fear is born, and a craving for the future, one that eats away the mind. Nothing is more wretched than doubt about where the things that are coming will end up. The mind, anxious about how much is left or what kind of thing it is, is driven about by a fear that cannot be untangled.

101.10  So hurry to live, my Lucilius, and count each single day as a single life. The man who has fitted himself in this way, for whom his life has been whole every day, is free from care. From those who live looking towards hope, each moment slips away as it comes nearest, and in come a greedy hunger and the fear of death, most wretched itself and making everything most wretched. Hence that most shameful prayer of Maecenas, in which he does not refuse crippling, or deformity, or last of all the sharp cross, provided that among these evils his breath is prolonged:



LETTER 102: ON FAME AFTER DEATH AND THE SOUL'S IMMORTALITY

102.3  You say that in my earlier letter I did not work through the whole inquiry in which I was trying to prove what our school holds: that the renown which comes after death is a good. For, you say, I did not resolve the objection that is made against us: “No good,” they say, “is made up of things that stand apart; but this one is made up of things that stand apart.”

102.17  Fame in any case needs a voice; renown can come even without a voice, content with a judgment. It is complete not only among people who are silent but even among people who cry out against it. I will say what the difference is between renown and glory: glory is made up of the judgments of the many, renown of the judgments of the good.

102.18  “Whose good,” he says, “is renown, that is, praise rendered to a good man by good men? Is it the good of the one praised or of the one praising?” Of both. It is mine, as the one who is praised: because nature bore me as someone who loves all people, I both rejoice to have done good and am glad to have found grateful interpreters of the virtues. That they are grateful is a good of several people, but it is mine too. For my mind is so made that I judge the good of others to be mine, at any rate the good of those for whom I am myself the cause of the good.

102.19  That is a good of those who praise, for it is done by virtue, and every act of virtue is a good. This could not have come to them unless I were such a man. So to be praised deservedly is a good of both, just as much, by Hercules, as to have judged well is a good of the one who judges and of the one in whose favour judgment was given. Surely you do not doubt that justice is both a good of the one who has it and also a good of the one to whom it pays what is owed? To praise one who deserves it is justice; therefore it is a good of both.

102.21  Say rather how natural it is to stretch one’s mind out into what has no measure. The human soul is a great and noble thing. It lets no boundaries be set for it except those it shares with god as well. First, it does not accept a lowly homeland, Ephesus or Alexandria or any ground there may be that is still more crowded with inhabitants or richer in buildings. Its homeland is whatever encloses the highest things and the whole of things in its circuit: this entire vault within which the seas and the lands lie, within which the air, while it separates the divine from the human, also joins them, in which so many divine powers are posted and stand watch, ready for their own work.

102.22  Next, it does not let a narrow span of life be given to it. “All the years are mine,” it says. “No age is closed to great intellects, and there is no time that is not open to thought. When that day comes which is to separate this mixture of the divine and the human, I will leave the body here where I found it, and myself I will give back to the gods. Even now I am not without them, but I am held back by what is heavy and earthly.”



LETTER 103: ON THE DANGER PEOPLE POSE TO EACH OTHER

103.1  Why do you look about for those things that can perhaps happen to you but can also not happen? I mean fire, the collapse of a building, other things that fall on us but do not lie in wait for us. Look rather at those, avoid those: the things that watch us, that try to catch us. Being shipwrecked, being overturned in a carriage: these are rare accidents, even if serious ones; from man to man the danger is daily. Make yourself ready against this, and keep your eyes fixed on this. No evil is more frequent, none more persistent, none more ingratiating.

103.3  For your part, however, think about what danger comes from man in such a way that you think about what the duty of man is. Keep your eyes on the one so that you are not harmed, on the other so that you do not harm. Be glad when things go well for anyone, be moved when they go badly, and remember what you ought to fulfil and what you ought to guard against.

103.4  What would you gain by living this way? Not that they will not harm you, but that they will not deceive you. But withdraw into philosophy as far as you can: she will shelter you in her bosom, and in her shrine you will be either safe or safer. People do not ram into one another unless they are walking on the same road.

103.5  But philosophy herself you must not show off. To many she has been a cause of danger when handled arrogantly and defiantly. Let her strip vices from you, not reproach others with theirs. Let her not be at odds with the ways of people at large, nor make it her business to seem to condemn whatever she does not do. One may be wise without parade, without ill will. Farewell.



LETTER 104: ON TRAVEL, HEALTH AND THE EXAMPLES OF GREAT MEN

104.3  For one must indulge honourable feelings; and sometimes, even if there are pressing reasons, the breath must be called back, even with torment, out of regard for one’s own people, and held back in the very mouth, since the good man must live not as long as it pleases him but as long as he ought. The man who thinks neither a wife nor a friend worth so much that he would stay longer in life, who will persist in dying, is soft. This too the mind should command of itself, when the interest of its own people demands it: not only if it wants to die but if it has begun to, it should pause and oblige its own people.

104.7  Place does not contribute much to this unless the mind puts itself at its own disposal; the mind will have seclusion in the midst of business if it wants it. But the man who picks out regions and chases after leisure will find everywhere something to pull him this way and that. For they say that Socrates, when someone complained that his travels had done him no good, answered: “This has happened to you, and not undeservedly, for you were travelling with yourself.”

104.26  But how much better I think of them! They too can do these things, but they do not want to. And after all, whom have those things ever failed when he tried them? To whom have they not appeared easier in the doing? It is not because things are difficult that we do not dare; it is because we do not dare that they are difficult.

104.27  If, however, you people want an example, take Socrates, a long-suffering old man, tossed through every hardship, yet unconquered both by poverty, which his household burdens made heavier for him, and by toils, which he bore in military service as well. By these he was harassed at home, whether [one thinks of] his wife, with her savage ways and abusive tongue, or [of] his children, unteachable and more like their mother than their father; [the text is damaged here] he lived either in war or under tyranny or in a freedom crueller than wars and tyrants.

104.28  There was fighting for twenty-seven years. After the war was ended the city was given up to be harmed by thirty tyrants, most of whom were his enemies. Last of all, his condemnation was carried through under the gravest charges: he was accused both of violating religion and of corrupting the young, whom he was said to be letting loose against the gods, against their fathers, against the state. After this, prison and poison. So far had these things been from moving the mind of Socrates that they did not move even his face. What a wonderful and unique distinction that is! To the very end no one saw Socrates more cheerful or more gloomy; he was unvarying amid such great variation of fortune.



LETTER 105: ON LIVING SAFELY AMONG OTHERS

105.4  As for not being feared, both a middling fortune and a gentle disposition will secure that for you. Let people know that you are someone they can offend without danger; let reconciliation with you be both easy and sure. To be feared, on the other hand, is as troublesome at home as outside it, by slaves as much as by free men: there is no one who does not have strength enough to do harm. Add now that he who is feared fears: no one has been able to be terrifying and free from care.



LETTER 107: ON ACCEPTING FATE WILLINGLY

107.2  None of these things is unusual, none is unexpected. To be jolted by such things is as ridiculous as to complain that you are splashed in public or dirtied in the mud. The terms of life are the same as those of a bathhouse, a crowd, a journey: some things will be thrown at you, some will fall on you. Living is not a soft business. You have set out on a long road: you are bound to slip, and knock into things, and fall, and grow tired, and cry out “O death!”, that is, tell a lie. In one place you will leave a companion behind, in another you will carry one out for burial, in another you will be afraid of one. Through jolts of this kind this rough journey has to be travelled to its end.

107.4  There is no one who has not gone more bravely to meet what he had long braced himself for and stood up even to hard things if they had been thought over beforehand; the unprepared man, on the other hand, has taken fright at even the lightest things. We must see to it that nothing is unexpected for us; and because all things are heavier for being new, constant thought will secure this: that you meet no trouble as a new recruit.

107.6  Let us be surprised at none of the things we were born to; no one should complain of them, for this reason, that they are equal for all. I mean it: they are equal, for even what someone has escaped he could have suffered. And an even-handed law is not the one that all have come under but the one that has been passed for all. Let the mind be commanded to keep an even temper, and let us pay the taxes of mortality without complaint.

107.9  Our mind must be fitted to this law; let it follow this, let it obey this; and let it think that whatever happens ought to have happened, and not wish to scold nature. It is best to bear what you cannot correct, and, without grumbling, to go along with the god by whose doing all things come about: he is a bad soldier who follows his commander groaning.

107.11  Lead me, O father and ruler of the high heaven, wherever you have decided: there is no delay in obeying; I am here, and eager. Suppose I am unwilling: I shall go along groaning, and as a bad man I shall be made to do what I was allowed to do as a good one. The fates lead the willing; the unwilling they drag.

107.12  Let us live this way, let us speak this way; let fate find us ready and eager. This is a great spirit, the one that has handed itself over to fate; the small and ignoble one, on the other hand, is the one that struggles against it and thinks ill of the order of the world and would rather correct the gods than itself. Farewell.



LETTER 108: ON HOW TO LISTEN TO PHILOSOPHERS

108.15  From that, some things have stayed with me, Lucilius; for I had come at everything with a great rush, and then, brought back to the life of a citizen, I kept few of the things I had begun well. From that, oysters and mushrooms were given up for life; for they are not foods but treats that force people who are full to eat (which is what the greedy, and those who stuff themselves beyond what they hold, like best), things that will go down easily, come back up easily.

108.16  From that, we keep away from perfume for life, since the best smell on a body is none. From that, a stomach that goes without wine. From that, we avoid the bath for life; we came to believe that boiling the body down and emptying it out by sweating is both useless and self-indulgent. The other things I threw off have come back, but in such a way that, where I have broken off my abstinence, I keep to a limit, and one in fact nearer to abstinence, and perhaps harder, since for the mind some things are more easily cut off than kept within limits.

108.21  “So hold back your judgment, but keep everything open for yourself. If those things are true, to have abstained from animals is freedom from guilt; if they are false, it is frugality. What does your credulity lose you in that case? I am taking from you the food of lions and vultures.”



LETTER 109: ON WHETHER THE WISE CAN HELP THE WISE

109.4  A bad man harms a bad man and makes him worse, by stirring up his anger, by going along with his gloom, by praising his pleasures; and bad men suffer most when they have mixed their vices most and their wickedness has been brought together into one. So, conversely, a good man will benefit a good man. “How?” you say.



LETTER 110: ON EMPTY FEARS AND TRUE RICHES

110.1  From my place at Nomentum I greet you and tell you to have a good mind, that is, all the gods well disposed; and whoever has made himself well disposed to himself has them appeased and favouring him. Set aside for now what some hold, that each one of us is given a god as a minder, not one of the regular rank, to be sure, but this one of a lower grade, from the number of those whom Ovid calls “gods from the common people”. Yet I want you to set this aside in such a way that you remember that our ancestors, who believed it, were Stoics; for to individuals, one by one, they gave both Genius and Juno.

110.2  We shall see later whether the gods have so much leisure that they manage the affairs of private persons. For the present know this: whether we have been assigned or neglected and given over to fortune, you can call down on no one anything worse than if you call down on him that he should have himself angry with him. But there is no reason for you to wish on anyone you think deserving of punishment that he should have the gods hostile: he has them so, I say, even if he seems to be carried forward by their favour.

110.4  But that falling itself has no evil in it, if you look to the end, beyond which nature has thrown no one down. The limit of all things is near; near, I say, is both that from which the fortunate man is thrown out and that from which the unfortunate man is let go: we stretch both out and make them long by hope and fear. But, if you are wise, measure all things by the human condition; draw in together both what you rejoice at and what you fear. And it is worth the price to rejoice at nothing for long, so that you may not fear anything for long.

110.10  Whatever was going to be for our good, our god and parent placed close at hand; he did not wait for us to search and gave it of his own accord: the things that would harm us he pressed down very deep. We can complain of nothing but ourselves: the things we were to perish by we have brought out, though the nature of things was unwilling and was hiding them. We have bound the mind over to pleasure, and to indulge pleasure is the beginning of all evils; we have handed it over to ambition and reputation, and to the rest, things equally empty and hollow.

110.17  So this is what I say to myself whenever something of that kind has dazzled my eyes, whenever I come upon a splendid house, a well-groomed troop of slaves, a litter set on handsome bearers: “Why do you marvel? Why are you struck dumb? It is a procession. Those things are shown, not owned, and while they please they pass by.”



LETTER 113: ON WHETHER THE VIRTUES ARE LIVING BEINGS

113.18  No rational living being does anything unless it has first been stirred by the impression of some thing, then has formed an impulse, and then assent has confirmed this impulse. I will say what assent is. I ought to walk: only then do I walk, when I have said this to myself and approved this opinion of mine; I ought to sit: only then do I sit. This assent is not found in virtue.

113.31  Let him teach me how sacred a thing justice is, which looks to another’s good and asks nothing from itself except to be put to use. Let it have nothing to do with ambition and reputation: let it win its own approval. Let each person convince himself of this before everything: I ought to be just without payment. That is too little. Let him convince himself of this as well: let me be glad to go further and even spend on this most beautiful virtue; let all thought be turned as far as possible away from private advantages. There is no reason for you to look to what the reward of a just act is: there is a greater one in what is just.

113.32  Fix this in yourself as well, what I was saying a little earlier: that it is nothing to the point how many people know of your fairness. Anyone who wants his virtue made public is working not for virtue but for glory. Are you unwilling to be just without glory? But, by Hercules, you will often have to be just with a bad name, and then, if you are wise, let a bad reputation well won delight you. Farewell.

LETTER 115: ON FUSSY STYLE AND THE LURE OF WEALTH

115.9  We marvel at walls coated with thin marble, though we know what sort of thing it is that is being hidden. We cheat our own eyes, and when we have flooded ceilings with gold, what else are we rejoicing in but a lie? For we know that under that gold ugly timber is lurking. Look inside, and you will know how much evil lies under that thin skin of rank.



LETTER 116: ON REMOVING THE PASSIONS ALTOGETHER

116.1  It has often been asked whether it is better to have moderate passions or none. Our school drives them out; the Peripatetics temper them. For my part, I do not see how any moderate degree of a disease can be wholesome or useful. Do not be afraid: I am taking from you none of the things that you do not want denied to you. I will show myself easy and indulgent towards the things you are aiming at and that you think either necessary for life or useful or pleasant: I will take away the vice. For when I have forbidden you to crave, I will allow you to want, so that you may do those same things unafraid, so that you may do them with a surer purpose, so that you may feel the pleasures themselves more. Why should they not reach you more if you command them than if you are their slave?

116.8  Do you know why we cannot do those things? Because we do not believe that we can. Or rather, by Hercules, the fact of the matter is something else: we defend our vices because we love them, and we would rather excuse them than shake them off. Nature has given man strength enough, if we would use it, if we would gather our powers and rouse all of them on our own behalf, or at any rate not against ourselves. Not wanting to is the cause; not being able to is put forward as the pretext. Farewell.



LETTER 118: ON SEEKING NOTHING FROM FORTUNE AND DEFINING GOOD

118.7  But ignorance of the truth afflicts everyone. Deceived by common talk, they are carried towards things as if towards goods; then, when they have got them and have suffered much, they see that these are bad, or empty, or smaller than they had hoped. And most people admire things that deceive from a distance, and commonly goods count as great things.



LETTER 120: ON HOW WE COME TO KNOW THE GOOD

120.22  This above all is how an unwise mind is shown up: it comes forward now as one, now as another, and it does not match itself, and I judge nothing more shameful than that. Count it a great thing to play one man. But no one except the wise man plays one; the rest of us are many-shaped. At one moment we will seem to you thrifty and serious, at another spendthrift and frivolous; we change our mask again and again and put on the opposite of the one we have taken off. So demand this of yourself: keep yourself, right to the end, such as you set out to show yourself. Bring it about that you can be praised, or, failing that, that you can be recognised. Of someone you saw yesterday it can rightly be said, “Who is this?”: the change is so great. Farewell.



LETTER 124: ON THE GOOD BELONGING TO REASON ALONE

124.12  But what is this good? I will tell you: a free mind, standing tall, putting other things under itself and itself under none. Infancy is so far from being able to take in this good that boyhood does not hope for it and youth hopes for it presumptuously; old age is doing well if it has reached it by long and concentrated effort. If this is the good, it is also a thing grasped by the understanding.

124.21  Do you ask what this discussion now leads to, and what use it will be to your mind? I say: it exercises the mind and sharpens it, and it keeps the mind, which will be doing something in any case, in an honourable occupation. And it is also of use in that it delays those who are hurrying towards what is wrong. But I say this: I can in no way be of more use than if I show you your own good, if I separate you from the mute animals, if I place you with god.

124.22  Why, I say, do you feed and exercise the strength of your body? Nature has granted that strength in greater measure to farm animals and wild beasts. Why do you cultivate your beauty? When you have done everything, you will be beaten in looks by the mute animals. Why do you dress your hair with enormous care? When you have let it flow in the manner of the Parthians, or tied it up in the way of the Germans, or, as is the habit of the Scythians, spread it loose, a thicker mane will toss on any horse you like, and a more beautiful one will bristle on the neck of lions. When you have trained yourself for speed, you will not be the equal of a little hare.

124.23  Will you not leave the things in which you are bound to be beaten while you strive for what is not your own, and go back to your own good? What is this? A mind, of course, freed from faults and pure, striving to equal god, lifting itself above human things, placing nothing of its own outside itself. You are a rational animal. What, then, is the good in you? Perfected reason. Summon it from here to its own goal, as far as it can possibly grow.
